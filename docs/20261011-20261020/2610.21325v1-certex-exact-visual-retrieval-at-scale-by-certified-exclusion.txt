CertEx: Exact Visual Retrieval at Scale by Certified Exclusion
Dongfang Zhao
Tacoma School of Engineering & Technology and eScience Institute
University of Washington
dzhao@uw.edu
Abstract
Exact k-nearest-neighbor (kNN) search over image de-
scriptors and embeddings underlies visual retrieval, near-
duplicate detection, the evaluation of learned representa-
tions, and increasingly the memory of LLM agents. How-
ever, exhaustive search computes every distance at full pre-
cision, whereas approximate indexes return recall below
one. We observe that exact search only needs a proof that
each excluded vector cannot be a neighbor. Because such a
proof only has to be conservative, it can be checked in low
precision, much as the retina examines the whole scene at
low resolution. We call this principle certified exclusion and
realize it in an exact kNN method for GPUs, which we call
CertEx. Specifically, CertEx stores a lower-bounding pro-
jection of each vector in half precision and folds a round-
ing certificate into the same tensor-core dot product, so that
only the few vectors that the test keeps need exact distances.
We prove that CertEx returns the exact kNN of every query.
On sixteen datasets with up to 100M vectors, CertEx is ex-
act for every query at 7.1× to 18.0× the throughput of the
fastest cuVS search on one H100 and outperforms quan-
tized and graph indexes whenever every query must be ex-
act.

Introduction
Exact kNN search over large collections of visual features is
a basic operation whose errors propagate into the tasks built
on top of it. For instance, image retrieval ranks database de-
scriptors such as SIFT [42] by their distance to a query de-
scriptor, so that a missed neighbor removes a relevant image
from the ranked list. Similarly, near-duplicate detection in
web-scale training corpora such as LAION [58] compares
every image embedding with its nearest neighbors [54], so
that a missed neighbor keeps a duplicate pair in the train-
ing data. Likewise, the kNN classifier that evaluates self-
supervised representations [12, 50, 70] reports the search er-
ror together with the quality of the representation whenever
its neighbors are approximate. Moreover, LLMs and agents
increasingly read long contexts and memories by nearest-
neighbor search [4, 11, 34, 38, 39, 67, 69, 71], so that a
missed neighbor drops a relevant memory. Finally, every
benchmark of approximate search measures recall against
exact neighbors and therefore inherits any error of the ex-
haustive search that computes them.
Existing systems respond to the cost of exact search in
two ways, both of which leave a gap.
First, exhaustive
search on GPUs [18, 35] computes every query-to-vector
distance at full precision.
The throughput of exhaustive
search is therefore bounded by one full-precision distance
per vector, and its capacity is bounded by device memory,
which must hold every vector at full precision. Second,
approximate indexes based on quantization [22, 28, 37] or
proximity graphs [43, 49, 63] are much faster. However,
these indexes trade recall for speed by design and rely on an
index that must be built before the first query and repaired
when the collection changes [74]. Practitioners therefore
choose between exact results at high cost and fast results
whose errors are not controlled per query.
To close this gap, we follow the division of labor in hu-
man vision: the periphery examines the whole visual field in
parallel at low resolution, whereas high resolution is spent
only on the few locations that attention selects [13, 65].
Transferred to kNN search, this principle examines every
database vector at low precision in one parallel pass and
spends full precision only on the few vectors that the pass
cannot exclude. However, a low-precision distance is not
exact. Consequently, unless every low-precision decision is
certified to be conservative, the pass loses neighbors in the
same way as an approximate index.
We address this difficulty with a principle that we call
certified exclusion: a test may run in any arithmetic as long
as it never excludes a vector within the threshold, so that
verifying the kept vectors yields the exact neighbors. We
realize this principle in an exact search method that we
call CertEx. Specifically, CertEx stores a lower-bounding
projection of every vector in half precision, together with
four coordinates that fold the rounding and accumulation
errors [10, 29] of the tensor-core dot product into the prod-
uct itself. As a result, one dot product and one comparison
per vector decide whether the vector can still be a neighbor,

In summary, we make three contributions.
• We propose certified exclusion, a new principle for ex-
act search that replaces the ranking of all N vectors by
one keep-or-exclude decision per vector. Because the
decision never excludes a vector within the threshold,
even when it runs in half precision, verifying only the
kept vectors returns the exact neighbors.
• We design CertEx as an exact kNN method that re-
alizes certified exclusion on GPU tensor cores [10].
We call its data structure the retina, which is a single
half-precision matrix with one row per database vector.
Each row holds a lower-bounding projection of one
vector and four coordinates of a rounding certificate.
We prove that its one-pass tensor-core test never dis-
cards a true neighbor, answers range queries and sim-
ilarity self-joins (all pairs within a radius) exactly, and
keeps survivors between two balls whose gap shrinks
as the principal coordinates capture more of the data.
• We evaluate CertEx on sixteen datasets with up to
100M vectors, including the embeddings of seven vi-
sion encoders such as CLIP [56], DINOv2 [50], and
DINOv3 [61]. CertEx answers every query exactly at
7.1× to 18.0× the throughput of the fastest exhaus-
tive search of NVIDIA cuVS [47] and is faster than
IVF-Flat [62], IVF-PQ [37], IVF-RaBitQ [22], and
CAGRA [49] whenever every query must be exact. On
the 12.8M web images of DataComp [21], CertEx is
exact for every query and finds all 31.4 billion dupli-
cate pairs, which makes exact deduplication [1] practi-
cal at web scale.

Related Work
Exact search with lower bounds.
Exact methods avoid
computing every distance by excluding the vectors whose
lower bound exceeds the current neighbor distance.
For
instance, the k-d tree [7] and the cover tree [9] bound
whole regions, whereas AESA [66] bounds single points
by the triangle inequality with precomputed pivots. How-
ever, the pruning power of such bounds decays with the di-
mension [8, 68]. The GEMINI framework [19] therefore
moves the bound into a contractive feature space with a
proof of no false dismissal, which the VA-file [68] turns
into a sequential scan of compact approximations. In con-
trast, random projections preserve distances only up to a
distortion factor [36] and support only approximate hash-
ing [14, 30], whereas exhaustive GPU search [24, 35] avoids
bounds altogether. In our terms, GEMINI and the VA-file
are certified exclusion tests in full precision whose survivors
are still ranked against a running candidate set; in contrast,
CertEx certifies a half-precision tensor-core decision inside
the same product and replaces the ranking by one compari-
son per vector.
Approximate indexes.
Approximate indexes trade exact-
ness for fewer examined vectors.
Specifically, product
quantization [6, 25, 28, 37] compresses vectors and probes a

few partitions, to which RaBitQ [22, 23] adds a probabilistic

among tree and k-means partitions. In contrast, graph in-
dexes such as HNSW [43], DiskANN [63], SONG [75],
GGNN [26], and CAGRA [49] walk a proximity graph to-
ward the query. Benchmarks [5, 60] accordingly rank these
methods by recall at a given throughput, whereas CertEx
examines every vector and returns exact results.
Nearest neighbors in visual recognition.
Visual recog-
nition uses nearest neighbors as a component whose errors
enter the task metric. For instance, object retrieval [3, 32,
53, 62] is evaluated against exhaustive ground truth [55].
Similarly, copy detection [17, 54] and dataset curation [1,
21] remove near and semantic duplicates from web-scale
corpora. In representation learning, weighted kNN classi-
fiers evaluate self-supervised representations [12, 50, 61].
Retrieval-augmented classification [41] even feeds the re-
trieved neighbors into the model.
In practice, these
pipelines obtain their neighbors from approximate indexes,
cluster-restricted comparisons, or kNN graphs of fixed de-
gree. In contrast, CertEx returns exact neighbors faster than
exhaustive search and answers deduplication with an exact
similarity self-join (Section 3.6).
Foveation and certified low-precision arithmetic.
Vi-
sion models allocate resolution unevenly, for instance
through recurrent glimpses [45], foveal-peripheral segmen-
tation [2], or foveated rendering [27, 31]. Numerical com-
puting allocates precision in the same way, for instance
in mixed-precision training [44]. Computational geometry
goes further: its floating-point filters evaluate a predicate in
fast arithmetic together with an error bound [10, 29] and fall
back to exact arithmetic only near zero [20, 59]. Following
this idea, CertEx folds the error bound into the same half-
precision product, so that every low-precision decision of
CertEx carries a proof of no false dismissal, unlike approx-
imate foveation.
3
Methodology
3.1
Overview
Exact kNN search must examine all N database vectors, al-
though only the few vectors near the query need full preci-
sion. Human vision solves the same problem by dividing its
work between two parts of the retina [13] (Figure 2(a)): the
periphery covers the whole scene at low resolution, whereas
the fovea sees only one spot at full resolution. Our exact
kNN method divides its work in the same way (Figure 2(b)).
We call this method CertEx, which stands for certified ex-
clusion.
The left half of Figure 2(b) shows the retina of CertEx,
which stores one half-precision vector ˆx for each of the
N database vectors x. We call ˆx the retina vector of x.
The first part of ˆx keeps a few principal coordinates of x
and summarizes the rest by one norm. We call this part
the lifted projection of x (Section 3.3). The inner product
of two lifted projections determines a lower bound on the
distance between their vectors. Although half precision is
the fastest arithmetic of GPU tensor cores [10], rounding to
half precision can overestimate the distance of a true neigh-
bor. To guard against this error, the second part of ˆx records
how large the rounding error of ˆx can be. We call this part
the certificate of ˆx. With the certificate, the test excludes a
vector only if the vector would be excluded even under the
worst possible rounding. Moreover, the tensor cores eval-
uate this worst case within the same product (Section 3.4).
Concretely, a tensor-core pass over the retina keeps a vector
x only if ⟨ˆq, ˆx⟩≥θ(q), where the retina vector ˆq encodes
the query q in the same form as ˆx and the threshold θ(q)
follows from an upper bound U on the distance of the k-th
nearest neighbor (Section 3.5). Because U is needed before
the pass, CertEx obtains it from a few sampled vectors. The
test of each vector therefore depends on no other database
vector, so that one pass over the retina decides all N vectors
without ranking them. We call this pass the retina pass. As
Figure 2(b) shows, the pass excludes most vectors (crosses)
but never a true neighbor; we call the kept vectors (checks)

survivors.
The right half of Figure 2(b) shows the fovea, which takes
over after the retina pass. For each survivor, the fovea reads
the original vector in full precision and computes the exact
distance between that vector and the query. As a result, the
k survivors with the smallest exact distances are the exact k
nearest neighbors of q, marked #1 and #2 for k = 2 in the
figure.

Certified Exclusion
Consider a database X := {x1, . . . , xN} ⊂RD and a

Definition 1 (Certified exclusion). A test T(q, x, U) ∈
{keep, exclude} is a certified exclusion test if its computed
output keeps every vector x with ∥q −x∥≤U, for every
query q, vector x, and threshold U ≥0.
Definition 1 constrains only the direction of the errors of
a test. Specifically, a certified exclusion test may keep a
vector farther than U at the cost of one exact distance, but it
never excludes a vector within U.
Proposition 1 (Exact search by certified exclusion). For a
certified exclusion test T and a threshold U ≥rk(q), the k
vectors with the smallest exact distances among the vectors
that T keeps form Nk(q).
Proof. Every x ∈Nk(q) satisfies ∥q −x∥≤rk(q) ≤U
and is therefore kept by Definition 1. The k smallest exact
distances among a superset of Nk(q) are the k smallest over
X.

Foveal Verification and Exactness
The fovea needs the threshold U before the retina pass and
the exact distances of the survivors after it. Before the pass,
CertEx samples every s-th retina vector, for a stride s, and
scores the sampled vectors by the same computed product
bP as the retina pass. Among them, the sampled vectors with
the largest products become the anchors A(q). The thresh-
old U is then the k-th smallest exact distance among the
anchors. Because the anchors are at least k distinct vectors
of X, U ≥rk(q) holds for every query. After the pass, the
fovea computes the exact distance of each survivor from the
full-precision difference q−x and returns the k smallest per
query.

3.6
Certified Exclusion at Any Threshold
Certified exclusion holds for every threshold, so that CertEx
answers any query that fixes its threshold in advance ex-
actly and without the anchors. Specifically, Definition 1 and
Proposition 3 hold for every U ≥0, so that the test of the
retina is certified whatever threshold a query uses. The an-
chors of Section 3.5 have a different role: they choose the
threshold U when a query does not fix it. For instance, kNN
search does not fix its threshold, because Proposition 1 re-
quires U ≥rk(q) and the distance rk(q) becomes known
only after the search. In contrast, a range query with ra-
dius r asks for all database vectors within r of the query, so
that its threshold is r from the start. CertEx thus answers a
range query by setting U := r, without any anchors. Since
the retina may also keep vectors farther than r, the fovea
computes their exact distances and removes them. CertEx
therefore returns exactly the vectors within r, by Proposi-
tions 2 and 3.
In particular, near-duplicate detection fixes its threshold
in advance as well, because it asks for all pairs of database
vectors whose distance is at most a given radius r. We call
the set of these pairs the similarity self-join. The self-join
therefore consists of the range queries of all database vec-
tors against the database, which CertEx answers exactly as
above. Because each pair appears in two of these range

Evaluation
4.1
Experimental Setup
Datasets and encoders.
The evaluation covers sixteen
descriptor, word, and image-embedding datasets that span
dimension and scale (Table 1).
Specifically, SIFT1M,
GIST1M, and the BIGANN prefixes up to 100M vec-
tors [33, 37] contain SIFT [42] and GIST [48] descriptors,
whereas Deep-image [72] and GloVe [52] contain 9.99M
CNN descriptors and 1.18M word embeddings.
More-
over, the ImageNet-1K datasets [15, 57] use 1.28M train-
ing images as the database and 10,000 validation images as
queries, both embedded by seven vision encoders, which
are vision transformers [16] of the CLIP [56], SigLIP [73],
DINOv2 [50], and DINOv3 [61] families. In addition, a
cross-modal workload queries the CLIP image embeddings
with 10,000 CLIP text prompts of the 1,000 class names. At
web scale, DataComp [21] contributes the CLIP ViT-L/14
embeddings of 12.79M web image-text pairs with 10,000
image and caption queries.
Platform, metrics, and baselines.
Every experiment
runs on one NVIDIA H100 GPU with the kernels of CertEx
written in Triton [64] on top of PyTorch [51]. Every ex-
periment keeps the database in device memory next to the
retina, which adds 2(m + 5) bytes per vector. Because the
fovea reads only the survivors, the database could also re-
side in host memory. Throughput counts the queries per
second over all queries with k = 10 after a warm-up pass.
For exactness, we recompute the squared distances of the
returned neighbors by direct differences in fp64 and call a
query exact when these sorted distances equal those of the
true neighbors to a relative 10−12, which accepts ties among
equidistant vectors and no other deviation. The true neigh-
bors come from an fp64 search that is reranked by direct dif-
ferences in fp64. We compare CertEx with six GPU base-
lines: the exhaustive search of NVIDIA cuVS [47] in fp32
at its fastest selection size, the same search in half precision
with exact reranking, and the cuVS indexes IVF-Flat [62],
IVF-PQ [37], IVF-RaBitQ [22], and CAGRA [49]. For a
fair comparison, each baseline uses the best configuration
of a grid, with exact reranking for the quantized indexes,
as does CertEx with a few widths and anchor strides per
dataset.

4.3
Ablation of the Design Elements
We then ask where the speed of CertEx comes from and
what its certificate costs, with every element isolated on the
same kernel and threshold (Figure 4). For 2048 queries, a
complete CertEx search takes 5.0 ms against 37.2 ms for
exhaustive search on SIFT1M and 170 ms against 3.6 s on
BIGANN-100M, of which the anchors and the fovea take
only 2.2 ms and 22 ms (Figure 4(a)). Most of the remain-
ing time therefore belongs to the retina pass, which costs
less than a plain fp16 matrix multiplication (GEMM) over
the same vectors (2.3 ms against 4.1 ms on SIFT1M and 148
ms against 289 ms on BIGANN-100M) because it compares
every product with its threshold inside one kernel instead
of writing the matrix of all products. In contrast, exhaus-
tive search must select the top 100 candidates among all
N products, which costs 3.3× to 12.5× the GEMM alone.
Moreover, the ablation in Figure 4(b) shows that certified
exclusion provides most of the speed: the top-k selection
instead slows the pass by 5.6× to 24.3×, whereas TF32 in-
stead of fp16 and the width D instead of m slow it by only
1.2× to 1.5× and 1.1× to 2.1×.
In addition, the certificate makes the half-precision deci-
sion exact (Figure 4(c)). The hardest test places every pair
exactly on the decision boundary: for four million random
pairs per dataset and width, we set the threshold of each
pair to its own lower bound. In this test, the retina keeps ev-
ery pair, whereas the same retina without the error terms of
Eq. (10) excludes 1.996M to 2.007M of the 4M pairs, be-
cause half-precision rounding errs in either direction with
equal frequency. Moreover, the largest accumulation error
that we measure for 108 pairs per dataset and every ker-
nel configuration is 0.43% to 0.74% of the certified bound,
so that the certificate holds with a margin of more than
two orders of magnitude. The certificate costs only 0.4%
to 2.7% of throughput, yet it decides real boundary pairs:
without it, the exact self-join of the DINOv2 [50] embed-
dings misses 299, 50, and 1 duplicate pairs at τ = 0.90,

5
Conclusion
Nearest-neighbor search serves image retrieval and dedu-
plication and, increasingly, agentic retrieval and the long-
context memory of LLMs. However, exact kNN search has
so far required either a full-precision distance to every vec-
tor or an index that gives up exactness. CertEx shows that
10


exact search needs neither: a lifted lower bound with a cer-
tificate for its rounding error excludes almost every vec-
tor in one half-precision tensor-core pass without a false
dismissal. On sixteen datasets, including seven vision en-
coders and 12.8M web images, CertEx is exact for every
query at up to 18.0× the throughput of the fastest exhaustive
GPU search. Exact search thus becomes a practical default
for image retrieval, web-scale image deduplication, and the
evaluation of visual representations.
