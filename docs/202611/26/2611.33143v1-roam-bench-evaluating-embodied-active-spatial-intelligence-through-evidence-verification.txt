# Code Scene Assets Project Page Abstract 

Embodied spatial intelligence requires agents to gather information through exploration and in-teraction, then use it to reason and act. Existing benchmarks insufficiently distinguish correct outcomes from completion of these task requirements. We introduce ROAM-Bench to evaluate these abilities under robot motion and collision constraints. Independent checks use simulation state and episode records to assess outcomes and task requirements, with stronger checks of ge-ometry, coverage, or placement. It comprises 250 manually validated tasks across 11 types in five groups and 27 indoor environments, covering object and room search, counting, geometric judgments, direction reasoning, and relocation. Human evaluators achieve 84% strict task success on 100 sampled tasks, with every correct outcome satisfying the required checks. Across seven frontier vision-language models, we identify two complementary failure modes: correct outcomes with unmet task requirements, and incorrect outcomes despite passing stronger checks. Oracle-input diagnostics show that more complete target observations and calibrated feedback improve final spatial judgments. 

# 1 Introduction 

Embodied spatial intelligence requires agents to gather information through exploration and interaction. Counting mugs across a home, for example, requires searching multiple rooms, opening closed storage, and tracking objects across views to avoid double-counting (Fig. 1). The agent must also choose views that reveal occluded targets and decide when the search is complete. Yet an agent may report the correct count without observing every mug or checking every room. It may also make incorrect spatial judgments after collecting the required observations. Outcome correctness must therefore be evaluated alongside the task’s required observations and interactions. Existing benchmarks address different parts of this process. VSI-Bench [29] and MMSI-Bench [31] evaluate spatial question answering from preselected videos or images. EmbodiedQA [4] and OpenEQA [16] shift in-formation gathering to the agent through question-driven navigation. EXPRESS-Bench [12] assesses answer grounding in the final observed image, while ESI-Bench [8] evaluates perception, locomotion, and manipula-tion for spatial QA. EmbodiedBench [30] and BEHAVIOR-Spatial [2] evaluate embodied tasks and spatial skills. Independent checks verify the final count, target observations, room coverage, and container inspection. 

To address this gap, we introduce ROAM-Bench, which evaluates task outcomes together with the observa-tions and interactions required to reach them. The benchmark comprises 250 manually validated tasks across 11 types in five groups: grounding, aggregation, geometry, reference-frame integration, and goal-directed spa-tial action. Independent checks use simulation state and episode records to assess outcomes and separately verify required observations, search coverage, ordered interactions, geometry, and object placement. This ver-ification helps distinguish missing observations from mistakes made after collecting them for deeper failure analysis. Task success requires these conditions together with a correct outcome, measuring how exploration, spatial judgment, and action work together. Our platform, built on Isaac Sim [17], provides 27 furnished indoor environments generated with Scene-Smith [19] and manually corrected for benchmark use. A vision-language model (VLM) chooses predefined navigation and interaction skills, including moving, observing, opening, picking, and placing. The simulator executes these atomic skills under declared motion and interaction constraints. After each skill, the agent receives a new view and action feedback before choosing its next step. The feedback reports collisions and object-handling outcomes, which the agent can use to adjust its plan. Our contributions are (1) A comprehensive benchmark and evaluation protocol for embodied spa-tial intelligence , connecting exploration, spatial judgment, and goal-directed action. (2) An executable exploration platform with atomic actions, leveraging simulation oracles to verify task outcomes and task-required evidence. (3) Human and frontier VLM evaluations with supporting diagnostics covering information acquisition, precision, relocation failures, input sensitivity, and costs that include failures.

From outcome scores to task completion assessment. These directions address complementary parts of embodied tasks: understanding space, choosing actions, and gathering observations. To evaluate how these capabilities work together under robot motion and collision constraints, ROAM-Bench independently checks outcomes and task-required observations and interactions across complete episodes. Additional checks examine geometry and post-placement support. This connects final performance to the exploration and interaction process and supports detailed analysis of failures in observation acquisition, spatial judgment, and action (Table 1). 

# 3 Benchmark Design 

3.1 Task Taxonomy and Interaction 

To evaluate embodied active spatial intelligence, we design tasks that combine exploration, spatial reasoning, and interaction in different ways. For example, counting may require searching several rooms, gap measure-ment requires observing both edges, and relocation requires finding and moving the correct object. On our platform based on Isaac Sim [17], the agent gathers observations and interacts with objects until it completes the task. We then check the final result and whether the task is carried out as required. We group the 11 task types by their main answer or action (Fig. 2). 3.3 Task Generation and Validation 

We generate questions, reference answers, and goal conditions from the objects, room assignments, and geometry recorded in each scene. We check task feasibility through an oracle trajectory generated using simulator state and the available skills. We then manually inspect the scene to verify the instructions, answers or goals, and observation and interaction checks described below. This includes checking which objects and rooms must be counted, how directions are defined, and which object must be picked and subsequently placed at which destination. 

3.4 Outcome and Evidence Verification 

3.4.1 Agent Interaction 

At each round, the agent receives the task, reference images, latest camera image, available sensor readings, and feedback from its actions. Earlier observations and a visualization of the traveled path help it relate the current view to previous locations. Before executing a skill or submitting an answer or completion report, it can retrieve relevant earlier images using their text summaries. Retrieved images are added to the current round. Executing a skill produces the next observation and action feedback. Actions that select image points refer to the latest camera image. Once the agent finishes, the evaluator checks the final result and whether the task is carried out as required (Fig. 3). 

3.4.2 Outcome and Evidence Criteria 

A correct answer alone does not show that the agent completes the task’s required exploration and interactions. Conversely, an agent can complete the required observations yet answer incorrectly. 3.4.3 Task-Specific Evidence Requirements 

The checks follow each task’s requirements. For example, counting checks which objects and rooms the agent observes, while relocation checks which object it picks and where it places it. 

Observation and search. N checks whether the required targets appear in the 768 × 432 camera images. Counting also checks the specified object instances and search scope. A room counts as observed when the agent enters it or obtains an object view that passes the visibility check in that room. S can additionally re-quire room visits and clear target views. Within a room, views from similar camera positions and orientations count once toward viewpoint coverage. 

Geometry. For nearer-object questions, A checks the selected answer and N checks whether all referenced objects are observed. S additionally checks that depth-based distances identify the correct nearest candidate and distinguish it from the next closest after accounting for measurement uncertainty. Distance, direction, and gap-width tasks similarly compare geometry reconstructed from the recorded views with the task reference. For room area, N requires entering the target room, while S requires valid depth views from several directions. 6Interaction. For relocation, N checks that the agent picks the specified object before placing that same object at the destination. For hidden targets, the checks also require opening the container before revealing the object. S additionally checks that the released object rests on the specified destination. 

Landmarks and the answer frame. In path integration, the agent searches for two landmarks and reports where its starting position lies relative to its final position and heading. The provided start and final poses supply the information needed to judge this direction. Accordingly, A checks the direction answer, while 

N checks whether the agent observes both landmarks, regardless of order. S additionally checks room and viewpoint coverage. A correct answer with N = 0 therefore means that the required landmark exploration is unfinished.

4.1.1 Models and Interaction

VLMs struggle with both task coverage and spatial judgment. Correct outcomes do not always mean that required observations and interactions are complete. The 106 path-integration gaps concern unmet landmark exploration. Across the other task types, 100/1,540 episodes have correct outcomes but unmet task requirements. All seven models exhibit this gap (6–18 cases each). Conversely, incorrect outcomes also occur after stronger corroboration checks (S) pass. Every VLM has a higher error rate in these episodes than human evaluators. In the 100-task human sample, all correct outcomes meet required coverage, and errors after S passes are rare. Together, these findings motivate linking target search and coverage tracking with reliable instance-level spatial judgment. 

Task-group performance reveals uneven strengths. Relocation is the weakest group for six of the seven models, while locating objects and identifying their rooms is generally easier. GPT-6.1-sol performs better on geometry than on grounding, whereas both Claude models show the reverse pattern. These profiles distinguish scale estimation, cross-view instance tracking, and intended-object transfer.

5.1 Spatial Judgment across Task Groups 

We examine G1–G4 using errors after observation checks and numerical precision. 

Room names require visual semantic grounding. Room options provide names, requiring models to infer room functions from layouts and furnishings. For GPT-6.1-sol and GPT-6-astra, room-function inference remains harder than local object recognition after N passes. This contrast highlights the need to connect visible objects with a room’s overall layout and function. 

Counting remains difficult after N and S pass. Counting errors persist for these models after both N and S pass. Completing observation checks does not ensure a consistent global count, highlighting cross-view instance identity and quantity integration as demands of spatial reasoning. 

Free-space width is a precision bottleneck. Gap width has the most models with median error above tolerance (Fig. 5). Halving tolerances preserves the overall A/T ranking (Appendix E.3). Continuous errors reveal precision differences that binary scores can obscure. 

Task-defined frames challenge direction judgments. Across all seven models, relative-direction errors exceed path-integration errors after N passes. These questions require constructing a frame from two reference objects to locate a third. Explicit representations of the frame’s origin and forward axis may help these judgments. 

5.2 Object Relocation 

Correct pickup is the largest relocation bottleneck. All seven models lose the most episodes before picking the correct object on the same relocation tasks. Later failures involve transferring that same object and verifying destination support (see the relocation milestones in Table S11). This shared pattern identifies target selection and correct pickup as the main relocation bottleneck. 

Target selection and correction dominate early failures. Wrong-object pickup dominates early failures in every model. Feedback can also explain why a pick fails: the selected image point is on a floor or wall, or the arm cannot reach the object. Some models select another point or move the base but repeat the same error. These failures highlight the need to verify target identity and use feedback to correct subsequent actions. 

5.3 Oracle Input Diagnostics 

We select 139 active-exploration tasks from G1–G4 in 26 scenes with recoverable final-answer inputs and target views. Four ground-truth (GT) settings test final judgments. GT retrieval adds all task-relevant object frames from each model’s own prior trajectory before answering. GT view supplementation adds target views from other trajectories of the same task, including unseen targets. Both retain the original exploration context. 5.4 Human Analysis of Exploration Gaps 

Human evaluators categorize all 206 gap cases ( A = 1 , N = 0 ) across the 1,750 VLM episodes. Categories distinguish target observations, search scope, landmarks, and access or event verification. Of the 100 non-path cases, 85 miss required-object observations. One model correctly answers six mirrors while observing only five. Another observes all four side tables but leaves a required room unexplored. These cases and unseen-view gains (Section 5.3) motivate collecting target views and tracking instances separately from search coverage. 

# 6 Conclusion 

ROAM-Bench evaluates exploration, spatial judgment, and goal-directed action through independent out-come and evidence checks. Human evaluators demonstrate successful task completion through the public observation and action interface. VLMs exhibit both correct outcomes with unmet task requirements and errors after stronger corroboration passes. Spatial-estimation and relocation failures further identify difficul-ties in grounding judgments and selecting the intended object. These findings motivate coherent cross-view instance representations, calibrated spatial judgment, and feedback-guided planning.

# A Task Rules and Coverage 

The tables document task groups, scene and task-type coverage, and the observations and actions required by each task. Outcome correctness (A), task-required evidence (N), and stronger corroboration (S) are assessed separately. S requires all N conditions plus task-specific corroboration, so S=1 implies N=1. Strict task success (T) requires A AND N. 

Environment categories and scale. The 27 environments cover residential, office, retail and hospitality, community and leisure, and specialized workspaces. Each has 1–13 rooms (median 6) and 45–604 furnishings and other objects (median 159). Task-required evidence conditions (N) operationalize the observations and interactions required by each task instruction and are frozen before model evaluation. They serve related but distinct purposes: observing relevant objects, covering the declared search scope, and completing specified exploration or interaction. A condition can serve more than one purpose. In path integration, public self-motion information supports the directional answer, while landmark observations check completion of the prescribed exploration. Counting-scope coverage checks that the agent searches the required rooms, including places where further instances might occur. 

Answer rules, viewpoint groups, and charged actions. The numeric scorer normalizes units. The reference distance d is the nearest-integer horizontal straight-line distance between the two scene object centroids. Reference area a is the room’s recorded rectangular footprint (width times depth), rounded to the nearest integer and including occupied floor space. Answer tolerances are ± max(2 m , 0.20 d) and 

± max(5 m 2, 0.25 a). Gap width uses a tolerance of ±10 cm . A records whether an estimate falls within the tolerance, while continuous errors describe its precision. Appendix E.3 reports tighter-tolerance results, retaining the stored reference values. Geometric S checks retain their separate frozen task thresholds. Viewpoints are grouped by room, position ( 0.5 m), yaw ( 30 ◦), and camera horizon ( 15 ◦). Each group con-tributes once to coverage, so repeated observations from the same viewpoint do not inflate it. Each task allows 25 high-level actions per physical room. Navigation, camera motion, and object interaction consume actions; retrieval and final answers do not. Internal walking and animation carry out each skill decision. 

Visibility and instance identity. A qualifying observation passes the task’s target-visibility rule. Regions belonging to the same instance are combined, including disconnected regions. The VLM receives RGB images 768 pixels wide and 432 pixels high. We inspect the delivered RGB views to select the visibility criterion before evaluation. The same criterion defines the scorer’s clear-observation check. Repeated sightings are matched by instance ID across rounds. Visual room coverage counts camera entry or a qualifying observation of an object in that room. Physical room coverage requires a recorded camera pose inside the room. 

Grounding and counting rules. For surface contents, N requires observing the designated surface or container and every listed object. S also checks that the objects have the required support relations and qualifying observations. For room localization, N requires observing the target or an accepted same-kind object in the answer room, with any required opening before a hidden target is revealed. S adds a qualifying target observation and a consistent object-to-room assignment. A verified copy made from the same object template can satisfy S through its own observation and room assignment. For object and room counts, N requires observing every counted instance and visually covering the specified search rooms. Room counts also check each instance’s room assignment and the set of rooms containing targets. Room comparisons additionally check per-room counts. For these exhaustive-search tasks, S adds visits to every search room and qualifying observations of every counted instance. 

Depth and geometric corroboration. Valid depth is finite and positive. The evaluator estimates each object’s position from the qualifying view in which it occupies the largest image region and has a valid depth-derived point. Landmark and relocation rules. For path integration, N checks whether both specified landmarks are observed, regardless of order. S adds the required room and distinct-viewpoint coverage. Public interaction and history. Each observation round supplies the inputs for the next skill or final answer. The interaction interface described in the main paper presents the task, sensor readings, action feedback, reference images, and the most recent 20 visual rounds. All image sources share a limit of 50 images per decision. Retrieved camera images and their original text can supplement this history. Intermediate camera frames from the preceding action are shown only at the first decision in a round. A path visualization and the latest camera image follow. Image-point actions refer only to that latest image. At the first decision, the agent may retain selected intermediate frames. The default retains none, and the selection remains unchanged for the round. Before executing a skill or submitting an answer or completion report, the agent may retrieve earlier observations at most twice, with at most 12 archived images in total. Empty or invalid retrieval requests count toward this limit. Retrieval updates the context without changing the environment or advancing the observation round. # D Task Difficulty Across Models 

We measure each task’s answer accuracy across the seven models, with each model contributing once. The denominator is seven for every task. Task type and scene are associated with 0.466 and 0.197 of the observed between-task accuracy variance ( η2), respectively. Their additive fit has in-sample R2 = 0 .545 . The associations overlap because tasks share both type and scene. We hold out each of the 27 scenes in turn and estimate task-type means from the remaining scenes. These means reduce squared prediction error by 41.46% relative to the training-mean baseline, showing that task type helps predict difficulty across the benchmark environments. Twenty-nine tasks receive no correct answer from any of the seven models. These shared failures indicate task difficulties that persist across models with different overall scores. Appendix F provides human-verified analysis of object visibility and behavior across all seven models. 

Overall performance comparison. GPT-6-astra achieves A=73.20% and T=69.20%, with 43.62 actions per episode, including failures. Its paired T advantage over GPT-6.1-sol is 22.40 percentage points (10,000 whole-scene resamples, percentile range 15.83–28.83).

E.2 Relocation Milestones 

Across the 210 relocation episodes, 94 successfully pick an acceptable target, 56 subsequently place the same object at the specified destination, and 40 pass the destination-support check after placement. Milestones use recorded event order, including events within one observation round, following the relocation rules in Appendix A. These milestones count pickup, placement, and support events. N and S additionally require the applicable access checks, while A checks the final goal state. Later actions can change the final state after a successful placement. 19 Wrong-object pickup and recovery. We inspect all 210 episodes using the task’s acceptable target in-stances and the recorded pick events. A wrong-object pickup is a successful pick of an object outside this acceptable set. Of the 116 episodes without any correct pickup, 102 contain a wrong-object pickup without later correction (87.9%). Thus, target selection errors that remain uncorrected account for most failures before correct pickup. Per-model counts are also reported in Table S25. Among the 116 episodes without a correct pickup, 36 contain a failed pick action. Fourteen contain failed picks at two or more distinct action steps. These categories can overlap with wrong-object pickup. Across all 210 episodes, 20 reach a correct pickup after a failed pick action, and 15 reach a correct pickup after a wrong-object pickup. These events show that recovery is possible, while the 102 uncorrected wrong-object episodes identify target verification and correction as major weaknesses. 

Responses to explicit failure feedback. The recorded actions illustrate how a strategy change can still fail to address the problem. In a DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash proof-card episode, two successive pick actions select different image points. Both receive feedback that the point lies on a floor, wall, or ceiling and the arm does not move. In a GPT-5.6-sol kettle episode, the model moves sideways and forward after reachability failures. The next pick still reports no reachable arm pose, and a later successful pick holds a different object. These episodes show attempts to adjust actions, but inadequate correction of the selected point or target identity. They motivate using feedback to decide whether to change the grasp point, approach position, or selected object, then checking the result in the next image. 

E.3 Numerical Estimation Errors 

Answer accuracy alone does not show the size of numerical errors. Each model answers the same 20 tasks in each of three numerical types. We convert parsed answers to the task’s scoring units and measure absolute error, relative error, and absolute error divided by the answer tolerance. A normalized error of one is the acceptance boundary. Error summaries use parseable answers, with coverage reported for every model and task type. Object distance, room area, and gap width are reported in metres, square metres, and centimetres, respec-tively. Normalizing by tolerance relates these different quantities to their acceptance rules. Gap width has the most models with median errors above tolerance, identifying physical free-space estimation as a priority for geometric reasoning. 

Human numerical estimates. Two human evaluators independently estimate all 20 tasks in each numerical type, giving 40 judgments per type and 120 overall. They receive the public task materials and standardized RGB views containing the targets, with depth, reference values, and tolerances withheld. This experiment evaluates numerical estimation from supplied views, separately from the 100-task human active-exploration evaluation. Table S13 reports absolute and relative errors, acceptance rates, and agreement. Default tolerances accept 110/120 estimates (91.7%), compared with 93/120 (77.5%) and 64/120 (53.3%) at the tighter scales. Ac-ceptance spans 87.5–95.0% across the three task types, with ICC(2,1) of 0.76–0.84. These results place the binary thresholds against human visual estimates. The continuous errors and tighter-score results preserve a separate measure of precision.

E.4 Action Cost

You are the decision-making brain of a household robot in a simulated house. Each round you receive three things: a photograph from the robot’s own camera, one line of text saying whether your hand is full, and a report of what the robot’s own body measured while carrying out your last action. You issue exactly one action per round. There is no description of this house, no list of what is in it, and nothing anywhere that names anything – everything you come to know about the place you will have worked out from pictures. - It is 768x432 pixels with a wide lens, taken from the robot’s eye height. Small or distant things are only a few pixels wide and are easy to mistake for something else, so treat a distant identification as a guess until you have walked closer. - Where a thing sits in the frame is not a hint about your action, it **is** your action: every skill takes a pixel coordinate read off this image. Look at the picture before every single move. - You are shown the 20 most recent photographs, not just the newest one. Use them together with the body report: the report says what the robot *did*, and the difference between consecutive frames says what that achieved. Where the report stops short – and it says so when it does – the pictures are all you have. - ‘holding:‘ says ‘(something)‘ or ‘(nothing)‘, and that is the only fact about the world’s state you are ever given in words. It will never say what you are carrying: a gripper measures force and finger position, not names. You are given no text description of this house. Not the rooms it has, not the furniture in it, not the name of a single object, and no list that fills in as you explore. The one line of text you get each round is ‘holding:‘, and it says only whether your hand is full or empty – never what is in it. Your senses are the photograph and what the robot can feel in its own body. Your only memory is this conversation: every THOUGHT line you write is kept and shown back to you on every later round, while the older photographs are dropped. So the notes you take are the only durable record you will have – when you see that the kitchen is through the left-hand doorway, write that down, because the picture proving it will be gone. If you pick something up and it matters later what it was, write that down too: the ‘holding:‘ line will not remind you.
