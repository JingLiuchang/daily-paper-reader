## Abstract 

In-context learning (ICL) lets agents acquire new rules from context and adapt to user-specific requirements at inference time. The same frontier agents now operate both computers and robots, yet their ICL remains insufficiently assessed: existing benchmarks are largely offline, rely on text or static images, and risk training-data contamination, while robot evaluations are limited to method-level ablations. We present 

ICLWorld , an online benchmark that evaluates the ICL of agents from digital to physical worlds, covering computer use and robot use, together with UIverse , a unified har-ness that turns every environment into a user interface. We build 30 environments, record 630 demonstration videos (10+ hours), and design 160 tasks with customized, often counter-intuitive rules that cannot be satisfied without learn-ing from the context. Each task comes with three rule ver-sions, given as text or video, and we test generalization at three levels, from replaying the demonstration to new in-stances and unseen environments. Across six frontier models, the strongest agents, Claude Opus 5.5 and GPT-6 Astra, suc-ceed on 82% and 76% of computer-use runs but only 27% 

1arXiv:2611.32649v1 [cs.CV] 24 Nov 2026 and 26% of robot-use runs, and a no-rule control reduces success to at most 8%. GPT-6 Astra succeeds under all three rule versions on only 38% of computer-use tasks, half its rate under a single rule, and answers 98% of robot rule questions correctly yet completes only 20% of these tasks. Frontier agents learn rules comparably well from video and from text and transfer them across environments, whereas weaker agents rely on text and lose most of their video-based success once the demonstrated situation is resampled. These findings reveal substantial gaps between the digital and the physical world and between identifying a rule and acting on it. 

## 1. Introduction 

In-context learning (ICL), the ability to acquire new rules, knowledge, and procedures from the input without any pa-rameter update [8, 21], is what allows a general-purpose model to follow the conventions of a particular user, team, or workplace rather than its pre-trained priors. This ability matters most for agents that act. Today, the same frontier models operate computers as computer-use agents (CUAs) and, increasingly, robots as robot-use agents (RUAs) [13, 64]. In both settings, users teach a task in the most natural way: they show a demonstration or state a rule, and expect the agent to apply it to new data and in new surroundings. Measuring this ability faithfully is difficult, and exist-ing ICL benchmarks fall short in four ways. First, most of them present the context as text or static images and eval-uate offline, whereas acting agents learn from videos and must carry out what they learned in interactive environments. Second, their rules and data are often drawn from public sources and may have entered pre-training. Third, as models grow stronger, many tasks become solvable without reading the context at all [9], so success no longer reflects learning. Fourth, with a single rule per task, they cannot tell whether an agent follows the given rule or its own prior, nor whether it can transfer the rule beyond the demonstrated situation. The physical world is even less understood. Recent RUAs increasingly rely on frontier models to learn from in-context demonstrations, yet this ability has been exam-ined only within individual systems, with conflicting conclu-sions [15, 32, 53, 89]. Moreover, although computer use and robot use share the same perceive–act loop, no benchmark asks whether a rule-learning ability that works on screens carries over to robots when the agent and its interface are held fixed. We present ICLW ORLD (Fig. 1), a benchmark that eval-uates the in-context rule learning of agents from the digital to the physical world. ICLW ORLD contains 160 tasks in two domains: computer use on web and OS platforms, and tabletop manipulation, in which a dual-arm robot learns from real-world human demonstrations and acts in physics sim-ulation. Each task asks the agent to reach a goal under a customized, often counter-intuitive rule that is given only in the context, either as a text description or as a video demon-stration. ICLW ORLD evaluates in-context learning end to end, from inferring the rule to completing the task under it. ICLW ORLD is built on four principles: • Leakage-free. We build all 30 environments and record all 630 demonstration videos (10+ hours) ourselves, around customized rules rather than public material. • Context-dependent. Without the rule, a task can only be completed by chance, and each task comes in three rule versions that demand different behavior in the same situation. • Challenging. Tasks range from short rule applications to long-horizon workflows and fine manipulation, on par with or harder than current frontier benchmarks. • Diagnostic. We test whether learned rules generalize to new instances and unseen environments, and probe the gap between rule recognition and task completion. All agents interact through UI VERSE , a unified harness that presents web applications, desktops, and robot environments through the same kind of graphical interface. This removes variations from domain-specific interfaces and enables a consistent evaluation across both worlds. We evaluate six frontier models in their native agent CLIs. The strongest, Claude Opus 5.5 and GPT-6 Astra, succeed on 82% and 76% of computer-use runs but only 27% and 26% of robot-use runs, and the other four models trail far behind in both domains. Without the rule, success drops to at most 8%, confirming that the tasks require learning from context. GPT-6 Astra succeeds under all three rule versions on only 38% of computer-use tasks, half its rate under a single rule. When asked about the rule before acting, agents usually answer correctly yet fail to carry it out, especially with a robot. Frontier agents learn rules comparably well from video and text and transfer them across environments, from real-world demonstrations to simulation and to unseen applications, whereas weaker agents rely on text and lose most of their video-based success on new instances. In summary, our contributions are: • ICLW ORLD , a leakage-free benchmark of 160 tasks with three rule versions each, spanning computer use and robot use, built on 30 self-built environments and 630 self-recorded demonstrations. • UI VERSE , a unified harness that turns every world into a user interface, and a diagnostic protocol that together eval-uate the same agents in the digital and the physical world across rule versions, generalization levels, rule modalities, and difficulty. • A systematic evaluation of six frontier agents, revealing a large gap between the digital and the physical world and between understanding a rule and executing it. 22. Related Work 

In-context and context learning. ICL, popularized by GPT-3 [8], has been studied extensively [21], from what makes demonstrations effective [58] to its mechanisms [29, 60]. Other work asks whether context can override priors, 

e.g . with flipped labels [79], counterfactual task variants [81], or judgments of when context should govern the answer [45]. Benchmarks evaluate learning a language from a grammar book [74] and context learning of expert-written knowledge and rules [23, 24, 48]. Multimodal ICL is evaluated with interleaved images [3, 9, 94] and multimodal context learn-ing of visual rules and procedures [14, 80]. Video contexts are studied for procedural QA [22], while LMAct [69] and V-ICAL Bench [26] evaluate in-context imitation of multi-modal agents in games. ICLW ORLD brings rule-centric, leakage-free ICL evaluation to interactive computer and robot environments, with text and video rules for the same task. 

Computer-use agents. CUAs are evaluated on web [17, 42, 90], desktop [7, 82], mobile [67], and grounding [44] benchmarks. As frontier models exceed 84% on OSWorld-Verified [59, 66], attention has shifted to long-horizon work-flows such as OSWorld 2.0 [87]. Agents build on proprietary or specialized GUI models [1, 4, 33, 61, 65, 78]. Several works let CUAs learn from demonstrations or tutorial videos at inference time [49, 56, 72], and benchmarks evaluate video-guided GUI automation [25, 38, 51], few-shot demon-strations [54], demonstration-guided office workflows [20], and user-specific preferences [39]. None of them varies the rule of the same task or tests it in unseen environments. 

Robot-use agents. Robot manipulation is dominated by vision-language-action (VLA) policies [6, 30, 41, 63, 93], evaluated on LIBERO [52], RoboTwin 2.0 [10], Sim-plerEnv [47], CALVIN [57], ManiSkill3 [75], and Ro-boDojo [11]; scores on saturated benchmarks collapse under perturbations [91]. LLMs and VLMs were first used as planners or code generators [2, 36, 50]; more recently, frontier agents act directly as robot-use agents (RUAs) through harnesses that expose robot control as tools [12, 28, 32, 35, 46, 86, 89], and RUA benchmarks evaluate them across tasks and embodiments [19, 70, 84]. ICL for robots has been explored with trained in-context policies, including VIMA’s multimodal prompts that specify a task [27, 40, 76], with LLMs reading demonstrations as to-kens [18, 85], and by imitation from human videos [37, 77]. For frontier RUAs, in-context demonstrations improve suc-cess in RoboDawn [32], RoboICL [53], GPT-Policy [15], and VIA [35], while one-shot demonstrations bring no ag-gregate gain when GPT-6 Astra acts as a policy directly [89]. These are method-level ablations with one or a few models.

A task in ICLW ORLD consists of a goal, a rule that changes how the goal must be achieved, an environment in which the agent acts, and a verifier that checks the final state against the rule (Fig. 2). We describe how tasks are de-signed (Sec. 3.1), the environments and demonstrations they build on (Sec. 3.2), the unified harness through which agents act (Sec. 3.3), the evaluation protocol (Sec. 3.4), and the metrics (Sec. 3.5). 

3.1. Task Design 

Rules as the object of learning. Every task is built around a rule that changes how its goal must be achieved, such as an arbitrary text format, a criterion for selecting items, a filing convention, or the order in which objects are stacked. We design rules to be customized and often counter-intuitive, so that they cannot be inferred from the goal or from common sense: without the rule, a model can complete the task only by guessing it. For each task, we design three versions (A/B/C) that share the goal and the initial situation but differ in the rule, e.g . rewriting the same notification draft in a different letter case, or boxing a different subset of the same flipped cards (Fig. 1); Appendix A shows further examples. The 160 tasks thus yield 480 task–rule pairs. 

Two domains, one taxonomy. ICLW ORLD covers two domains (Fig. 1, right). Computer use (110 tasks) takes place in web applications (75 tasks) and an OS desktop (35 tasks), with scenarios ranging from messaging, publishing, finance, coding, and puzzle games to file, document, and system management; robot use (50 tasks) consists of table-top manipulation. We categorize the tasks of both domains by what their rule specifies: selection (which items to act on), formatting (how an output is written, named, or filled in), labeling and matching (which label or target each item receives), ordering (the order of items or actions), arrange-ment (where objects end up), and procedure (the steps or tools used to reach the goal). Selection and formatting rules dominate computer use, whereas arrangement and ordering rules dominate robot use. 

Difficulty. We grade each task by its execution demands, independently of its rule: • Computer use (36/37/37 easy/medium/hard). Easy : apply the rule to several objects within one application. Medium :complete a dependent workflow in which the rule is one crucial module. Hard : complete an end-to-end workflow across applications, in which the rule is again one module. • Robot use (7/19/24). Easy : a simple manipulation. 

Medium : multiple operations or bimanual coordination. 

Hard : fine manipulation, or a long-horizon workflow in which the rule is one module. Medium and hard tasks are on par with or harder than the hardest tasks of current computer-use and robot bench-marks [11, 87]. 

3.2. Environments and Demonstrations 

Self-built environments. To rule out leakage, we build ev-ery environment ourselves: 15 original environments, each paired with a counterpart, 30 in total. The digital world com-prises 13 web applications, from messaging, music, news, and video to travel, shopping, banking, an online judge, and games, and one OS desktop with its own file system and system tools. Each application maintains realistic state and logic, so a rule must be applied to actual records rather than to a static page. The physical world is a tabletop simulator built on Isaac Lab, in which a dual-arm robot manipulates objects. Each counterpart offers the same functionality as its original but changes the environment: a web application or the desktop receives its own layout, navigation, and vi-sual style, and the simulator receives a different robot arm, tabletop, and physical parameters such as friction. The coun-terparts allow us to test whether a learned rule transfers to an unseen environment. 

Demonstrations. For every task and rule version, a hu-man demonstrates the rule, and we write a matching text description. Computer-use rules are demonstrated as screen recordings in our applications; robot rules are demonstrated by hand in the real world, and each real-world video is paired with a simulated video of the same rule. A demonstration shows the rule being applied without stating it, so the agent must infer the rule from what it sees. Every task version is designed by hand and completed by a human through the same UI VERSE interface before release, so it is solv-able through that interface and its verifier accepts a correct solution. 

3.3. Unified Harness 

Computer use and robot use share the same perceive–act loop. Our harness, UI VERSE , exploits this commonality by turning every world into a user interface (Fig. 3): a single frontend renders every environment, whether a web applica-tion, the OS desktop, or the robot simulator, as a graphical interface in a browser, and offers the agent the same small set of tools to observe the screen, act on it, view the demon-stration, and declare completion. UI VERSE thus unifies the interface between agent and world, i.e . observations and ac-tions, while each model keeps its own agent framework. On the robot interface, the agent sees the head and wrist camera views and operates the motion and gripper controls of each arm; on web and OS interfaces, it clicks, types, and scrolls. 3.4. Evaluation Protocol 

Rule versions. Since the three versions of a task demand different behavior in the same situation, an agent that acts on a fixed habit or prior can satisfy at most one of them; consistent success across versions requires following the given rule. 

Generalization levels. We distinguish three levels. R E-

PLAY restores the demonstrated situation. R ESAMPLE sam-ples a new instance of the task independently of the demon-stration, from the same distribution and in the same envi-ronment (IID). R EPLAY is defined only where the demonstration shows the whole task: easy computer-use tasks and most robot tasks, whereas medium and hard computer-use demonstrations teach only the rule-bearing module of a longer workflow. Diagnoses. To separate understanding a rule from execut-ing it, every task comes with a multiple-choice question about its rule. It is posed in a separate branch of the agent’s context, created after the agent has inspected the demonstra-tion and before its first action, so the question and the run share the same context, while the answer never reaches the execution run. To confirm that a task requires ICL, we also run it with the task goal alone and no rule. 

3.5. Metrics 

Success and process score. When the agent declares com-pletion or exhausts its budget, a verifier checks the final state of the environment against the rule version given in the context. A run j succeeds, sj = 1 , only if the goal is reached under that rule. Since binary success hides partial progress, the verifier also assigns a process score pj ∈ [0 , 1] ,with pj = 1 if sj = 1 , which gives partial credit for the rule-bearing steps that a failed run completes correctly. The verifiers and the scoring protocol of each domain are detailed in Appendix D.

4.2. Main Results 

The physical world is much harder. Agents that master in-context rules on screens largely fail to apply them with a robot. In Tab. 3, Claude Opus 5.5 and GPT-6 Astra reach overall scores of 50–55%, and the other four models 9–20%. The strongest agents solve 76–82% of computer-use tasks but only 24–28% of robot-use tasks, and no other model exceeds 12% in robot use. With the model, interface, and rule design shared, the gap lies in applying a learned rule in the physical world, where it must be grounded in camera views and carried out through long sequences of precise arm motions; Sec. 4.4 separates these parts, and process scores show the same gap (Appendix E). 

Difficulty. Success falls with difficulty in computer use, and hard tasks are the hardest in both domains. In com-puter use, success decreases from easy to hard for every model and modality: every model solves about 39% or more of easy tasks, while on medium and hard ones only Claude Opus 5.5 and GPT-6 Astra exceed 30%. In robot use, hard tasks, which require fine manipulation or long workflows, are the hardest for every model, with no model above 8.3%, whereas easy and medium robot tasks are solved at similar rates. 

4.3. Analysis 

4.3.1. Text vs. Video Frontier agents learn rules comparably well from video and from text, whereas weaker agents rely on text. In computer use, the text–video gap ∆tv of Claude Opus 5.5 and GPT-6 Astra lies within two points of zero (Fig. 4a). The other four models all do better with text, by 6.1 points on average ( p = 0 .02 after correction) and by up to 9.1 points for DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash. The same holds in robot use, where their gaps are −2.0 and −4.0 points. 

Video is harder to decode when the rule is a hidden criterion. Rules that are visible in the final state, such as a format, an arrangement, or an order, are learned equally well from both modalities (Fig. 4b). Rules that act as a hidden criterion, i.e . which procedure to follow, which la-bel each item receives, or which items to select, show the largest text advantage, from 4.5 to 9.6 points, most clearly for procedure rules. 4.3.2. Generalization Evaluating only in the demonstrated situation overesti-mates learning from video. In the demonstrated situation, an agent can succeed by repeating what it saw without learn-ing the rule. On the 13 computer-use tasks with R EPLAY 

(Tab. 4, Fig. 4c), text-based success is the same at R EPLAY 

and R ESAMPLE , but video-based success falls by 29.5 points once the instance is resampled ( p < 0.001 ). The fall comes almost entirely from the weaker models (84.6% to 46.2%), whose success at R EPLAY thus largely reflects repeating the demonstrated actions, whereas the frontier agents stay above 85% at both levels. Testing only in the demonstrated situation would thus credit imitation as rule learning. 

Frontier agents transfer learned rules across environ-ments. In computer use, they keep their success when a task moves to a counterpart application with a different layout, navigation, and visual style, staying within three points of RESAMPLE on the 37 tasks with R ELOCATE ; the weaker models even gain, suggesting that some counterparts are easier to navigate. In robot use, every run already transfers a rule from a real-world demonstration to a simulated scene, and this domain gap costs little: a simulated demonstration that matches the execution domain adds at most one task (Tab. 5, Sim ). R ELOCATE further changes the robot arm, the tabletop, and physical parameters, which makes execution itself harder and plausibly explains the lower robot success at R ELOCATE (Fig. 4d).

Rule versions. Few tasks are solved under all three rules. GPT-6 Astra solves 48.6%, 48.6%, and 75.7% of computer-use tasks under versions A, B, and C, but only 37.8% under all three (Tab. 5, bottom); in robot use, no model exceeds 11.8%. Part of this drop reflects the stricter criterion, but the differences between versions also point to properties of the rules themselves, such as their agreement with the agent’s priors, their counterfactual strength, i.e . how far they depart from the behavior the goal alone suggests, and the cues the environment offers for them (Appendix F). Either way, a single rule per task overestimates how reliably an agent follows a user’s rule. 

Understanding vs. execution. Agents usually identify the rule correctly but fail to carry it out. GPT-6 As-tra answers 97.3% of computer-use and 98.0% of robot-use multiple-choice rule questions correctly before acting (Fig. 5), yet succeeds on only 57.7% and 19.6% of the corre-sponding runs; GLM-5.3-Flash answers every robot question correctly but succeeds on 5.9% of the runs. Over all (task, version) pairs, the rule is identified but not executed in 53– 69% of the cases, while success after a wrong answer is rare ( ≤6%). Identifying the rule is thus far from sufficient for completing the task, especially in the physical world (Appendices E, G, and H). 

## 5. Conclusion 

We presented ICLW ORLD , a benchmark that evaluates the in-context learning of agents from the digital to the physi-cal world, with three rule versions per task, text and video rules, and three generalization levels, all under the unified harness UI VERSE . Across six frontier models, the strongest agents solve most computer-use tasks but fail on about three 8quarters of robot-use tasks, and they usually identify the rule correctly yet fail to carry it out, which places the main ob-stacle in execution, above all in the physical world. Frontier agents learn rules comparably well from video and text and transfer them across environments, whereas weaker agents rely on text and, in the demonstrated situation, succeed by repeating the shown actions. Few tasks are solved under all three rules, so evaluating a single rule per task overestimates how reliably agents follow what users ask. We hope that ICLW ORLD helps measure and close these gaps on the way to agents that truly learn from what users show them. 

## 6. Limitations 

Hand-built environments limit ICLW ORLD to 160 tasks, ablations use three models, robot tasks run in simulation, and no task yet combines computer and robot use.

## B. The UIverse Harness 

UIverse presents every world as a graphical interface and gives the agent the same kinds of tools in every world: ob-serve the interface, act on it, inspect the demonstration, and stop (Tab. 7). Each model runs in its own agent CLI with all built-in tools disabled, and the tools of UIverse, served over the Model Context Protocol, are its only access to the world; the agent can neither inspect internal state nor read verifiers or answer keys. 

Episodes. For each episode, the runner binds a task, a rule version and modality, a generalization level, and a seed, and creates an isolated world instance with its own interface and a fresh model session. The first message gives the goal, the rule as text or as a demonstration video, and a screenshot of the interface. 

Digital world. The interface is the application’s own screen. The agent acts in screenshot pixel coordinates with mouse and keyboard inputs, as a human user would, and receives the new screenshot. 

Physical world. The interface is a control panel (Fig. 3) with the head camera and both wrist cameras, buttons that move each arm along its axes, rotate it, and open or close its gripper, fields for the step size and the rotation angle, and 

Commit , Done , and Give up buttons. A status area shows the robot’s public state, such as the end-effector poses, the gripper openings, and the outcome of the previous actions; object poses are never given, so the agent must locate objects in the camera views. In each turn, the agent selects a short sequence of panel actions, each an arm button with its step size or angle, and commits them at once. The simulator exe-cutes the actions in order: an inverse-kinematics controller turns each end-effector motion into joint commands, and the physics engine resolves contacts, friction, and grasping. The agent thus decides where to approach, how far to move, how to orient the gripper, and when to grasp, as a teleoperator would. After the actions, the simulation pauses, and the agent receives the new panel view with feedback on whether each motion was completed or blocked.

Rule versions. No version is uniformly easier: version C is the easiest for GPT-6 Astra in computer use but the hard-est for DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash and GLM-5.3-Flash (Tab. 5). Which version an agent follows thus depends on how the rule meets the agent. Two examples illustrate the properties discussed in Sec. 4.4. In a 2048 task, the rules to maximize the merged tiles or the score agree with common objectives of the game, whereas the rule to keep the largest tile in a corner departs from them; models succeed far more often on the former. In a task that labels problem difficulty, rules based on the number of examples shown on the screen are followed more often than a rule based on the parity of the problem ID, which the interface does not suggest. 

## G. Termination in Robot Use 

A robot episode ends when the agent presses Done , gives up, or reaches the step limit. Tab. 11 shows how failed robot runs end. GPT-6 Astra and Qwen3.8-Max usually give up when they cannot complete a task, whereas Claude Opus 5.5 and GLM-5.3-Flash more often declare completion although the task is unfinished, i.e . they misjudge the final state in the camera views; the step limit rarely ends a run. Together with the rule questions (Fig. 5), this shows that GPT-6 Astra usually knows the rule and, when it fails, recognizes that its execution falls short, which locates the gap in execution rather than in understanding.

## H. Case Study 

Fig. 7 shows a run of GPT-6 Astra on a robot task in which four blocks must be loaded into a 2 ×2 box in a demon-strated color order. Before acting, the agent answers the rule question correctly, and it starts with the first block of the demonstrated order. It lowers the block into the box, but the block does not settle into its cell; the agent repeatedly tries to re-grasp it, then gives up, reporting that it cannot correct the block’s position. The agent knew what to do and failed in how to do it, the pattern behind the gap between understanding and execution.
