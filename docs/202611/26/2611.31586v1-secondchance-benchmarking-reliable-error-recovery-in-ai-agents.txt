# SecondChance: Benchmarking Reliable Error Recovery in AI Agents 

# Atahan Dokme, Adar Avsian, Larry Heck 

AI Virtual Assistant (AVA) Lab, Georgia Institute of Technology {adokme3, adar, larryheck}@gatech.edu 

Abstract 

AI agents make errors while they act, and their reliability de-pends on what they do afterwards. Existing recovery bench-marks mostly inject faults, involve no user and score whether the task is eventually completed, so they cannot show whether an agent undid its own silent error, told the user, or faced an error that could not be fixed. We introduce SecondChance , an error checkpoint dataset and evaluation harness that profiles and measures error-recovery behaviour of AI agents. The er-rors are natural: they come from agents’ own task attempts in 

τ 2-bench, BFCL and AppWorld. Different agents then resume from the same checkpoints under one of four conditions: no as-sistance, an error notice, the error location, or repair guidance. We measure error correction, task success, policy compliance, preservation of environment state, repair durability and cost. We evaluate 12 models under all four conditions in 5,760 con-tinuations. Recovery is rare: across its environments, the best model completes only 16% of recoveries unaided and 27% with repair guidance. Correcting is not recovering: with re-pair guidance, Llama-3.3-70B corrects 26% of τ 2 errors but completes none, and 40 of 84 successful AppWorld continu-ations leave the error in place. SecondChance exposes these differences that task success alone cannot capture. 

# 1 Introduction 

Language models increasingly act through tools: they retrieve information, modify records and carry out tasks through re-peated interaction with users and environments (Yao et al. 2023, 2025; Trivedi et al. 2024). Their actions have conse-quences that persist after the agent moves on, and some of those actions are errors. A reliable agent therefore needs more than task competence. What it does after an error, whether it notices, repairs the damage, tells the user and still finishes the task, determines how far it can be trusted. Task success does not reveal this behaviour. In our Ap-pWorld evaluation, 40 of 84 successful continuations leave the original error in place, including cases where a duplicate payment remains. Conversely, correcting an error does not guarantee that the agent completes the task, follows the ap-plicable policy or keeps the correction. Comparing agents is also difficult: they make different errors at different times, so their recoveries can be compared only if they face the same error from the same state. Existing benchmarks leave this gap open (Table 1). Task benchmarks such as τ 2-bench, BFCL and AppWorld start every episode from a clean state (Barres et al. 2025; Patil et al. 2025; Trivedi et al. 2024). Recovery benchmarks start from an error, but most inject it: UndoBench drops the ac-knowledgment of a state-changing call, Hell or High Water makes functions fail, and CRITICTOOL and ParaRecover insert tool errors into recorded trajectories (Sah et al. 2026; Wang et al. 2025; Huang et al. 2025; Guan, Yin, and Shen 2026). Such errors announce themselves through a failure or a missing acknowledgment, whereas an agent’s own error usually does not: enabling roaming on the wrong line returns a success message. CUA-Recovery and GUI-RobustEval do start from agents’ own errors, but in desktop computer use without a user, and they evaluate error awareness and sub-sequent task completion (Hu et al. 2026; Bu et al. 2026). None of these benchmarks asks whether the agent told the user about the error or establishes for each error whether a fix exists, and none separates being told that an error occurred from being told where it is or how to repair it. 

SecondChance is an error checkpoint dataset and evalua-tion harness built for this comparison (Figure 1). It asks one question: given the same error and environment state, which agents recover, what assistance helps them, and what does recovery require? The errors are natural. We identify them in agents’ own successful and unsuccessful task attempts, select them by a fixed procedure, and reconstruct the recorded his-tory and environment state immediately after each selected error. Using the original tasks, tools, policies and evalua-tors, the harness then resumes different agents from the same checkpoint under four conditions: U (Unaided) , with no as-sistance; N (Notified) , with an error notice; L (Localized) ,with the error location; and PA (Plan-assisted) , with repair guidance. The conditions are cumulative and run from hard-est to easiest: L names the action that N only says was wrong, and PA adds what that action changed, a verified way to undo it and when to involve the user. Before any agent is evaluated, we also establish whether each error can be fixed at all: a fix is demonstrated by executing it on the restored checkpoint, proven impossible from the environment’s own code, or left unknown. This separates an agent that did not fix an error from an error that could not be fixed. We evaluate 12 models under all four conditions across 129 checkpoints, 5,760 continuations in all. # 2 Related Work 

Evaluating tool-using agents. τ -bench and τ 2-bench eval-uate agents interacting with users, tools and domain policies (Yao et al. 2025; Barres et al. 2025), while AppWorld pro-vides persistent application environments with executable task and state checks (Trivedi et al. 2024). Evaluation be-yond task completion is also central to ST-WebAgentBench, which measures completion under applicable safety policies (Levy et al. 2026). Reliable interaction also depends on how a model uses the conversation: in task-oriented dialogue, mod-els often retain the information they need but fail to resolve which constraints currently apply or to let them govern the next action (Dokme and Heck 2026a). SecondChance builds on these environments and concerns, focusing on what hap-pens after an agent has already made an error. 

Recovery from agent errors. Recovery benchmarks dif-fer first in where the error comes from. Most inject it: UndoBench drops acknowledgments of state-changing API calls and reports recovery conditional on the agent’s suc-cess without the fault (Sah et al. 2026), Hell or High Wa-ter makes functions unavailable (Wang et al. 2025), CRIT-ICTOOL, ReflecTool-Bench and ParaRecover test the cri-tique and repair of mostly synthetic tool-call errors (Huang et al. 2025; Liu et al. 2026; Guan, Yin, and Shen 2026), and Lost in Recovery perturbs desktop states (Bang et al. 2026). CUA-Recovery and GUI-RobustEval start from computer-use agents’ own errors (Hu et al. 2026; Bu et al. 2026), and harm recovery has been studied from scripted harmful desktop states (Li et al. 2026). AgentErrorBench locates the root-cause errors in real agent rollouts and turns them into corrective feedback for a new attempt (Zhu et al. 2025). SecondChance takes errors from agents’ own tool-use trajectories, restores the same checkpoint for every agent, grades the assistance, scores correction, protected state, dis-closure and task success separately, and records whether each error can be fixed (Table 1). 

Self-correction and feedback. Self-correction without re-liable feedback can fail even when a model is prompted to reconsider its answer (Huang et al. 2024; Avsian et al. 2026). CRITIC uses external tools to support verification and revi-sion, while Reflexion uses feedback and verbal reflection to improve subsequent attempts (Gou et al. 2024; Shinn et al. 2023). Our four assistance conditions hold the error and the state fixed and vary only the information the agent is given, as controlled studies of reasoning do with the wording or context of a fixed problem (Shi et al. 2023; Mirzadeh et al. 2025; Dokme, Reichman, and Heck 2026). 

Reversibility and side effects. Relative reachability con-nects side-effect penalties to changes in the states an agent can still reach (Krakovna et al. 2019), and learned reset policies seek to avoid states from which returning becomes difficult (Eysenbach et al. 2018). SecondChance brings this concern to the evaluation of tool-using agents: for each recovery tar-get we distinguish an executed repair route, a certificate of impossibility under stated assumptions, and unknown reach-ability. 

# 3 The SecondChance Benchmark 

SecondChance has three parts (Figure 1). The task envi-ronment is the original task with its tools, state, policies and interaction protocol, including a simulated user where applicable. The checkpoint dataset contains saved histories and restorable states after selected errors, with their eval-uation annotations. The evaluation harness is the software that restores and verifies a checkpoint, supplies the assigned assistance, runs the model, records its actions and scores the continuation. A continuation is one model’s attempt to proceed from a checkpoint under one assistance condition. 3.1 Task Environments 

We reuse three sources of tasks, each with its own tools, protocol and task evaluator: 

• τ 2-bench: telecom and banking tasks in which agents in-teract with a simulated user, call domain tools and follow service policies (Barres et al. 2025). 

• BFCL multi-turn: sequences of scripted user requests requiring tool calls that change persistent application state (Patil et al. 2025). 

• AppWorld: tasks completed through APIs across per-sonal applications, with database changes checked by ex-ecutable tests (Trivedi et al. 2024). Ten open-weight models attempted these tasks and produced 4,568 valid source episodes on 487 tasks. Errors arise during these attempts; none is inserted. 

## 3.2 Error Checkpoints 

Finding and selecting errors. A mechanical screen re-plays every source episode, successful or not, and flags state changes that depart from the task’s reference, such as an incorrect field value, a modified protected attribute or an unwanted database record. A flag is a candidate, not an er-ror label: an action that differs from the reference solution may be a valid alternative. Candidates are then selected by a fixed procedure that balances kinds of error and source-model sizes and never reads how any model later recovers, and each selected candidate must restore exactly when its episode is replayed. Finally, an AI scan flags the selected candidates whose status as an error is doubtful, and each flagged candidate receives an individual evidence-based re-view of whether the action was an error given the request, policy and information available when it occurred; candi-dates without a flag are kept on the mechanical evidence. Each checkpoint’s error still leaves something to repair, and the screen finds no other unresolved error in the state after it, so a resuming agent inherits exactly one detected error. This yields 129 checkpoints: 38 from τ 2, 37 from BFCL and 54 from AppWorld (Appendix A.1). The dataset describes the evaluation population, not how often agents make errors. 

Contents. Each checkpoint has two parts. The first is what an agent needs to resume: the environment state after the error and the history visible to the agent. The second is an evaluation record (Figure 1; Appendix Table 6), which is never included in the model’s input unless an assistance con-dition explicitly supplies part of it. The surrounding history is kept as recorded, so it can show earlier errors that were undone before the checkpoint. Recovery is defined for the selected effect together with the registered task, protection and interaction requirements. 

Fixability. Each checkpoint also carries a label recording what we could establish about fixing its error, assigned be-fore any agent is evaluated and with no model involved. A fix is demonstrated when a sequence of permitted actions, executed on the restored checkpoint, satisfies the correction target; it is proven impossible when the environment’s own code shows that no available operation can produce the tar-get; otherwise it is unknown . ## 3.3 Evaluation Harness 

For each run, the harness restores a fresh copy of the check-point and verifies it before the model acts. The model receives the recorded history and continues through the environment’s own tools and interaction protocol; where there is a simulated user, it responds live to the new continuation. The original error remains in the restored state, and the harness does not repair it: the model must address it through its available ac-tions. Most continuations inherit another model’s history, so the experiment measures recovery from a shared starting point, not only from a model’s own errors. The harness implements runners for the three environ-ments above and connects to models through an OpenAI-compatible chat-completions endpoint or the OpenAI Re-sponses API. An agent that can be served through one of these interfaces can be evaluated on the same checkpoints; other models and environments require an adapter. 

Assistance conditions. The harness varies the information supplied about the error (Table 2). Repair plans describe verified routes where they exist, state established limits when a target is unreachable, and remind the agent of relevant consent requirements. They do not invent user consent or supply values available only in the task reference. Assistance texts are fixed before the continuations they are used in. A separate control, in which the harness itself applies the correction, is labelled as such and reported apart from these conditions (Section 4). Step budgets and simulated-user models are listed in Ap-pendix Table 9. 

## 3.4 Scoring 

Each continuation is scored on separate measures: 

• Correction: the selected effect satisfies its correction tar-get, at an observed state and at termination. 

• Task success: the continuation passes the environment’s original task evaluator, applied from the task’s original starting state (Appendix B.1). • Protection: the required protected state is intact; a change the user requested and authorized is not a violation. 

• User-goal achievement: in environments with a user, whether the user’s requests as expressed in the conversa-tion were fulfilled, judged separately from the task evalu-ator. 

• Policy and authorization: whether each executed agent write was permitted by the environment’s policy. 

• Disclosure and false claims: whether and when the agent told the user about the error, whether it asked before correcting it, and whether it claimed something that did not happen. 

• Closing report: whether the agent’s last messages state the outcome, and accurately. This is reported separately and is not required for acceptance. 

• Durability and work: whether a later action undoes an observed correction, and the additional agent calls, to-kens, simulated-user turns and API expenditure. 

Recovery outcomes. Each continuation is scored sepa-rately against the checkpoint’s correction and protection tar-gets. State repair requires the error to be corrected and pro-tected state preserved at termination, regardless of task suc-cess. Complete recovery additionally requires task success. In τ 2 and the retail and airline track, it also requires achieve-ment of the user’s expressed goal, policy-compliant agent writes, no false claims, and disclosure: the agent must tell the user about the error, before or after correcting it. Si-lence fails, while asking for agreement before the corrective write is allowed but not required: the correction restores the user’s own state, and asking costs an extra turn. Disclosure is a SecondChance requirement, distinct from the source policies; Appendix D.2 also reports a stricter consent-first reading that requires disclosure before the correction and the user’s explicit agreement to it. An otherwise acceptable correction that leaves the request unfinished is classified as correction only when the agent acknowledges the remaining work or hands the user to a human agent. A closing report is not mandatory, but false claims prevent acceptance. Appendix B.2 specifies the full decision rule. 

Scoring and cost. Environment checks measure correc-tion, task success and protection. In τ 2 and retail/airline, can-didates passing the mechanical checks receive an evidence-based review by a pinned Qwen3-235B-A22B judge with a fixed rubric. Unsupported judgments are rechecked, with remaining cases adjudicated by the authors; Appendix D.2 reports these decisions and a stricter consent sensitivity anal-ysis. Complete-recovery rates use all scheduled runs as the denominator. We record agent calls, tokens, simulated-user interactions and API expenditure from the checkpoint onward. Costs are reported alongside outcomes, so unsuccessful early stops are not interpreted as efficient recovery. Work to first correction is reported separately, conditional on reaching it. Two con-duct measures describe runs whether or not they recover. 

Acknowledged records whether the agent tells the user about the error at any point after the checkpoint; it is judged from the agent’s messages and requires a quoted message. Further changes records, from the executed calls, whether a run that never corrects the error makes another change on the entity that the error affected; it does not judge whether that change is itself an error. We also check whether a correction is sub-sequently undone, distinguishing temporary correction from correction preserved at termination. 

Repairability over time. For selected source trajectories, we examine whether correction remains possible at succes-sive states. An exact closing point requires a demonstrated route immediately before an action and an impossibility cer-tificate immediately after it. This differs from undoing a cor-rection, which may still be repairable. 

# 4 Results 

## 4.1 Evaluation Setup 

The main study evaluates 12 models: ten open-weight mod-els and the hosted GPT-5.4-mini and GPT-6.1-sol. Every model runs every τ 2 and BFCL checkpoint under all four conditions, and the open-weight models also run AppWorld, with one run per checkpoint, model and condition: 5,760 continuations. Open-weight models decode greedily. Three supporting studies are reported in the appendix: a compari-son that adds GPT-5.5 on the 37 τ 2 and BFCL checkpoints with a demonstrated fix, repeated runs with three samples per cell on the same checkpoints, and competence controls on 18 BFCL checkpoints. Appendix C lists models and de-coding settings, and Appendix C.3 gives study populations, exclusions and run counts. We report 95% percentile confidence intervals from 2,000 bootstrap resamples of source tasks, retaining all check-points, models, conditions and repetitions within each sam-pled task to preserve matched comparisons. Table 3 is the main evaluation: every model on every check-point under all four conditions, pooled over the environments the model was run in, with the rate of runs that correct the error and the rate that complete the recovery; Appendix Table 12 gives the counts in each environment. Complete recovery is the primary outcome. Figure 2 and Appendix Ta-ble 11 are a diagnostic analysis on the 57 checkpoints with a demonstrated fix (Section 4.6). AppWorld is run with the ten open-weight models only, and we do not combine the measures into one score.

## 4.3 Assistance Helps Different Models in Different Ways 

For most models a notice or the location leaves correction unchanged. Summed over models, corrected runs go from 9 unaided to 11 with a notice and 14 with the location in 

τ 2 (of 455), stay at 11 in BFCL (of 444), and go from 8 to 11 and 8 in AppWorld (of 538). A plan raises them to 54, 72 and 25. Against unaided continuation, a plan adds 10.0 percentage points of correction in τ 2 ([5 .7, 14 .9] ), 13.7 in BFCL ( [6 .8, 21 .2] ) and 3.3 in AppWorld ( [0 .8, 6.2] ), and 2.0 ( [0 .9, 3.2] ), 6.8 ( [2 .4, 11 .7] ) and 2.1 ( [0 .2, 4.4] ) points of complete recovery. The location points to the erroneous step in a history the agent can read, yet most models still do not correct it. As in task-oriented dialogue (Dokme and Heck 2026a), holding the information does not by itself update the agent’s state of the task or govern its next action. A plan supplies that update; without one, the agent must itself select the evidence in its history that bears on the error (Dokme and Heck 2026b). Plan-assisted results measure recovery given repair guidance, not unprompted error detection. The conditions do not act on every model in the same way. Eleven models correct no τ 2 error under the first three conditions, so for them only a plan matters. For GPT-6.1-sol each step of assistance raises both correction (24%, 29%, 37% and 42%) and complete recovery (21%, 24%, 29% and 34%). 

Telling the user is enough, and asking costs more. Told where the error is, GPT-6.1-sol corrects 14 τ 2 errors and completes 11 recoveries; in nine of them it fixes the error first and tells the user afterwards, and in ten it never asks. With a plan, which tells it to explain the error and ask before correcting, it completes 13 of its 16 corrections, all after asking. Asking takes longer: its complete recoveries take a median of 15 agent calls with a plan, against 11 without one. Three samples per checkpoint show the same ordering (Appendix Table 13). 

## 4.4 What Agents Do Instead of Recovering 

The conduct measures of Appendix Table 11 describe the runs that do not recover. Silence. Without a plan, GPT-6.1-sol tells the user about the error in 74% of its τ 2 runs; no other model does so in more than 6%, even when it has been told that an error exists or where it is. Saying without fixing. 

With a plan, six models tell the user in more than 60% of their runs, but several of them correct far less often: gpt-oss-20b acknowledges the error in 61% of its τ 2 runs and corrects none, and Qwen3-4B acknowledges it in 67% and corrects 17%. Further changes to the affected entity. Some models that never correct the error go on changing the entity it affected, for example by enabling roaming on the same line again or by buying data for it: gpt-oss-20b does so in 26% of its τ 2 runs and Llama-3.1-8B in 25%, against none for GPT-6.1-sol, Gemma-4-31B and Qwen3.5-35B-A3B. We report this descriptively and have not judged whether each further change is itself an error. 

## 4.5 What Complete Recovery Still Requires 

Correcting the error is where recovery starts. In τ 2, 54 plan-assisted runs correct the error and 17 of them are complete recoveries; 30 are correction only, three are rejected and four are held. ## 4.6 Diagnostic Analysis: When a Fix Is Known to Exist 

The main table counts every checkpoint, including errors that cannot be fixed. Figure 2 and Appendix Table 11 restrict the analysis to the 57 checkpoints with a demonstrated fix, where a run that does not correct the error has missed a fix known to exist. Even there, the best model completes 54% of the recoveries in τ 2 and BFCL with a plan, six of the twelve models complete fewer than 10%, and without a plan nine models correct nothing; in AppWorld the best open-weight model completes 30%. 

Fixability. No run in any condition corrects an error whose fix was proven impossible, and runs correct only four of the 57 checkpoints whose fixability is unknown. Of the 57 checkpoints with a demonstrated fix, 12 are never corrected in any run we have, including the supporting studies: three in BFCL and nine in AppWorld. Figure 2 shows the spread between these errors and the ones that most models correct. 

Kind of error. Among the BFCL checkpoints with a demonstrated fix, a plan leads to the correction of an un-wanted change elsewhere in 57 of 156 runs, of a wrong entity in 11 of 48 and of a wrong value in 2 of 24; unaided, wrong-entity errors are the ones corrected (8 of 48, against one run for each of the other kinds). In AppWorld a wrong value on a task record is the one kind corrected without help (8 of 40 runs unaided, 6 with a plan), an unwanted record elsewhere needs a plan (0 against 11 of 40), and a removed record is almost never restored (2 of 60 with a plan). In τ 2, 17 of the 18 checkpoints with a demonstrated fix are wrong-entity errors, so kinds cannot be compared there. 

Errors with no way back. In the 30 BFCL source trajecto-ries that we analysed state by state, every exact closing point of the repair window, nine in all, falls on the erroneous ac-tion itself, while 14 errors were still fixable at the end of the trajectory (Appendix E). The first group calls for prevention; for the second, recovery remained possible to the end. 

## 4.7 Fixes Mostly Last, and Recovery Is Expensive 

Most corrections survive to the end of the run: on the check-points with a demonstrated fix the share kept is between 67% and 100% for every model. Still, across all checkpoints 13 of the 84 plan-assisted BFCL runs that corrected the error at some point later undid it, as did four of 58 in τ 2 and one of 26 in AppWorld. Even a correction supplied by the harness is not safe: in the corrected-state control, 20 of 180 runs undid it at least once. On the τ 2 and BFCL checkpoints with a demonstrated fix, plan-assisted runs take a median of 7 to 12 agent calls. Counting every attempt, one complete recovery costs GPT-6.1-sol 21 agent calls and Gemma-4-31B 27, but Qwen3-235B 391 and Llama-3.3-70B 415, because most of their runs do not complete. 

Supporting studies. The appendix reports three further studies on the 37 τ 2 and BFCL checkpoints with a demon-strated fix: repeated runs with three samples per cell, a com-parison that adds GPT-5.5 (Appendix Table 13), and compe-tence controls (Appendix F), together with a retail and airline track. 

# 5 Conclusion 

SecondChance pairs a dataset of error checkpoints with a harness that resumes different agents from the same error and scores what they do next. Across 12 models, telling an agent that an error occurred or where it occurred barely changes correction, while a repair plan raises it substantially. Correcting the error does not ensure that the agent finishes the task or meets the remaining recovery requirements: on the τ 2 and BFCL checkpoints with a demonstrated fix, the best model completes 54% of the recoveries with a plan, and six of the twelve models complete fewer than 10%. Models also differ in what they do when they do not recover: most stay silent without a plan, some tell the user about the error without fixing it, and some go on changing the entity that the error affected. Task success can conceal unrepaired damage, later actions can undo a correction, and 12 of the 57 errors with a demonstrated fix are never corrected by any model. Agents should therefore be evaluated on whether they address their errors, keep the correction and complete the user’s task, together with the work this takes. 

# Limitations 

SecondChance measures recovery from selected errors with verifiable checkpoints. Of the 129 checkpoints, 102 were re-tained after mechanical screening and an AI scan, and 27 were reviewed individually. The screen covers errors with an effect on state, so an earlier error without one, such as wrong information given to the user, can remain in a check-point’s history. The diagnostic analysis focuses on 57 simpler errors with demonstrated fixes, typically involving a single field or effect. Fixability remains unknown for 57 check-points. In τ 2-bench, two authors independently agreed with the AI judge on all 60 answers across ten cases. The ac-knowledgment measure lacks human validation, and 32 of 1,795 judgments without matching message evidence were scored as unacknowledged. The main study uses one run per checkpoint, model, and condition, with repeats and GPT-5.5 coverage limited to 37 checkpoints. All 18 τ 2-bench check-points with demonstrated fixes come from 14 telecom tasks. AppWorld includes only open-weight models, hosted mod-els come from one provider, and one hosted model uses an undated alias. Models may have seen the public AppWorld, retail, and airline tasks. The repair-window analysis covers a subset of BFCL trajectories. 

# Ethics Statement 

The benchmark uses public simulated environments and sim-ulated users; no human participant data was collected. The dataset contains fictitious users, orders, phone lines and app data from τ 2-bench and AppWorld. Human reviewers of sam-pled continuations see only simulated data. Releasing agent failure traces could in principle show how agents act on the wrong account or without consent; we release them be-cause they are already reproducible from public benchmarks and because measuring such behaviour is the purpose of the benchmark.

# A Benchmark Construction 

## A.1 Source Episodes and Selection 

Ten open-weight models generated 4,568 valid source episodes: 2,800 BFCL, 1,470 AppWorld and 298 τ 2 episodes from the telecom and banking domains. Successful and un-successful episodes are both screened, because passing the task evaluator does not show that every earlier action was correct. The 24 retail and airline checkpoints are kept apart from the main bank and run with five open-weight models under all four conditions (480 continuations); their tasks and AppWorld’s are public, so models may have seen them. As in the main bank, no other agent write before any of them leaves a detected error unresolved; consequences the inter-face cannot undo, such as price differences, are recorded as residuals. A mechanical screen flags candidate errors: an attribute moved away from its reference value, a change to protected information or an unwanted database row. An AI scan then flags candidates whose status as an error is doubtful; each flagged candidate is reviewed individually against the user’s request, the policy and the information available when the action occurred, and an unflagged candidate is kept on the mechanical evidence. A valid alternative to the reference is not an error, a later request does not authorize an earlier action, a failed tool call is not a state change, and an error whose effect a later request of the task removes leaves nothing to repair. A checkpoint can follow other, earlier errors only if the screen finds all of them undone in the state at the checkpoint; the history is kept as recorded. The screen flags 1,886 candidates in 1,185 of the 4,568 episodes (Table 4). Candidates are grouped by environment, kind of error and size class of the source model (up to 8B pa-rameters, 20B to 35B, 70B and above), 55 groups in all, and ordered within each group by a SHA-256 hash of their iden-tifier; groups take turns, one candidate per round, until the environment’s quota is reached or every group is exhausted. Selection takes at most two checkpoints per task, passes over a candidate that fails the restoration check (15 in τ 2), does not refill a shortfall and never looks at continuation outcomes. Of the 129 checkpoints, 102 rest on the mechanical screen and the scan, and 27 on an individual review. Table 5 gives the kinds of error and the fixability established for each. ## A.2 Checkpoints, Restoration and Continuation 

A checkpoint holds the environment state immediately af-ter the selected error, the agent-visible history up to that point, and the task, source trajectory, selected action, tool configuration and restoration procedure. Its evaluator anno-tations (selected effect, correction predicate, protected infor-mation, policy requirements and supporting evidence) are never shown to the model unless a condition supplies part of them. Each continuation starts from a fresh restoration, verified with the checks implemented for that environment: recorded tool outputs, database or field values and state hashes. The model acts only after these checks pass, and the simulated user responds to the new continuation; recorded user replies are never replayed. Restoring a checkpoint and replaying a finished continuation are separate operations: a replay can diverge, for example on a network-dependent command, and outcomes that depend on the divergence are then unverified. If a serving stack rejects the restored history before the model acts, the job has no model outcome; it counts as not recovered and is reported separately (Appendix C.3). 

# B Outcome Definitions and Acceptance 

## B.1 Component Outcomes 

Correction holds when the checkpoint’s predicate for the selected effect is satisfied. It is checked at every observ-able state (a tool action, code block or scripted turn) and at termination, and is never inferred for an unobserved state; finishing later requested work does not by itself correct the selected effect. Task success is the original evaluator’s re-sult, computed from the task’s original starting state and never substituted for correction or for the user’s goal: τ 2’s full evaluator applied to the whole conversation (also for the retail and airline tasks), AppWorld’s evaluation of the final state confirmed by replay, and BFCL’s per-turn state check on every turn from the checkpoint’s turn to the last, so a run that stops early fails; BFCL’s comparison of returned tool outputs is not used. Protection requires other protected state to stay intact; in τ 2 and the retail and airline tasks a protected change is accepted only when the review shows that the user requested and authorized it. 

Authorization is judged at the time of each action: a later request does not authorize an earlier write, user-device ac-tions are not attributed to the agent, and a failed or ineffective write is not a change. The policy check applies the environ-ment’s policy, in which some prerequisites hold whatever the user says and some clauses direct an action without a separate confirmation; beyond the policy, complete recov-ery does not require the user’s agreement to the corrective write. The stricter consent-first reading requires the user’s explicit agreement to the corrective write after the agent has described it (agreement to another action, help from the experimenter or a future choice does not count, and the re-quirement does not apply when the agent made no corrective write); the strict reading also requires explicit consent to ev-ery other state-changing write. Each stricter reading can only remove acceptances. 

Disclosure is telling the user about the error, before or after correcting it; complete recovery requires it, and the consent-first reading requires it before the correction. The review also asks whether the user’s goal was achieved, whether unfin-ished work was acknowledged (the user is handed to a human agent, or the agent’s last message says the request is not re-solved, says what remains or gives a step not carried out by the end) and whether the agent made a false claim , checked against execution evidence; an absent statement is not a false one. The closing report (none, accurate, inaccurate or un-certain) is a secondary measure that no acceptance line uses. 

Durability records whether an observed correction is later undone and whether it is corrected again before termination, and work covers agent calls and tokens, simulated-user turns and words, and calculated API expenditure (Appendix F). 

Acknowledged is YES when a message to the user after the checkpoint says that the earlier action or change was an error, was wrong or unintended, or has to be or has been undone; the pinned judge must quote the message, and an answer whose quote is not found counts as not acknowledged. Fur-ther changes is YES when a run never corrects the error and executes another state-changing call whose arguments name an entity of the correction target; it is computed from the executed calls of τ 2 runs and does not establish a violation of the request, the policy or a protection requirement.

## B.4 Statistics and Denominators 

All intervals are 95% percentile intervals from a cluster boot-strap with 2,000 resamples and seed 0. runs per model and condition are not 54 independent tasks. Paired comparisons join identical checkpoint, model and rep-etition keys, and work differences are medians of within-pair differences. ## C.2 Assistance Texts 

Every condition starts from the same restored state and his-tory. The notice (N) is one sentence: “Note: an earlier action taken in this conversation was a mistake.” The location (L) adds the erroneous action, for example “. . . a mistake: the tool call enable_roaming(...) (message 50 of this conversation)”, with the call’s arguments, in τ 2 and “. . . the code you ran at step 5” in AppWorld. Plans (PA) link each clause to its source: the observed history, the earlier state, in-formation the tools can return, the policy and verified routes. They never supply invented consent, future user choices, or corrective values and goal steps that are available only in the task reference, and an unverified route is not presented as a solution. For a target proven impossible, the plan separates restoration, which is unavailable, from disclosure, mitigation and the remaining task work. All texts were fixed before the runs that use them.

# E Repairability, Repair Windows and Durability 

## E.1 Reachability Evidence 

Each checkpoint records reachability evidence separately for correction, task completion and complete recovery, as one of three classes. Demonstrated : an executed route, called a witness, reaches the target from the restored checkpoint under recorded assumptions. Proven impossible : a certifi-cate shows that no permitted sequence of operations reaches the target under its stated assumptions. Unknown : neither is established; an unsuccessful search is not an impossibility certificate. Route provenance is recorded: a route shows that a fix exists, not that a model would find it, and a route that needs a privileged operation or applies user-device actions directly does not show availability to the agent. Checkpoint selection and the fixability labels were set before evaluation; repairability evidence found later is recorded separately and changes neither.

## E.4 Correction Durability 

A regression is a correction that a later observed state un-does (Table 18). # F Competence Controls and Recovery Costs 

## F.1 Competence Controls 

The controls use the 18 BFCL checkpoints whose attached corrective route re-derives exactly and meets the declared correction and protection targets, with ten open-weight mod-els (180 cells per condition). The selection did not look at continuation outcomes. Two conditions add 360 continua-tions. With exact corrective calls (PO), the plan also lists the demonstrated corrective calls and their arguments, which the model must still execute before continuing the task. With 

corrected state and history (CS), the harness executes those calls and inserts the resulting tool-call step into the history, and the model continues with the unaided instructions. CS is a privileged intervention that changes both state and his-tory; it is compared with U, PA and a clean start from the task’s initial state, taken from the source episodes of the same models and tasks. Exact corrective calls improve correction more clearly than complete recovery: against PA, PO adds 14.4 points of cor-rection ( [5 .8, 23 .5] ; 26 runs) but only 3.3 points of com-plete recovery ( [−1.2, 8.2] ; six runs). The corrected state raises task success over unaided continuation by 44.4 points ([30 .0, 60 .0] ), yet 20 CS runs undo the supplied correction at least once and 17 end with it undone. Task success after a clean start is 10.6 points lower than after CS ( [−25 .3, 2.0] ); the comparison is uncertain and does not show that correc-tion restores clean-start performance. Intervals are paired source-task bootstrap intervals, a difference near zero does not establish equivalence, and these controls are specific to BFCL. 

## F.2 Work and Expenditure 

Work counts new requests from the resumed checkpoint to termination, including unsuccessful, truncated and unfin-ished attempts. Historical calls that reconstruct the check-point are excluded, but the input tokens that re-send the re-stored history in each new request are included. Unreported usage is missing, not zero, and simulated-user turns measure the simulator’s participation, not human effort. Table 17 re-ports work in matched cells for the ten open-weight models (five of them in τ 2) and for the three-sample hosted compar-ison; agent calls of all 12 models are in Table 11. Among the open-weight plan-assisted runs that reached a correction, the first correction took a median of two agent calls and one simulated-user turn in τ 2 (21 runs), one agent call in BFCL (57) and two in AppWorld (26); a run without a correction has no work-to-correction value and is not assigned zero. Calculated expenditure uses recorded token usage and de-clared prices; it is not a provider bill, and money moved by actions inside a task is an environment action, not an API cost. In the repeated and hosted study, the hosted models cost $92.19: $13.24 for the agents in BFCL, $39.22 for the agents in τ 2 and $39.73 for the τ 2 simulated user. The τ 2 simulated user of the open-model runs cost $52.01; local GPU time is not converted to dollars. The remaining main-study runs cost a further $47.10, calculated in the same way, for the hosted agents and the simulated user. 

# G Validation and Reproducibility 

## G.1 Human Review 

A human-review packet presents ten resumed τ 2 conversa-tions from the reviewed runs of the repeated and hosted study in full: the selected error, the assistance text, the conversation, the tool calls and results, and the agent’s policy. Annotators use only the packet, work alone until both have answered, judge each action with the information and policy the agent had at that moment, count only the user’s own words as consent, and answer Unsure whenever the record does not settle a question. For each case they answer six questions: (1) whether the erroneous fields are back at their correct val-ues at the end; (2) whether the agent told the user what the error was before correcting it; (3) whether the user explicitly agreed to the corrective change after the agent described it; (4) whether the user’s requests were achieved, explicitly left incomplete by the agent’s last message, or not achieved; (5) whether the agent’s statements about what it did, or what something costs, match the tool results; and (6) whether the agent executed any other change without the user’s explicit agreement to it. Question 4 is compared with the judge’s goal label and the last-message part of the unfinished-work rule, and question 6 with the consent component rather than pol-icy compliance. ## G.2 Consistency Checks and Reproduction 

Released checks confirm that every complete recovery meets its acceptance requirements, that corrections, regressions and call totals reconcile with the records, that each stricter read-ing only removes acceptances and that paired comparisons join identical keys. They establish internal consistency, not the correctness of every semantic judgment or certificate. A reproduction restores and verifies each checkpoint, resumes it under the declared settings without evaluator-only fields, and applies the acceptance rule with the declared pairing and denominators. The release identifies checkpoint mani-fests, assistance texts, runner and scorer versions, fixability evidence and the attempt chosen for each job; distribution follows the source environments’ licenses and restrictions.
