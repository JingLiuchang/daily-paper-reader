Abstract 

In multi-agent systems (MAS) built on large language models (LLMs), errors can propagate through intermediate messages and compromise final answers. However, outcome-based evaluation obscures how agents amplify, attenuate, or correct these errors. We propose Response-Aware Error Propagation among Agents (REPA), a structural model that connects measured local responses to system-level error propagation. REPA separately estimates input assembly and agent processing across multiple error channels, while theoretical analysis relates local modeling errors to endpoint prediction error. Experiments span three Qwen base models, six MAS workflows, and ten knowledge and reasoning settings in ErrPropBench-10. REPA achieves a mean condition-level Spearman correlation of 0.84 across base models, compared with 0.47 for direct regression. Controlled experiments also demonstrate its utility for targeted recovery. REPA-guided retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) improves accuracy over no retrieval by an average of 19 percentage points with 16% additional tokens. By linking local agent behavior to system susceptibility, REPA provides an interpretable foundation for studying error propagation and developing targeted optimization in MAS. 

# 1 Introduction 

Multi-agent systems (MAS) built on large language models (LLMs) address complex tasks through role specialization and structured communication (Wu et al., 2023; Li et al., 2023; Hong et al., 2024). Within these workflows, downstream agents incorporate intermediate results from upstream agents into their reasoning and decision-making. This dependence, however, also creates pathways through which inaccurate external information or erroneous agent responses can propagate and compromise the final answer (Huang et al., 2025; Jia et al., 2026). Understanding MAS reliability therefore requires examining how individual agents respond to erroneous information and how workflow dependencies link these local responses to the final task outcome. This local-to-system relationship cannot be captured by final-answer correctness alone (Liu et al., 2026; Jia et al., 2026). An agent may reject, revise, or adopt misleading information, and the workflow then determines which downstream agents receive its response (Huang et al., 2025; Shen et al., 2025). Moreover, different patterns of error adoption and correction can produce the same final correctness label, obscuring how erroneous information was handled within the workflow. Therefore, a propagation analysis should examine intermediate inputs and outputs alongside task outcomes, distinguishing changes in message content from changes in task performance.

Some optimization approaches require additional training, whereas post-hoc attribution relies on completed execution traces (Shen et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2025). A complementary question is whether system-level propagation can be predicted from local measurements without fine-tuning the underlying LLMs or observing downstream test outputs. Answering this question requires distinguishing how erroneous information changes an agent’s input from how the agent responds across multiple error channels. These local responses must then be composed through downstream processing and aggregation, since local susceptibility alone does not determine the final system response. The central challenge is therefore to connect separately estimated local responses with independently validated system-level predictions. To connect measured local responses with system-level predictions, we propose Response-Aware Error Propagation among Agents (REPA), a structural model of error propagation across multiple error channels. Reference and perturbed executions of the same instance are compared under matched conditions, yielding continuous-valued changes at corresponding agent inputs and outputs for separately estimating input-assembly and agent-processing responses. REPA then composes these locally estimated response models along the executed workflow, explicitly accounting for aggregation and finite-round feedback. Once fitted, it predicts system-level propagation from source measurements and execution structure, without fine-tuning the underlying LLMs or observing test-time intermediate states or final outputs. Under the modeling assumptions, theoretical analysis characterizes this composition and relates local modeling errors to endpoint prediction error. Our evaluation covers six MAS workflows and single-agent baselines using open-source foundation models of varying sizes (Qwen Team, 2024; 2026a). To establish a reliable measurement basis for local response estimation and system-level validation, we first assess observer agreement with independent reference judgments, then evaluate REPA’s predictions within fixed conditions. To extend the evaluation across multiple knowledge and reasoning tasks, we construct ErrPropBench-10, covering seven domains and three multi-hop question-answering datasets. Further experiments demonstrate that REPA-derived estimates can guide node selection, targeted prompt restoration, and retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), supporting both agent-level refinement and MAS-level optimization at a reasonable inference cost. Failure diagnosis and propagation modeling. MAST characterizes failure modes (Cemri et al., 2025), while Who&When attributes failures from execution traces (Zhang et al., 2025). Controlled-fault evaluations assess robustness (Huang et al., 2025; Jia et al., 2026; Yu et al., 2025), and MASEval evaluates system-level performance (Emde et al., 2026b). Structural studies examine influence-driven topology learning, uncertainty preservation, hallucination dynamics, and preference transmission (Shen et al., 2025; Emde et al., 2026a; Jamshidi et al., 2026; Jamshidi, 2026; Liu, 2026; Niu et al., 2026). These studies establish the importance of interaction structure, although their influence, uncertainty, and preference measures target different quantities from paired semantic-error changes. 

Agent refinement and targeted recovery. Self-Refine and Reflexion use feedback to revise outputs, while RAG and Self-RAG supply retrieved evidence (Madaan et al., 2023; Shinn et al., 2023; Lewis et al., 2020; Asai et al., 2024). ResMAS combines learned topology optimization with prompt refinement; MASC detects anomalies through next-execution reconstruction and triggers correction (Zhou et al., 2026; Shen et al., 2026). Self-Refine and Reflexion operate through additional inference rather than parameter updates, whereas the full ResMAS and MASC pipelines include learned components. These approaches improve task outcomes through refinement or correction, but do not directly test whether locally measured responses can predict system-level error propagation. REPA connects these research directions through a local-to-system model. Its key distinction is the separate estimation of input-assembly and agent-processing responses across multiple error channels, composed through downstream workflows. Once fitted, the model predicts system responses without fine-tuning the underlying LLMs or observing test-time intermediate states and final outputs. Theoretical analysis links local modeling errors to end-to-end prediction error, while controlled restoration and evidence-placement experiments demonstrate REPA’s ability to guide targeted optimization of agents and MAS. Appendix B gives more comparison details. 

# 3 REPA: Formulation and Analysis 

As illustrated in Fig. 1, REPA predicts system-level responses to a source perturbation by composing locally estimated input-assembly and agent-response maps. Paired measurements distinguish changes in agent inputs from output responses. The resulting diagnostics inform targeted refinement to improve task performance at reasonable inference cost. Theoretical analysis establishes the finite-execution composition and relates local modeling errors to system-level prediction error. Further details on estimation and theoretical derivations are provided in Appendix A.

# 4 Experiments 

Our experiments evaluate how multi-channel local errors affect MAS performance and whether REPA can predict system-level susceptibility by composing locally estimated agent responses. These studies connect measurement validation, controlled error propagation, structural prediction, and MAS recovery. We evaluate recovery through final-answer accuracy and inference cost, while ErrPropBench-10 provides broader task coverage. Further protocols, prompts, analyses, and results are provided in Appendices C and D. 

4.1 Experimental Setup 

Datasets and benchmark construction. The main propagation, prediction, and recovery studies use HotpotQA (Yang et al., 2018), a Wikipedia-based multi-hop question-answering dataset. Its questions require reasoning across multiple documents, with sentence-level supporting-fact annotations identifying the evidence underlying each answer. This structure provides a useful setting for examining how errors in intermediate information affect subsequent reasoning and final answers. With HotpotQA as one of its three multi-hop question-answering settings, ErrPropBench-10 also includes examples from 2WikiMultiHopQA and MuSiQue-Ans (Ho et al., 2020; Trivedi et al., 2022). The remaining seven domains contain questions constructed from source fact sets covering the Roman Empire (Berger et al., 2016), photosynthesis (Johnson, 2016; Cardona et al., 2018), DNA (National Human Genome Research Institute, 2020), black holes (Reddy, 2020), CRISPR (Zhou and Yao, 2023), blockchain (Yaga et al., 2018), and machine learning (Zhang et al., 2023; Atherton et al., 2023). 4.4 Predicting System Responses and Exploring Fault Localization 

To link local agent behavior to system responses, we need to test whether local measurements predict independently measured endpoint responses on held-out instances. REPA composes fitted maps of input assembly and agent processing along the execution graph, using only the measured source discrepancy at evaluation. We compare REPA with three estimators (scalar, diagonal and direct-matrix) that map source discrepancies to endpoint responses. Predictions are evaluated using Spearman correlation between predicted and observed condition gains and mean absolute error (MAE) between predicted and observed endpoint vectors. To assess whether REPA can distinguish a fault source from its downstream effects, we compare local differences, original REPA and gated REPA on new instances with a predefined node disabled. Performance is evaluated using localization success rate and false positive rate (FPR). Appendices C.3 and D.2 provide more details. As shown in Table 1, REPA achieves correlations of 0.84, 0.85 and 0.83 on Q14, Q9 and Q122, respectively, and the lowest MAE among the four estimators. The results show that composing local responses provides predictive information beyond a single source-to-endpoint mapping. In the inverse test, REPA achieves a localization success rate of 0.77, compared with 0.43 for local differences, while lowering the FPR from 0.23 to 0.05 (Fig. 4). This exploratory finding motivates the recovery experiments in Sec. 4.5. 4.5 Diagnosis-Guided Optimization of MAS 

We test whether REPA guides useful optimization in MAS, separately from prediction and fault localization. We disable selected agent functions in six Q14 workflows. Each repair policy can restore the original role prompt at no more than one node, allowing us to compare where it chooses to intervene under the same repair budget. The comparators select a random node, the endpoint or the node with the largest local change. We further evaluate evidence-based optimization through RAG (Lewis et al., 2020), using BM25 retrieval (Robertson and Zaragoza, 2009) followed by maximal marginal relevance (MMR) reranking (Carbonell and Goldstein, 1998). We compare four correction methods on clean inputs, with None providing no evidence, Entry supplying evidence to root agents, Global supplying it to every node, and REPA-RAG supplying it to one REPA-selected node. Appendix C.4 gives more details. With the same one-node restoration allowance, REPA brings accuracy closer to Healthy per-formance than the other repair policies (Fig. 5a). The displayed midpoint rises from 0.17 under disablement to 0.31, compared with 0.32 for Healthy. Its advantage over local-difference selection supports accounting for propagated parent effects when selecting a repair location. These results support REPA-guided optimization at the agent level, improving final-answer accuracy through targeted prompt restoration under a fixed repair budget. As shown in Fig. 5b, REPA-RAG uses fewer tokens than Entry while achieving higher accuracy on all three models without changing the underlying workflow structure. REPA-RAG uses 0.55 to 0.60 of Global’s token cost, with accuracy 0.02 lower on Q9 and 0.04 higher on Q122. Although Q14 shows a larger accuracy loss, selective delivery retains much of Global’s accuracy on Q9 and Q122 at substantially lower token cost. Together with the prompt-restoration findings, these results support REPA-guided optimization at both the agent and system levels, balancing final-answer accuracy and inference cost.

REPA estimates error propagation across all ten domains, with reported Spearman correlations ranging from 0.50 to 0.93 and MAE values from 0.02 to 0.49 (Fig. 7). The mean rank correlation of 0.81 indicates overall agreement with measured propagation rankings, while MAE shows that accurate ordering does not ensure accurate magnitudes. Domains with higher direct-QA EM tend to have lower prediction error, with Pearson r = −0.55 . Taken together, the experiments support REPA as an effective framework for predicting system-level error propagation from local agent responses and guiding targeted optimization of MAS. 

# 5 Conclusion 

We propose REPA, a structural model that composes measured agent-level error responses to predict system-level propagation. Experiments across models, workflows, and settings support its ability to capture relative propagation strength, while controlled recovery studies illustrate its potential to guide targeted MAS optimization. REPA’s strength lies in connecting local behavior with system susceptibility through explicit, interpretable structural composition. It provides the community with a framework for measuring, predicting, and investigating error propagation, establishing a foundation for future research into the reliability and design of multi-agent systems.

B.1 Methodological Attributes 

To clarify how REPA relates to prior work, Table S2 summarizes seven methodological attributes of the studies discussed in Section 2. It traces the steps from controlled perturbation and intermediate measurement to system-level prediction and recovery. 

Attribute definitions. Controlled perturbation specifies changes to inputs, outputs, routing or evaluator instructions. Graded signals measure nonbinary output, message or node states, rather than only averaging binary task correctness. Topology analysis treats structure, order or depth as a modeling or experimental factor. Composed prediction combines measured local relations and checks the resulting system prediction. B.2 Comparison Scope and Advantages of REPA 

Relation to prior work. Existing studies offer complementary perspectives on failure diagnosis, propagation dynamics and recovery. Attribution and robustness studies characterize observed failures and their consequences (Cemri et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025; Jia et al., 2026). Propagation models examine uncertainty, preference transmission or hallucination dynamics (Emde et al., 2026a; Liu, 2026; Jamshidi et al., 2026; Jamshidi, 2026). REPA complements these perspectives by connecting 20 measured local responses to quantitative endpoint predictions across multiple error channels. SFCoT evaluates and calibrates the safety of chain-of-thought reasoning (Pan et al., 2026). 

Experimental comparison. We did not include these external methods as direct experimental baselines because their targets, available observations or intervention procedures differ from ours. Aligning them would require additional adaptation and, for learned pipelines such as ResMAS and MASC, training (Zhou et al., 2026; Shen et al., 2026). These extensions are outside the present experimental scope. Our prediction controls instead share eligible records and source measurements, while restoration controls share the same one-node repair operation and allowance (Appendices C.3 and C.4). These comparisons isolate the contributions of response composition and intervention selection. 

Advantages of REPA. REPA’s central advantage is an explicit connection between local agent behavior, system susceptibility and targeted intervention. It separately estimates assembly and processing responses, then composes them across error channels and the executed workflow. The residual analysis links local modeling errors to endpoint prediction error. Once the response maps are fitted, forward prediction uses the paired source measurement and execution structure without downstream test observations. Inverse diagnostics on paired traces guide prompt restoration, while structural response estimates guide evidence placement. This connects interpretable prediction with intervention selection while keeping task-model parameters and workflow structure fixed.

C.6 Cross-Domain Prediction and Direct QA 

Q14 answers 100 questions per domain using the original question and provided evidence. Decoding uses temperature zero, top-p one, and a maximum output length of 768 tokens. We normalize predictions and reference answers by lowercasing, removing ASCII punctuation and English articles, and standardizing whitespace. Exact match (EM) is the fraction of predictions that exactly match their normalized references, with abstentions receiving a score of zero. Across the ten domains, REPA achieves a macro-average Spearman correlation of 0.81 and an MAE of 0.29. Eight domains achieve correlations of at least 0.82, indicating broadly consistent ordering of propagation strength across the evaluated settings. Absolute prediction accuracy varies more substantially. DNA achieves a correlation of 0.93 and an MAE of 0.02, whereas Photosynthesis achieves a correlation of 0.82 but an MAE of 0.39. These results distinguish the ability to rank propagation strength from the ability to estimate its magnitude accurately. Direct-QA EM averages 0.24 across domains. Higher EM is associated with lower REPA MAE, with a domain-level Pearson correlation of −0.55 . However, Roman Empire and DNA both have EM of 0.25 while their MAEs are 0.32 and 0.02, respectively. MuSiQue has the lowest EM, at 0.10, without the largest MAE. Direct-QA performance therefore provides relatively useful task context but does not fully explain variation in propagation-prediction accuracy.

# E Limitations and Future Directions 

Our evaluation covers three Qwen checkpoints and six workflows, leaving generalization to other model families and dynamically changing execution structures open. Future work could extend the evaluation to heterogeneous agents and adaptive, tool-using workflows under shared tasks and evaluation protocols. Such comparisons would help distinguish model-dependent responses from recurring structural effects and clarify whether REPA retains its predictive accuracy as agent capabilities and interaction patterns change. ErrPropBench-10 covers a bounded set of knowledge and reasoning domains, leaving broader domain variation to be explored. Adding domains and controlled experimental settings could reveal further propagation patterns and examine how they relate to foundation-model capabilities, pretraining characteristics and MAS configurations, providing a broader basis for understanding when local responses yield reliable system-level predictions. 26
