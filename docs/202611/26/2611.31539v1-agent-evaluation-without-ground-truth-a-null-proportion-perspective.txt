# Agent Evaluation Without Ground Truth: A Null Proportion Perspective 

## Brian M. Cho 1, Michael Lindon 2, and Nathan Kallus 1,2 1Cornell Tech 

2Netflix 

Abstract 

The correctness of actions taken by agents is typically evaluated against known, externally verified ground truth labels, such as whether a proof is correct or whether code compiles and passes unit tests. However, as coding and science agents are entrusted with more end-to-end autonomy – proposing interventions, running ex-periments, and deciding what to deploy and investigate next – whether an agent’s actions genuinely improve an outcome must be inferred from noisy, often inconclusive experiments. To evaluate agents in this setting, we define agent quality as a weighted sum of correct decisions along its trajectory, using sequences of e-values as statistical evidence regarding correctness in place of ground-truth labels. Our framework ac-commodates arbitrarily dependent decisions, data-dependent trajectory termination, dynamically generated hypotheses, and quality measures defined after the trajec-tory concludes. We construct upper and lower bound estimators and confidence sets for agent quality with statistical guarantees that hold uniformly over agent trajec-tory distributions and establish instance-wise sharpness of our estimator class. We demonstrate our methods with a case study comparing the success of Claude Opus and Claude Haiku in improving the runtime of an open-source Python package. 

1arXiv:2611.31539v1 [cs.AI] 23 Nov 2026 1 Introduction 

In recent years, agentic AI has proliferated across real-world systems. Coding agents routinely propose changes to production software and infrastructure that are then deployed to its user population (Alshahwan et al. 2024, Novikov et al. 2025). Multi-agent systems are used to generate scientific hypotheses (Gottweis et al. 2026, Xie et al. 2026), which are then tested in real-world experiments. As agents are given authority to make autonomous decisions, a fundamental task is reliable evaluation for the quality of an agent’s decisions. While benchmarks remains the de-facto choice for measuring agent quality, these meth-ods fall short when assessing the decision made by an agent during deployment. Consider an autonomous coding agent that updates the user interface, with the goal of increasing average user engagement. A change that increases user engagement from the baseline is con-sidered correct. In traditional software-based benchmarks, such as SWE-Bench (Jimenez et al. 2024), each decision can be labeled as correct or incorrect with unit tests or reference answers. For our coding agent, however, whether its changes actually improved average user engagement is unknown: it corresponds to a statement about the unknown distribu-tion of the system. While data may be collected to test this claim, the expected increase in user engagement over the entire user population is never directly observed . Our work focuses this setting, where correctness is never observed, and only noisy measurements are available to serve as evidence for and against the correctness of an agent’s decision. To deal with unknown decision quality, popular approaches are the use of estimated correctness labels, from either statistical methods or another agent. Popular heuristics include the LLM-as-judge framework, where a separate model is used a judge for the agent’s decisions (Zheng et al. 2023). Alternative frameworks use a small set of decisions with labeled correctness to construct estimated labels (Wang et al. 2020, Angelopoulos et al. 2023). In our setting, however, neither approach provides reliable evaluation with statistical guarantees. Imputed labels by LLMs are prone to substantial bias and invalid confidence intervals, even with relatively high accuracy for estimated labels (Egami et al. 2023). While statistical frameworks such as prediction-powered inference construct unbiased correctness estimates (Boyeau et al. 2025), these methods require that at least a small set of true labels 2are observed, failing to capture our setting with no observed labels. In this work, we cast the problem of agentic evaluation as a multiple hypothesis testing problem, where each decision is associated with the hypotheses regarding the correctness of that decision. For example, in a software deployment setting, each decision is associated with a pair of hypotheses claiming that the change increased or decreased user engagement, with a correct change being defined as one that increases expected engagement. Agent quality is defined as a weighted sum of correct decisions, such as the proportion of the agent’s changes that improved engagement. Our framework corresponds to evaluating an agent’s decisions as a weighted sum of null hypothesis counts, building upon null proportion estimation (Storey 2002, Meinshausen and Rice 2006, Efron 2008). Distinct from existing works, we develop methods tailored to three key features of agent applications in practice. First, agents act adaptively: each decision is chosen in response of the outcomes of earlier ones and the system shifts in response. Consequently, decisions and the evidence on their quality may be arbitrarily dependent. Second, agent applications are often monitored continuously, and may be terminated at any data-dependent time. For example, when data collected about the agent indicates harmful behavior, it is typically terminated in response. Third, quality may be defined only after the trajectory concludes; the proportion of improving changes, for example, depends on whether the agent chose to adopt a change and how many total changes were proposed. Classical null estimation settings assume a fixed collection of hypotheses with p-values available at a fixed time. In agent applications, however, the number of changes may be random, and classical p-values are often invalid under data-driven stopping. Our primitive for evidence is the e-value (Vovk and Wang 2021, Grünwald et al. 2024, Ramdas et al. 2023a). For each decision we use two sequences of e-values that test the correctness of the agent’s decision during deployment. Unlike p-values, e-values remain valid under optional stopping and can be combined under arbitrary dependence, enabling the flexibility required for agentic evaluation. They are also readily available: anytime-valid tests based on e-values are already used to gate software releases and to monitor experiments in industrial applications (Lindon et al. 2022, Johari et al. 2022). Our contributions are as follows. 31. Framework. We formalize agent quality as a data-dependent weighted sum of un-observed correctness indicators along an adaptively generated trajectory. Our model allows for the number of decisions, trajectory horizons, and definition of agent quality to be random and data-dependent. 2. Estimation. We construct e-value-based upper and lower bound estimators that are uniformly valid over deployment trajectories. Our estimator class contains estimators that are nearly sharp estimators for any instance. In a simplified setting, we show our method dominates Storey’s estimator (Storey 2002). 3. Inference. We construct the confidence set for agent quality that provide uniform coverage over trajectory deployments. We relate our class with e-value merging and discuss the effect of random hypotheses on confidence set construction. 4. Real World Studies. To test our class of estimators and confidence sets, we con-struct estimators and confidence intervals with predictable tuning heuristics based on trajectory simulation. We demonstrate the practical application of our methods with case studies comparing agent performance for code improvement on the open-source 

NetworkX Python package (Hagberg et al. 2008). Section 2 formalizes agentic evaluation and the identification problem. Section 3 and Section 4 develops the estimators and the confidence intervals respectively. Section 5 presents our real-world case study. Further discussion on related works, proofs, tuning procedures, and experiment settings are provided in the supplement.

# 5 Case Study: Comparing Coding Agents 

For our real-world case study, we compare the performance of two large language models for generating code speed-ups on the NetworkX Python library (Hagberg et al. 2008). 

Setup We evaluate Claude Opus 5 and Claude Haiku 4.5 run at high effort and asked to propose up to 50 localized runtime optimizations to the NetworkX package. Each agent was run separately. Changes were proposed sequentially on the code tree left by the agent’s previously accepted changes. Along each agent’s trajectory, change i was correct if the geometric-mean runtime decreases by at least p% from the last change. We set ci = 1 /M .

Random Stopping and e-Values For each agent, its i-th change records the contrasts (Yi, 1, . . . , Y i,N i ) over Ni generated graphs, where Yi,t is the log runtime for change i minus the log runtime for change i−1. The graphs used for evaluations use seeds unavailable to either agent, corresponding to an unknown distribution of user inputs. For a minimum speedup of p%, we set δp = − log(1 − p/ 100) and apply the one-sided right-Haar construction of Appendix 8.1 to the shifted contrasts Xi,t = Yi,t + δp. This produces Ei,t and ¯Ei,t , with increment pairs ei,t = Ei,t /E i,t −1 and ¯ ei,t = ¯Ei,t / ¯Ei,t −1, providing evidence against θi = 1 and θi = 0, respectively. The sample size Ni is determined adaptively. After the first 10 pairs, stopping is assessed every five additional pairs; sampling stops when either Ei,t or ¯Ei,t reaches 100 or when 150 pairs have been observed. 

Predictable Tuning. Across all experiments, we use a predictable tuning heuristic based on trajectory simulation at each time t to select weights ( wi,t , ¯wi,t ). For our estimator, our approach aims to minimize and maximize the conditional expectation of the upper and lower bound estimators. For inference, we similarly minimize a convex upper bound on the expected width. Both methods leverage 64 trajectory simulations at each time t to approximate conditional expectations at each t ∈ N to obtain weights ( wi,t , ¯wi,t ). ## 5.1 Discussion of Results 

In Figure 1, we plot our estimated upper/lower bound estimators and confidence sets as a function of p, the minimum speedup threshold required for a change to be considered correct. Across all thresholds, Opus exhibits a higher estimated proportion of correct changes than Haiku. For example, at a minimum speedup of 5%, the lower estimated bound is approximately 0.15 for Opus, while the estimated upper bound for Haiku is 0.11. The gap persists as the minimum speedup threshold increases, suggesting that Opus produces more meaningful runtime improvements than Haiku. Our confidence sets provide additional information about the reliability of these con-clusions. Despite adaptively determined sample sizes and dependence between successive code changes, our procedures yield informative 90% pointwise confidence sets across the considered speedup thresholds. In particular, the lower confidence sets remain positive even when requiring a 10% speedup, providing statistical evidence that both agents produce a nonzero proportion of meaningful improvements. Our case study demonstrates that that agent quality can be meaningfully evaluated on tasks without ground-truth correctness labels, while preserving rigorous statistical guar-antees. In our experiment, the true geometric-mean runtime improvement of each code change is unknown, and evidence is collected and stopped adaptively. Our framework 15 converts this sequential evidence into interpretable estimates and confidence bounds on a user-defined notion of agent quality, such as the fraction of beneficial decisions. For practi-tioners choosing between agents, metrics such as the proportion of correct changes enable comparisons based on the frequency of practically meaningful improvements, rather than benchmark performance or noisy empirical correctness labels. Additionally, correctness, such as minimum improvement thresholds, can be selected to reflect deployment priorities, allowing practitioners to define correctness based on operational significance. 

# 6 Conclusions 

As AI agents increasingly make consequential decisions in real-world systems, evaluating their performance requires moving beyond static benchmarks or heuristic correctness la-bels. We introduced a null-proportion estimation framework for evaluating agent quality based on sequential e-values, allowing for adaptive decisions, data-dependent stopping, and quality metrics defined after deployment. Our results establish that uniformly valid estima-tion and inference are possible under these conditions, with estimators that can approach instance-wise sharp bounds. Our coding agent case study illustrates how this framework can provide interpretable assessments of agent performance. Our work opens several directions for future research. Our setting is intentionally gen-eral, and we construct uniform estimated bounds and confidence sets. While our approach ensures our desired statistical guarantees across any distribution of agent trajectories, more efficient procedures for estimation and inference may be possible under stronger assump-tions, such i.i.d. e-value pairs, a fixed data collection policy, or restrictions on agent behavior. A practical direction of interest is the development of more efficient tuning pro-cedures, including improved predictable methods for allocating evidence across hypotheses and over time. Lastly, our experiment is intended as a case study, rather than a rigorous evaluation across agents, tasks, and correctness objectives. Because our methods have uniform guarantees, testing them on large scale evaluation across recorded agent trajecto-ries offers the promise of selecting agents in a statistically principled manner based past performance, tailored to the relevant task. 16 Data Availability Statement

Null proportion estimation. Our work builds on the literature of estimating the pro-portion of true null hypotheses in multiple testing. Storey (2002) introduced an estimator based on the proportion of large p-values, while Meinshausen and Rice (2006) developed confidence bounds on the proportion of false null hypotheses. Related empirical Bayes approaches are discussed by Efron (2008). These methods generally concern a prespecified collection of tests with p-values observed at a fixed time. In contrast, our setting allows decisions and their correctness labels to be generated adaptively, evidence collection to terminate at a data-dependent time, and the weights defining the target quantity to de-pend on the observed trajectory. We further characterize sharp bounds on expected agent quality over joint distributions of correctness labels and observed evidence compatible with a given observable distribution. 

Sequential inference with e-values. The use of e-values is motivated by their suitabil-ity for inference under optional stopping and dependence Vovk and Wang (2021), Ramdas et al. (2023a), Grünwald et al. (2024). Conditional e-values can be accumulated over time by predictable betting strategies, while arithmetic averages preserve validity across arbi-trarily dependent e-values. Vovk and Wang (2024a) study martingale-based merging of sequential e-values, and Wang (2025) characterize admissible merging rules under arbi-trary dependence. Our approach combines evidence both within and across hypotheses, with predictable weights for estimation and a shared wealth budget for confidence set construction. Furthermore, the sharpness result for our estimator class follows from the conditional moment restrictions on evidence evaluated at random correctness labels, rather than from an assumed model for their joint distribution. 

Confidence bounds in multiple testing. Our confidence sets are related to simulta-neous inference on the numbers of true and false hypotheses. Goeman and Solari (2011) used closed testing to obtain confidence statements that remain valid after selecting a set of hypotheses, while Katsevich and Ramdas (2020) developed simultaneous false discov-22 ery proportion bounds in structured and online settings. In the e-value literature, Vovk and Wang (2023, 2024b) construct confidence bounds for discovery counts, with the latter exploiting independence or sequential validity across the base e-values. Xu et al. (2024) obtain false coverage rate control for e-value-based confidence intervals reported after data-dependent selection. Our construction likewise inverts e-value tests, but targets a possibly data-dependent weighted sum of correctness indicators, with a potentially random number of hypotheses. Its shared betting process is designed to preserve validity when the true vector of correctness labels is itself random and may depend on the observed trajectory, unlike the approaches discussed above. 

Predictive inference for random labels. Our inference approach is most closely re-lated to conformal e-prediction Vovk (2025), which constructs e-values for candidate labels and guarantees validity at the random true label under exchangeability. Its conditional for-mulation imposes calibration restrictions involving label indicators, resembling our condi-tional moment constraints for random correctness labels. Related work by Vovk and Wang (2026) employs a shared allocation across possible labels to ensure predictive validity in the presence of confounding. This is analogous to our shared betting weights, although their allocation is static whereas ours evolves predictably with sequential evidence. Unlike these predictive inference settings, our goal is to construct confidence sets for a data-dependent weighted sum of unobserved correctness labels associated with adaptively generated deci-sions. Our framework allows arbitrary dependence between labels and observed evidence, data-dependent stopping, and quality measures defined after the trajectory concludes. To accommodate these features, our construction uses a common predictable betting policy across candidate correctness vectors, with wealth transfers across hypotheses that preserve validity without requiring exchangeability. 23 Lower Bound Estimation

Agents and task. The agents are Claude Opus 5 ( claude-opus-5 ) and Claude Haiku 4.5 ( claude-haiku-4-5-20251001 ), both at high effort. The code base is NetworkX at upstream commit 2622da75c . Each agent ran 50 sessions in sequence. A session received the current source tree and the number of changes kept so far, and was asked to make one localized change that reduces runtime. The prompt required the agent to run unit tests after changing the code to ensure that the edit did not functionally change the behavior of the software. 

Trajectory. Each code-change was applied to the tree seen by all later sessions. Each change is therefore proposed with knowledge of its predecessors, and the changes of one agent are dependent. Both agents completed all 50 sessions before any measurement de-scribed below was taken, so the measurements could not influence the changes. 

Measurement. Every change that altered the code was measured against its parent tree. One measurement is a timing pair. A fresh random graph is drawn from a workload family determined by the target the agent declared, using a seed that the agent never saw. The parent tree and the changed tree each run the workload on that graph in a separate process, one immediately after the other, and the order alternates from pair to pair. Each run performs a warm-up and then times a repeat loop. The pair is kept only if the two runs print the same output checksum, and no pair failed this check. The contrast is

Tuning Procedures We generate S = 64 conditional continuations from a scale-equivariant posterior-predictive model with standardized-effect prior variance 4. Each simulated con-45 tinuation reproduced the sampling rule used in the experiment: stopping was checked after 10 pairs and every 5 pairs thereafter, and the trajectory ended when either Ei,t or ¯Ei,t 

reached 100 or when 150 pairs had been observed. The pooled-LP estimator was retuned before each new measurement block: initially before the first 10 pairs, and subsequently before every block of 5 pairs. The selected weights, constrained to lie in [0 , 0.05], were held fixed over the next block and retuned again if sampling continued. For inference, the shared-wealth procedure was updated after each observed pair, using betting fractions 

λi,t = ¯λi,t = 1. Transfers bi,t were selected by minimizing the convex expected-width surrogate over a 33-point grid of the available wealth; thus, our implementation used a dis-cretized approximation to the continuous transfer LP in Algorithm 2. Simulated evidence increments were truncated at 10 6 for numerical stability when selecting tuning parameters, Truncation was not applied to the observed evidence used to construct the confidence set.
