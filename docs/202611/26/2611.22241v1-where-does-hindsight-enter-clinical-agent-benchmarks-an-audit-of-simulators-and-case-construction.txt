Abstract 

Benchmarks for LLM diagnostic agents are built from retrospective records: the case an agent reads is rewritten from discharge sum-maries, and unrecorded test results are gener-ated by a simulator that often sees the final diagnosis. H IND AUDIT separates three hind-sight channels (the diagnosis given to the simu-lator, the retrospective context it conditions on, and the agent-facing case text) and measures each with judges trained only on real data. On 27,489 emergency admissions from MIMIC-IV, five registered tests found no leakage into sim-ulated laboratory results: providing the diag-nosis to five simulators had a negligible effect, and prompted gatekeepers diluted the diagnos-tic signal, although exploratory results suggest they can raise agent scores. The largest effect lay in the case text. Against a presentation-time reconstruction from triage records, benchmark-style summaries raised the zero-shot accuracy of four LLMs from 25.4% to 39.7% and dis-charge histories to 45.3%, and raised six in-teractive agents’ scores while barely changing their ranking. The exploratory gap was repro-duced in a registered replication on held-out patients. Masking diagnosis names left it un-changed. Because MIMIC lacks a contempo-raneous bedside history, the gap is an upper bound on hindsight. We report all registered outcomes and recommend construction prac-tices. 

1 Introduction 

Large language model (LLM) agents are increas-ingly evaluated as sequential diagnosticians: the agent reads a short presentation of a patient, orders examinations, receives their results, and commits to a diagnosis (Schmidgall et al., 2024; Nori et al., 2025; Qiu et al., 2025; Hager et al., 2024). Scores on these benchmarks are used to compare models and to argue that agents are approaching clinical 

> *Corresponding author.

usefulness, so whether a benchmark measures di-agnosis under realistic uncertainty is not a techni-cality: if the answer is easier to see than it would be at the bedside, every score built on it is inflated. No real patient can be re-examined, so these benchmarks are built from retrospective electronic health records, and two construction steps are shared by most of them. The case the agent reads is extracted or rewritten, often by an LLM, from notes written after the diagnosis was known, most com-monly the discharge summary. Moreover, exami-nations the agent requests but that were never per-formed are answered by a simulator (a fine-tuned “world model” (Qiu et al., 2025) or a prompted “gatekeeper” LLM (Nori et al., 2025)), which is usually given the full record, including the final diagnosis. Both steps invite hindsight : information that exists only because the answer is known can reach what the agent sees. Benchmark builders have focused on the second step and on its most visible form, the diagnosis name. Simulators are in-structed not to reveal the answer, and case texts that name the diagnosis are filtered out (Qiu et al., 2025; Nori et al., 2025; Hager et al., 2024). Whether they address the main source of hindsight is unknown. We ask where hindsight actually enters, and how much. To answer this, we propose H IND AUDIT ,an audit that separates three channels (the diagno-sis line in the simulator’s input, the retrospective context the simulator conditions on, and the case text the agent reads) and measures each against real data: judges trained only on real records score how strongly simulated results point to the true diagnosis, and LLMs and text classifiers are com-pared across versions of the same case, including a presentation-time reconstruction built from the emergency-department triage record. The tests of the simulator-side concern were registered before each run, and we report all of them. On 27,489 emergency admissions from MIMIC-IV, the channel that builders guard most carefully 1arXiv:2611.22241v1 [cs.CL] 17 Nov 2026 mattered least: providing the diagnosis to five sim-ulators (seven configurations) had a negligible ef-fect (at most 1.4 pp) on the laboratory-result signal detected by real-data judges, and prompted gate-keepers diluted that signal, although an exploratory analysis suggests that they can still raise the scores of agents that rely on laboratory results. In the case text, four local LLMs diagnosed 25.4% of presentation-time cases correctly, but 39.7% of the corresponding benchmark-style summaries and 45.3% of discharge histories, a gap that replicated on held-out patients under a registered rule and was unchanged when diagnosis names were re-moved, and six interactive agents showed it too. As MIMIC lacks a contemporaneous bedside history, this gap is an upper bound on hindsight. Whatever its source, diagnosis-name filters cannot remove it. These results locate the main threat to validity in case construction rather than simulators, and sug-gest building agent-facing cases from presentation-time information. Our contributions are: • An audit method and a reference construc-tion. HIND AUDIT measures three hindsight channels separately with real-data judges, and a presentation-time reconstruction from triage records provides a reference input. • The case text as the main channel. Bench-mark texts make diagnosis 12–22 percentage points easier for each of five LLMs from three families. The gap survives diagnosis-name masking and replicates on held-out patients. • The simulator side, with registered tests. 

Five registered tests found no leakage into simulated laboratory results: the diagnosis line had a negligible effect, and gatekeepers kept 16–62% of the real signal (world models 70–84%). • Two pitfalls for leakage audits. Placeholder masking creates a cue for text-based judges, and comparing raw probabilities across classi-fiers can hide a large effect. 

2 Related work 

Simulated environments for diagnostic agents. 

AgentClinic (Schmidgall et al., 2024) uses LLM-played patients that know the symptoms but not the diagnosis. MIMIC-CDM (Hager et al., 2024) replays recorded results only and masks diagnosis names in them. SDBench (Nori et al., 2025) im-plements a gatekeeper (o4-mini) “with access to the full . . . case file, including the final diagnosis”, which synthesizes case-consistent findings for tests not in the case and is described as removing hind-sight bias from the case write-ups. DiagGym (Qiu et al., 2025) trains a world model on MIMIC-IV whose input profile, taken from the discharge sum-mary, includes “the final diagnosis”. The agent in-stead reads an initial inquiry produced by an LLM from the restructured record and the discharge note, filtered by asking whether it “directly contain[s] the name” of the diagnosis. EHR world models for long-horizon trajectories (Mu et al., 2026) widen the use of learned simulators. 

Leakage and evaluation validity. PULSE-CARE (Dutta et al., 2026) showed that agent scores change substantially when the patient simulator is swapped, so scores hold only conditional on the simulator. Explicit leakage is commonly ad-dressed by filtering diagnosis names (Hager et al., 2024; Qiu et al., 2025). Dynamic case genera-tion from knowledge graphs avoids retrospective notes (Tang et al., 2025). Closest to our case-text finding, an audit of rare-disease diagnosis showed that LLMs infer diagnoses from retrospective thera-peutic cues in clinical narratives (Yang et al., 2026). We study common emergency diagnoses and quan-tify how much each construction step contributes. “Hindsight bias” has been studied for the evaluated 

model when it is shown future data (Matsuura et al., 2026). We study hindsight in benchmark construc-tion. Models are known to exploit shortcuts in their data (Geirhos et al., 2020). In NLP, annotation ar-tifacts and hypothesis-only baselines showed that benchmarks can be solved from cues unrelated to the intended reasoning (Gururangan et al., 2018; Poliak et al., 2018; McCoy et al., 2019), and data contamination is a recognized threat to the validity of LLM evaluation (Sainz et al., 2023; Golchin and Surdeanu, 2024). The case-text channel we study resembles the former: the cue arises from how the evaluation input is built, not from training data.

Case-text channel. We compare benchmark-style texts (an LLM-written pre-hospital summary and the discharge history) with presentation-time texts (V3, built from the triage record and pre-admission history, and P ) for the same admission. Zero-shot LLMs and text classifiers trained on real data read each text, and the accuracy gap between texts measures what the construction adds (§4). 

Judges trained on real data only. For the simu-lator channels, leakage is measured as how well a fixed judge, trained only on real training data, re-covers D from real or simulated laboratory results. The primary judge ( num ) is a multinomial logistic regression over values, abnormal flags and miss-ingness of the 300 most common laboratory items, with lenient numeric parsing. Cross-simulator com-parisons use a values-only variant, as DiagGym writes no abnormal flags, and a gradient-boosted judge checks that conclusions do not hinge on a linear model. A text-and-value judge ( clf ) was pri-mary in the first two registered tests, and a local LLM judge serves as a reference. Diagnosis key-words are masked in all results, real or simulated. 

Metrics. For each case we compute the probabil-ity the judge assigns to the true category ( ptrue ), av-eraged over five simulator samples, plus top-1/top-5 accuracy. Contrasts are paired by patient, and accuracy differences are given in percentage points (pp). Uncertainty is given as 95% percentile inter-vals from a patient-level bootstrap. Signal retention 

is (¯ psim − c)/(¯ preal − c), where ¯p is the mean ptrue 

of simulated or real results and c = 1 /50 is chance (1: as informative as real results). Typicalization is the fraction of real results that are atypical for the diagnosis (category-item abnormal rate ≥ 0.6 but real result normal, or ≤ 0.2 but real abnormal) that a simulator “corrects” to the typical state. 

Sequential registration. Before each run, the hy-pothesis, primary metric, decision rule and action on failure were written into a timestamped, ver-sioned protocol and not changed afterwards. Reg-istration was sequential and internal: each test was designed after the previous result, so the record documents what was decided and when, not a sin-gle pre-specified plan. We label the registered tests D1, A, A2, A ′1–A ′3 and E1 in the order they were run (Table 3). The tests A ′1–A ′3 form one fam-ily with Holm correction (Holm, 1979), and the replication E1 was registered after the exploratory case-text analyses. Error control is claimed only within these sets, and the one positive registered result (E1) has its own Holm correction. Rules, timing and the protocol history are deposited at OSF (Appendix A). 

4 Data and experimental setup 

Cohort. We use MIMIC-IV v3.1 with MIMIC-IV-Note v2.2 (Johnson et al., 2023c, 2024, 2023b). Principal diagnoses are mapped to AHRQ CCSR categories (Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality, 2026) (ICD-9 via CMS GEMs). We keep emergency or urgent admissions whose discharge 3summary contains a history, a physical examina-tion and a discharge diagnosis section (264,058 cases). We exclude injury (INJ) and mental and behavioral (MBD) categories, as well as transfers and post-procedure admissions, whose diagnosis is often established before arrival. We keep one admission per patient, take the 50 most frequent categories with at most 600 cases each, and split the data 8:1:1 by patient. Following DiagGym, examination events are taken from 24 h before to 24 h after admission, keeping the first occurrence of each. Admission physical examinations are structured by a local Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct (Qwen Team, 2024) model (98.6% parse success), and cases with only a discharge examination or a diag-nosis judged known before admission are removed, leaving 27,489 cases (21,994 / 2,748 / 2,747 for training, validation and test). Simulator analyses use 460 test cases (about 10 per category). All models run locally (Appendix L), and differences from DiagGym’s pipeline are listed in Appendix B. 

Simulators. FT-3B : Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct with LoRA (Hu et al., 2022) trained on the DiagGym text format with diagnosis dropout 0.5, so the same model runs as S+ or S−. GK-14B : Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct, AWQ-quantized (Lin et al., 2024), prompted as an SDBench-style gatekeeper. GK-32B : Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct-AWQ gatekeeper, ad-ditionally told the most common real unit for each test. DiagGym 7B : the public weights, used with its own inference format. Phi-4 (Ab-din et al., 2024): a 14B model from a different family, prompted exactly like GK-32B. GK-72B :Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct-AWQ, prompted like GK-32B. An unquantized (BF16) copy of GK-14B serves as a quantization check. Results are sam-pled five times per item at temperature 1.0, either conditioned on preceding real results ( sequential )or from the profile alone ( independent ). The regis-tered tests used FT-3B, GK-14B and GK-32B. The others are descriptive replications. DiagGym was also run on 225 DiagBench cases its authors held out from training (Appendix I). 

Agent-facing views and LLM diagnosis. For the case-text analyses (§5.2) we compare four views of the same admission: V1 , a DiagGym-style pre-hospital summary written by Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct-AWQ with DiagGym’s verbatim instruc-tion and kept only if it passes DiagGym’s leakage filter. V2 , the discharge-summary history (Diag-Gym’s simulator context). V3 , a presentation-time reconstruction: sex, the MIMIC-IV-ED (Johnson et al., 2023a) triage record of the visit that led to the admission (chief complaint, vital signs, pain score, acuity) and the discharge-summary sections describing the patient before the admission (aller-gies, past medical history, family history), exclud-ing the history of present illness and the proce-dure section. Because these sections come from the discharge summary, 32% of V3 texts name a diagnosis term (53% of histories, 19% of chief complaints), partly as legitimately known chronic conditions. 5.2 Presentation-time reconstruction and LLM diagnosis (exploratory)

The V1 −V3 and V2 −V3 contrasts therefore measure how much easier benchmark text makes diagno-sis than the structured presentation-time record, an upper bound on hindsight. We analyze the 1,053 test cases with an ED triage record (about 60% of admissions) and a summary that passed the leakage filter. Restricting to triage-linked admissions did not change the history-versus-complaint gap (vali-dation split: +19.3 vs. +20.2 pp). These analyses are exploratory.

The gap lies beyond diagnosis names. Delet-ing diagnosis terms from every view lowered all accuracies by 3–5 pp but left the gap unchanged (V1 −V3 +14.8 pp [12.5, 17.1], V2 −V3 +20.3 pp [18.0, 22.7], four-model mean). Deleting classes of words from the summaries and refitting the text classifier changed top-1 by less than 1 pp for di-agnosis terms or for work-up, treatment, hospital-course or history words. Deleting all of them to-gether still left 0.499, 14 pp above the chief com-plaint (0.358, Appendix G). The most weighted words are symptom descriptions, synonyms and abbreviations that a name list misses, such as me-lena for gastrointestinal hemorrhage or flatus for intestinal obstruction. Some of these could have been elicited at the bedside, and the text alone can-not show whether they were reported at arrival or selected by an author who knew the answer. In either case, they form a lexical shortcut that name filters cannot remove and that lets a model score by matching words rather than by clinical reasoning. 

Structured presentation-time information adds little. Triage vital signs and pre-admission his-tory did not improve on the chief complaint (V3 −V4 +0.2 pp [ −2.2, 2.8]). The gap persisted, smaller, under a differently worded prompt (+10.0 pp for V1) and appeared in 82% of the 49 cate-gories with at least ten cases (Appendix H). 

Interactive agents show the same gap, but keep their ranking. Six local agents (7B to 72B pa-rameters, three families) read V1, V2 or V3 of the same 1,053 cases, ordered up to eight examinations with real results (laboratory panels, the first imag-ing report, the admission examination), and then diagnosed. Every agent scored higher from the benchmark text, on average by 7.2 pp [5.6, 8.8] for V1 and 12.5 pp [10.8, 14.2] for V2, but the ranking of the agents barely changed (Kendall τ = 0 .87 

between V1 and V3; Appendix H). Hindsight in the case text thus inflates absolute agent scores more than it reorders agents, and such scores should not be read as bedside accuracy.

5.4 Prompted gatekeepers dilute diagnostic signal, unlike trained world models 

Two kinds of simulator behaved very differently (Table 4, Figure 6 in Appendix I). With the values-only judge, independent S+ results from prompted gatekeepers retained 16–32% of the real signal above chance (43–62% with the boosted judge), against 72% for the trained world model Diag-Gym. In sequential generation, the trained models retained 77–84% and GK-14B 25%. Our FT-3B and the public DiagGym are thus close, and the latter showed no sign of memorizing our cases (Ap-pendix I). The magnitude depends on the judge, but the ordering does not. Retention rose from GK-14B to GK-32B, which also adds unit hints, but not fur-ther from GK-32B to GK-72B, which share them, so dilution did not disappear with scale. Its degree may still reflect the limits of the open models we could run locally and should not be extrapolated to frontier models. Quantization does not explain it: an unquantized (BF16) copy of GK-14B retained 19% [9, 30] of the signal (boosted judge 47%), against 16% and 43% for the quantized version (Appendix I). The gap was not a formatting artifact (Appendix D). Retention measures the information in simulated results, not changes in agent scores. 

Hindsight leaves a small, consistent trace. 

S+−S0 was positive for every simulator (+0.014 to 7+0.059 in ptrue , largest for DiagGym, Table 4), and gatekeepers typicalized more with the retrospective profile. Because S+ and S− were indistinguish-able, the trace comes from the retrospective history, not from the diagnosis line. These contrasts are secondary. 

6 Discussion 

Where the leakage lies. Benchmark builders in-vest in hiding the diagnosis from simulators and filtering diagnosis names from agent inputs. Our results suggest that the first measure addresses a small effect, while the second leaves untouched most of the extra diagnostic signal that retrospec-tive text carries. 

How firm is the case-text evidence? The central result was found exploratorily, after the registered simulator tests had failed, but it passed a registered replication on held-out patients and holds across five models from three families, a non-LLM classi-fier, a narrative rewriting of the presentation-time view, masking by placeholder or deletion, an alter-native prompt, 82% of categories and a sensitiv-ity analysis (Appendix F). It cannot establish how much of the gap is hindsight rather than history a clinician could have taken at the bedside. That requires contemporaneous admission notes, which MIMIC lacks. 

The choice of simulator matters more than what it is told. Whether the simulator sees the diag-nosis made little difference (at most 1.4 pp), but whether it was a prompted gatekeeper or a trained world model changed signal retention substantially (16–32% vs. 72% with the values-only judge). The exploratory agent subset suggests that a gatekeeper holding the full case file, including the diagnosis, can inflate the accuracy of an agent that uses its results, even though these results carry less signal than real ones. Whether simulators change agent scores and rankings is the key open question. 

Two pitfalls for leakage audits. Masking diag-nosis terms with a placeholder gave text classi-fiers a new cue (summary top-1 0.525 vs. 0.491 unmasked), whereas deleting the terms did not. Comparing raw probabilities across differently cal-ibrated classifiers hid a 13.4-pp accuracy gain in A′1. Audits should mask by deletion and compare classifiers by accuracy or proper scoring rules (Ap-pendix J). 

Recommendations. Benchmarks should build agent-facing cases from information documented at presentation, or report a presentation-time base-line next to every score. They should not rely on diagnosis-name filters, should report the signal re-tention of simulated results against real results, and should verify that agents use the simulated results before attributing score differences to simulators. Table 11 (Appendix K) links each recommendation to its evidence. 

7 Conclusion 

Telling simulators the diagnosis made simulated results at most marginally more diagnostic (at most 1.4 pp) for judges trained on real data, and never more diagnostic than real results. Prompted gate-keepers retained only a fraction of the real signal, whereas trained world models retained most of it, and simulators may still affect the scores of agents that rely on examination results. The case text made diagnosis markedly easier than the structured information available at presentation, for zero-shot models and interactive agents alike, in ways that diagnosis-name filters cannot remove. Benchmarks should therefore be built from, or at least compared against, presentation-time information. How much of this gap reflects hindsight, rather than history a clinician could obtain at the bedside, can only be re-solved with contemporaneously written admission notes. 

Limitations 

Data and models. We use a single data source (MIMIC-IV, one US center), and all models were served by local inference. The simulators span two model families (Qwen2.5, including DiagGym, which is fine-tuned from it, and Phi-4), and the study includes no proprietary model such as the o4-mini gatekeeper of SDBench. DiagGym’s training split is unpublished, so our memorization check relies on its authors’ held-out DiagBench cases (225 usable) and compares different case sets. The DiagGym, Phi-4, GK-72B, BF16 and Llama-3.1-8B analyses were added after the registered tests and are descriptive. 

Measurement. The size of the dilution depends on the judge, because a boosted judge recovered about two to three times the signal of a linear one, so we report ranges rather than a single number. Judges stronger than ours might detect simulator 8leakage that ours missed. The simulator audit cov-ers laboratory panels only. Imaging, pathology and endoscopy reports are free text that a gatekeeper aware of the diagnosis could phrase in ways that point to it, so the simulator channels may be larger for such examinations. 

Case-text analyses. The presentation-time anal-yses were found exploratorily on the pilot test split and replicated on its validation split, which be-longs to the same cohort. They cover the 60% of admissions with an ED triage record. The triage chief complaint is terser than the discharge-summary one, social history is de-identified, and medication reconciliation was not included, so V3 may understate legitimately available infor-mation. V3n matches the narrative format of V1 but not its length or the amount of information it carries. Above all, V3 has no history of present illness, because MIMIC-IV has no contempora-neous emergency-physician note. The V1 −V3 and V2 −V3 contrasts, like the history-versus-complaint contrasts, are therefore upper bounds on hindsight rather than estimates of it. The size of the LLM gap was prompt-sensitive, and only one alternative prompt was tested. 

Agents and registration. The A ′2 agents were weak (top-1 about 20–24%, gaining at most 2 pp from laboratory results across all cases) and could order only five panels, which limits the power of A′2. As noted in §5.1, the primary metric of A ′1was poorly chosen. The registration was inter-nal, timestamped and sequential, with each test designed after the previous result, rather than a sin-gle plan deposited in a public registry beforehand. 

Scope. We studied 50 common emergency cat-egories, excluding injury and mental disorders, and all three channels may behave differently for rare diseases. Six interactive agents kept nearly the same order on V3 as on the benchmark text, but whether presentation-time benchmarks sepa-rate agents of similar strength remains untested. 

Ethics Statement 

All data come from MIMIC-IV, MIMIC-IV-Note and MIMIC-IV-ED, used under the Phys-ioNet (Goldberger et al., 2000) credentialed data use agreement. Every language model ran locally on a server accessible only to credentialed project members. No record-level text was sent to an on-line service, and only aggregate statistics left the server. We do not release patient-level text or gen-erated records. Code and aggregate results will be released. The work audits evaluation practice and does not deploy any clinical system.

An invented illustration. The following pair is invented for illustration and is not derived from any patient record. A presentation-time view might read: “Female, 68. Chief complaint: weakness. Triage: HR 112, BP 98/60, pain 0. Past medical history: hypertension, osteoarthritis.” A summary written from the discharge record of the same (fictional) admission might read: “68-year-old woman with two 13 days of black, tarry stools and lightheadedness on standing, taking daily ibuprofen for knee pain.” The summary does not name the bleeding, yet black, tarry stools and the NSAID use point to gastrointestinal hemorrhage. A filter that looks for the diagnosis name would therefore accept it. Both details could have been elicited at the bedside. From the text alone one cannot tell whether they were reported at arrival or selected because the answer was known when the summary was written. This is why the V1 −V3 contrast is an upper bound on hindsight rather than an estimate of it, and why a name filter, which detects neither possibility, cannot address it. The example thus illustrates a lexical shortcut. It does not by itself demonstrate hindsight. 

H Additional results for the case-text channel 

History versus chief complaint by category. History was more predictive in 43 of 50 categories (median gain +17 .8 pp). The largest gains were for secondary malignancies ( +63 pp), other complications of pregnancy ( +62 ), asthma ( +56 ), inflammatory bowel disease ( +50 ), pancreatic ( +45 ) and biliary tract disease ( +42 ). The history was less predictive than the chief complaint for symptom-defined categories such as nausea and vomiting ( −37 ), nonspecific chest pain ( −14 ) and hypotension ( −9), where the complaint is itself the label. 

Robustness and breadth. With a differently worded prompt (no persona, candidates listed first), all accuracies fell and the long discharge history was affected most, but the summary still raised accuracy by 10.0 pp [7.2, 12.8]. The magnitude is prompt-sensitive while the direction is not. The summary beat the presentation-time view in 82% of the 49 categories with at least ten cases (median +11.5 pp, Figure 5). Gains were largest for diagnoses typically confirmed by imaging or a procedure, such as intestinal obstruction (+48 pp), abdominal hernia (+45), cerebral infarction (+34), gastrointestinal hemorrhage (+27) and spondylopathies (+25), as well as for heart failure (+32). J Pitfalls for leakage audits 

Both arose in our own analyses. First, masking diagnosis terms with a placeholder, as is common practice (Hager et al., 2024), gave text classifiers a new cue: masked summaries became more predictable than unmasked ones (top-1 0.525 vs. 0.491), and masked histories likewise (0.574 vs. 0.559), whereas deleting the terms changed little (0.495 and 0.558). A likely mechanism is that the number and position of placeholders encode how often and where the diagnosis was named, which differs between categories. For the LLMs the choice hardly mattered (summary gap 14.4 vs. 14.8 pp for Qwen2.5-7B and 15.3 vs. 15.4 for -14B with placeholder vs. deletion), but judges that treat the placeholder as a token are affected. Second, our registered test of the summaries compared the mean probability of the true class across two different 

classifiers and found no difference ( +0 .002 ), although accuracy rose by 13.4 pp, because the summary classifier was less confident (ECE 0.239 vs. 0.063), and a proper scoring rule (negative log-likelihood) or temperature scaling recovers the effect (Appendix E). Comparisons across classifiers should use accuracy or proper scores, not raw probabilities.

L Compute and data governance 

Every model in this study runs on a local server accessible only to credentialed project members (vLLM (Kwon et al., 2023)). No record text is sent to any external service. This is our data-governance choice for MIMIC text, and it excludes proprietary API models such as the o4-mini gatekeeper of SD-Bench. We therefore report how each result changes with model size and family (3B to 72B parameters from the Qwen2.5, Phi-4 and Llama-3.1 families) rather than with a frontier API model. All experiments ran on one server with four NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPUs (24 GB each). Jobs that completed successfully used about 155 GPU-hours in total (85 hours of wall-clock time, excluding failed and restarted runs). The largest items were the six interactive agents reading V1–V3 (62 GPU-hours, of which 43 for the 72B agent), the 72B gatekeeper (31), the other simulators and gatekeepers (14B, 32B, Phi-4 and DiagGym: 23), zero-shot LLM diagnosis over all models, views and variants including V3n (14), fine-tuning FT-3B (10), DiagGym-style summaries and preprocessing with local LLMs (7), the A ′2agents (6) and the LLM judge (1). Judges, text classifiers and statistics ran on CPU. Data were used under the PhysioNet credentialed data use agreement. No record-level text left the server, and all reported numbers are aggregates.
