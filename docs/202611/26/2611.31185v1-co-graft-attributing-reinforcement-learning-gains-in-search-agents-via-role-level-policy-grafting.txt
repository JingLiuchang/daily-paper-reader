# CO-GRAFT: Attributing Reinforcement Learning Gains in Search Agents via Role-Level Policy Grafting 

Ramraj Chandradevan, Sayontan Ghosh, Vinoth Selvendran 

{first.last}@gmail.com 

Abstract 

Reinforcement learning (RL) improves large language model search agents by up to 32 exact-match (EM) points over their pre-RL ini-tializations, yet overall accuracy gains do not identify which underlying functional capabil-ities improve or interact during training. We introduce CO-GRAFT, a reward-agnostic in-terventional attribution framework in which an RL-trained agent and its pre-RL initializa-tion jointly generate a single trajectory under closed-loop execution with live retrieval. Each policy controls a mutually exclusive subset of four functional roles: intermediate reasoning, search-or-answer decision, search query gener-ation, and final answer extraction. Evaluating all 24 = 16 role assignments without modify-ing weights or retraining defines a cooperative game whose Shapley values decompose the total RL gain across roles, while Möbius in-teraction terms distinguish standalone role im-provements from multi-role coupling. Apply-ing CO-GRAFT to Search-R1 agents across Qwen2.5 and Llama-3.2 models (spanning two sizes, base and instruction-tuned initializations, GRPO and PPO optimizers, and NQ and Hot-potQA datasets), we show that query genera-tion never exceeds 15% of the total gain; in-stead, gains are governed by pre-RL answer va-lidity, search retention, model scale, and fixed task-level search budgets, shifting credit from reasoning toward answer extraction in larger or well-formatted answer response checkpoints while smaller base models rely on reasoning– decision coupling. 

1 Introduction 

Large language model (LLM) search agents answer multi-hop and knowledge-intensive questions by in-terleaving reasoning with external retrieval across multiple turns (Jin et al., 2025b; Song et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025; Zheng et al., 2025b; Sun et al., 2025; Gao et al., 2025; Guo et al., 2025). At each turn, the agent executes four distinct functional roles in sequence (Jin et al., 2025b; Song et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025): it generates intermediate reasoning ( R), makes a binary decision ( D) either to invoke search or to terminate and answer, for-mulates a search query ( Q) when search is chosen, and extracts the final answer ( A) once retrieval con-cludes. Post-training these agents with reinforce-ment learning (RL) improves exact-match (EM) accuracy by 6 to 25 points over non-RL inference and retrieval baselines (Jin et al., 2025b,a), and by up to 32 points over prompted pre-RL search agents in our evaluation. However, overall EM improvements do not re-veal which functional capabilities change during RL post-training or how multiple capabilities inter-act to increase accuracy (Jin et al., 2025a). Whether an agent is trained with outcome rewards (Jin et al., 2025b; Chen et al., 2025; Guo et al., 2025), process rewards (Lightman et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024b; Zheng et al., 2025b), or auxiliary reward shaping such as format (Jin et al., 2025b; Guo et al., 2025) and retrieval-utility rewards (Song et al., 2025; Sun et al., 2025; Gao et al., 2025), an increase in fi-nal EM can result from four distinct role behav-iors (Jin et al., 2025b,a): formulating more effec-tive search queries, reasoning more accurately over retrieved passages, deciding more appropriately when to search versus answer, or formatting final answers to match evaluation criteria. Identifying which of these roles actually drives EM gains is crit-ical, especially for outcome-reward RL (Jin et al., 2025b; Guo et al., 2025), where supervision is ap-plied only to the final answer, yet applies generally to any trained search agent for diagnosing failure modes and targeting future training improvements. Existing evaluation and credit-assignment ap-proaches do not isolate the causal contribution of each functional role under closed-loop execu-tion. Observational behavioral analyses measure surface statistics, such as response length (Jin et al., 2025b; Guo et al., 2025), search frequency (Jin arXiv:2611.31185v1 [cs.IR] 23 Nov 2026 et al., 2025b,a), or query lexical overlap (Jin et al., 2025a), which correlate with training progress with-out verifying whether changes in a given role actu-ally increase end-to-end EM accuracy. Single-role ablations (Jin et al., 2025a) perturb one component in isolation and therefore miss multi-role interac-tions where two or more roles must be controlled jointly by the RL policy to produce a gain. Offline trajectory interventions (Zheng et al., 2025b) evalu-ate static prefixes rather than closed-loop execution (interactive feedback with live retrieval), whereas process-reward (Lightman et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024b) and step-level credit-assignment methods (Gao et al., 2025) alter reward signals during opti-mization rather than decomposing the learned ca-pabilities of an already-trained checkpoint. To overcome these limitations, we introduce 

CO-GRAFT (Co-Policy Grafting ), a reward-agnostic interventional attribution framework in which an RL-trained agent and its pre-RL initial-ization jointly generate a single trajectory with live retrieval. Each policy controls a mutually exclu-sive subset of the four roles within the same tra-jectory (Figure 1b), generating the token spans for its assigned roles and passing the resulting pre-fix to the other policy at role boundaries. Eval-uating all 24 = 16 role combinations defines Shapley (Shapley, 1953) values without modify-ing model weights, requiring access to training re-wards, or retraining. From these 16 measurements, Möbius interaction terms (Harsanyi, 1963) separate standalone single-role improvement from pairwise and higher-order (multi-role) dependencies, and Shapley values aggregate each role’s marginal con-tribution across all role combinations into a single additive attribution score. Applying CO-GRAFT to Search-R1 agents (Jin et al., 2025b,a) across Qwen2.5 (Qwen et al., 2025) and Llama-3.2 (Grattafiori et al., 2024) models (spanning two model sizes, base and instruction-tuned initializations, and GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) and PPO (Schulman et al., 2017) optimizers on NQ (Kwiatkowski et al., 2019) and HotpotQA (Yang et al., 2018) datasets) reveals how RL redistributes capability across roles. First, search query genera-tion ( Q) never accounts for more than 15% of the total RL gain on either dataset, showing that im-proved retrieval queries are not the primary driver of accuracy gains. Second, pre-RL answer validity, search retention, and model scale govern how credit is distributed between reasoning and answer extrac-tion: larger models and instruction-tuned check-points that already produce well-formed answers before RL shift credit from reasoning ( R) toward final answer extraction ( A), whereas smaller base models exhibit strong positive coupling between reasoning and decision ( R×D). Third, decision ( D)gains reflect learning a fixed task-level search bud-get rather than deciding when to stop based on question-level evidence sufficiency. We summarize our contributions as follows: • We propose CO-GRAFT , an interventional attribution framework that segments a search agent trajectory into four functional roles ( R,

D, Q, and A) and evaluates all 24 = 16 

role-assignment combinations between an RL-trained agent and its pre-RL initialization un-der live retrieval without modifying weights or retraining. • We formulate an exact decomposition of RL total EM gains via Shapley values and Möbius interaction terms, isolating standalone role im-provements from pairwise and higher-order interactions. • We evaluate Search-R1 agents using our framework across Qwen2.5 and Llama-3.2 models on NQ and HotpotQA, showing that RL gains are driven by pre-RL answer validity, search retention, model scale, and fixed task-level search budgets while query generation remains bounded at ≤ 15% of total gain. 

2 CO-GRAFT 

We introduce CO-GRAFT, an attribution frame-work that decomposes the performance gain of an RL-trained search agent across the functional roles of its trajectory. To isolate where RL improve-ments originate, we first partition every trajectory into four exhaustive, non-overlapping roles. We then construct grafted rollouts in which the RL policy and its pre-RL initialization jointly gener-ate trajectories under controlled role assignments, and finally quantify both standalone and synergis-tic contributions using Shapley decomposition and Möbius interaction terms.

Crucially, this partition assigns every model-generated token in the trajectory to exactly one role, ensuring that any shift in task accuracy traces directly to the specific roles whose output tokens change. 

Grafted rollouts. A grafted rollout is a trajectory generated jointly by the RL agent and its pre-RL model, each owning a non-overlapping subset of the roles (Figure 1b). Let π+ denote the RL-trained agent (the donor ) and π− denote its exact pre-RL initialization (the recipient ), both sharing the same tokenizer and prompt template. For a grafted pol-icy S ⊆ N , every role segment ρ is generated by π+ if ρ ∈ S and by π− otherwise. During generation, whichever policy writes the active seg-ment conditions on the full preceding prefix, includ-ing tokens produced by the paired model and live passages returned by the retriever for each search query. To prevent artifacts during policy hand-offs, our harness enforces three invariance constraints: (1) because a model generates reasoning and its subsequent action tag in one pass, the harness dis-cards the tag produced by the owner of R and lets the owner of D decode the action tag from the identical prefix; (2) all hand-offs occur strictly at whole-token boundaries so split tokens (e.g., “ ␣< ”in “ <search> ”) do not distort downstream tok-enization; and (3) the harness parses an action only when a complete opening-and-closing tag pair is generated, treating malformed or unclosed tags as invalid turns that pass an error prompt to the next step. All remaining execution mechanics follow the standard search-agent inference loop, recovering pure recipient behavior at S = ∅ and pure donor behavior at S = N .

3 Experimental Setup 

In this section, we detail our search agents, datasets, metrics, training recipe, retrieval setup, trajectory harness, and auxiliary control tests to study the RL performance gain attribution across agent roles using our proposed CO-GRAFT framework. We examine the publicly available Search-R1 (Jin et al., 2025b,a) checkpoints, and supplement them by training additional models using the same Search-R1 training and evaluation setting. 

3.1 Agents Recipients. We choose search agents across two families × two starting points × two sizes. The first family comprises Qwen2.5-3B and Qwen2.5-7B, each as base and instruct (Qwen et al., 2025). The second comprises Llama-3.2-1B and Llama-3.2-3B, each as base and instruct (Grattafiori et al., 2024). Every recipient is run under its donor’s training prompt, so v(∅) is the prompted pre-RL model that prior work reports as the baseline (Jin et al., 2025b). 

Donors. For each corresponding recipient model above, we obtain an RL-trained (GRPO) donor agent. For Qwen2.5-3B, Qwen2.5-7B and Llama-3.2-3B (base and instruct) the donors are available publicly. For Llama-3.2-1B (base and instruct), we train the donors ourselves by following the same recipe (§3.3). 

3.2 Evaluation Datasets and Metrics 

We use the same evaluation datasets from the Search-R1 setting. Because running every question through 16 role assignments is computationally ex-pensive, we evaluate on fixed random subsets rather than full test sets: 1,000 questions each of NQ (Kwiatkowski et al., 2019) and HotpotQA (Yang et al., 2018). Our primary metric is EM on the last answer block, and we also report the valid-answer rate (the fraction of questions with a well-formed answer block). 

3.3 Training Recipe 

We adapt the Search-R1 GRPO recipe to train search agents, including outcome EM reward on the final answer plus a 0.2 format bonus for a well-formed trajectory, retrieved tokens masked from the loss, four search turns of at most 500 gener-ated tokens per turn, group size 5, learning rate 

10 −6, batch size 512, KL coefficient 10 −3, and 1 k

steps of 240 prompts. Moreover, we train on the combined training split of NQ and HotpotQA and evaluate separately on both test sets. 

3.4 Retrieval Environment 

Following the Search-R1 setup (Jin et al., 2025b), we use E5-base-v2 (Wang et al., 2024a) as the dense dual-encoder over the 21M-passage 2018 Wikipedia dump with exact inner-product search. The retriever is queried live during every rollout, returning the top three passages as retrieved evi-dence. 3.5 Harness 

To execute multi-turn rollouts, each trajectory in both training and evaluation is bounded to at most four search turns followed by one final answer turn, with a maximum of 500 generated tokens per turn. Any turn that terminates without a complete action tag receives an invalid-action prompt and passes to the next turn if the turn budget permits. Because trajectory grafting modifies rollout ex-ecution flow, we verify that the CO-GRAFT har-ness preserves single-policy behavior when no role switching occurs, by evaluating S = ∅ (pre-RL agent only) and S = N (RL agent only), under the original Search-R1 codebase and the CO-GRAFT harness across identical evaluation sets. Across 32 comparisons (8 model pairs, two datasets, and two policy behaviors), CO-GRAFT matches Search-R1 accuracy within single-point EM noise in 28 cases, with the remaining four cases differing by at most 2.3 points. Thus, the harness reproduces baseline policy behaviors without introducing im-plementation artifacts. 

3.6 Control Experiments 

We run three control experiments to isolate role attributions from formatting artifacts, fixed search budgets, and query-text conditioning. 

Answer Content vs. Formatting. To separate tag compliance from factual gains under strict EM, we recompute attributions using lenient metrics (substring match, token F1, and valid-answer rate). Under lenient scoring on NQ, A attribution col-lapses for Qwen2.5 models (e.g., from 38% to −5%on Qwen2.5-7B base) but persists for Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct (12 vs. 16 EM points), showing that A

gains reflect format compliance in Qwen2.5 and content improvement in Llama-3.2. However, Ta-ble 1 reports strict EM. 

Adaptive Stopping vs. Fixed Search Depth. To test whether D learns per-question adaptive stop-ping or a uniform search count, we replace D with a fixed budget B ∈ { 0, . . . , 4} while retaining the RL agent’s remaining roles. Across all evaluated pairs, the optimal fixed budget matches the full RL agent within one EM point (e.g., B = 1 on NQ and B = 2 on HotpotQA for Qwen2.5-3B PPO), indicating a learned task-level search depth rather than adaptive stopping. 

Passage Retrieval vs. Query Conditioning. To separate retrieval quality from query-text condi-tioning, we hold the RL agent’s retrieved passages fixed across all 16 combinations on Qwen2.5-3B and Qwen2.5-7B (PPO). Freezing passages drops 

Q attribution by 60–100% (0.6–2.5 EM points), confirming that Q gains come from retrieval rather than text conditioning. However, providing RL-retrieved passages to the pre-RL model adds at most 3 EM points, whereas giving the RL agent control of R yields 11–22 EM points under both live and frozen retrieval. 

4 Results 

In this section, we present our main results in Ta-ble 1 and understand the findings across multiple factors, including question complexity, model scale, and instruction tuning. 

4.1 Effect of Question Complexity: Single-hop vs. Multi-hop 

Question complexity has only a secondary effect on role distribution, mainly shifting credit between Reasoning and Answer while overall patterns re-main driven by search behavior. Comparing role attributions across single-hop NQ and multi-hop HotpotQA for seven models (two models are ex-cluded because their RL gain fell below the report-ing threshold), we find that the Query role remains invariant across task types and stays bounded at or below 15%. In contrast, Reasoning and Answer ex-hibit a clear trade-off: Reasoning attribution is con-sistently higher on multi-hop HotpotQA (across six of seven models with maximum up to 33 percent-age points), whereas Answer attribution is higher on single-hop NQ (across six of seven models with maximum up to 29 percentage points), reflecting how multi-hop queries promote and shift credit from final answer generation toward intermediate reasoning steps. Meanwhile, the Decision share remains below 25% when search behavior is sta-ble (reaching non-zero search calls), but increases substantially when models either increase or de-crease search calls (reaching up to 38–86% when searches drop to zero). Overall, the distributional shift across question complexity is smaller than the divergence induced by changes in search behavior.

4.3 Effect of Instruction Tuning: Base vs. Instruct 

Instruction tuning does not produce a uniform shift in credit allocation; instead, its effect depends on how post-training alters a model’s valid-answer rate and search trajectory. Across four base–instruct pairs, we find that instruction-tuned models with high valid-answer rates realize smaller RL gains and shift attribution toward Answer, whereas mod-els that abandon search stall the RL gains and break the pattern. Specifically, in the Qwen2.5 family (3B and 7B), instruct variants start 18 to 44 percentage points higher in valid-answer rate than their base counterparts, leaving less format learning for RL and yielding 9.9 to 17.5 points lower overall gains while shifting credit from Reasoning ( −6 to −34 

percentage points) to Answer ( +8 to +22 points on NQ). Conversely, Llama-3.2-1B reverses this trend: both variants begin with low valid-answer rates, yet the instruct model gains 6.1 to 11.2 points more because it preserves search (0.9 to 1.0 vs. 0.2 to 0.0 for base), raising Query attribution by 10 to 12 percentage points. Finally, Llama-3.2-3B instruct illustrates the combined effect of both be-haviors—starting at a high valid-answer rate (86% vs. 66% on NQ) while abandoning search entirely (1.4–1.6 to 0.0), resulting in negligible gains ( +1 .3

to +3 .2 points vs. +22 .4 to +31 .6 for base). To-gether, these comparisons demonstrate that pre-RL answer validity and search retention predict role attribution far more reliably than instruction tuning alone.

5.1 Role Interactions: Pairwise and Higher-Order Terms 

While Shapley values quantify how much credit each role receives, Möbius interaction terms (Eq. 2; Table 2) reveal whether those gains arise indepen-dently or through multi-role coordination. A pair-wise interaction is positive when two roles produce synergistic gains beyond the sum of their individ-ual effects, and negative when they overlap as sub-stitutes. Across our four focal checkpoints and two datasets, pairwise terms already reshape how Table 1 shares should be read: in Llama-3.2-3B base, R×D is the largest pairwise term on both NQ (+8 .8) and HotpotQA ( +5 .4) while solo Reason-ing ( +0 .1 and −0.2) and Decision ( −2.5 and −0.5)contribute little or nothing on their own, showing that both roles must act together to improve ex-act match. Conversely, in Qwen2.5-3B instruct on NQ, negative pairwise terms with Answer ( R×A

5.2 RL Optimizer Sensitivity: GRPO vs. PPO 

The choice of RL optimizer alters the leading role only when it pushes search behavior to the turn limit; otherwise, the starting checkpoint governs which roles capture the performance gain. 6 Related Work 

Prior work on RL for search agents primarily eval-uates end-to-end task performance rather than de-composing which behavioral changes produce the gain. Existing RL search agents (Jin et al., 2025b,a; Song et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025; Zheng et al., 2025b; Sun et al., 2025; Gao et al., 2025) optimize outcome-based rewards and report aggregate Exact Match (EM) scores. Methods that restrict opti-mization to query generation (Jiang et al., 2025) or assign separate rewards to search and answer steps (Wang et al., 2025) pre-specify which trajectory roles require improvement rather than measuring their empirical contributions, while ablations on training configurations (Xu et al., 2026) vary al-gorithmic hyperparameters rather than functional roles. Furthermore, intra-policy credit-assignment methods, including step-level rewards (Zheng et al., 2025a; Wang et al., 2026), turn deletion (Zhu et al., 2026), counterfactual branching (Li et al., 2026a), document replay (Mukhopadhyay et al., 2026), and per-role advantage estimation (Wu et al., 2026), compute signals exclusively within a single policy without comparing the trained agent against its pre-RL initialization. CO-GRAFT directly measures the performance difference between the trained pol-icy and its initialization across functional roles. A second line of research combines multiple models during inference or localizes post-training behavioral changes. Venhoff et al. (2026) and Choi et al. (2026) switch between base and post-trained checkpoints at the individual token level during closed-book generation, whereas CO-GRAFT as-signs complete functional roles across multi-turn trajectories with live retriever feedback to obtain an exact Shapley attribution of the RL gain. To lo-calize RL improvements, Li et al. (2026b) attribute gains to parameter subsets in weight space, whereas Lyu (2026) demonstrate that out-of-domain gains from math RL with verifiable rewards stem largely from answer formatting, a pattern consistent with our Qwen2.5 findings. Finally, ShapleyFlow (Yang et al., 2026) applies Shapley values to static mod-ules within a fixed agentic workflow, whereas CO-GRAFT attributes the training gain between two policies across functional trajectory roles during interactive retrieval. 

7 Conclusion 

We introduce CO-GRAFT, a framework that de-composes the EM gain of an RL-trained search agent across its four trajectory roles under closed-loop execution, showing that pre-RL answer va-lidity, search retention, and model scale govern whether gains stem from reasoning–decision cou-pling, fixed task-level search budgets, or final an-swer extraction, while search query generation ( Q)never exceeds 15% of the total gain on NQ and HotpotQA. Because outcome-based EM obscures which functional behavior improves during train-ing, future RL reward designs targeting retrieval skill should explicitly supervise query formulation (Q) and evidence-sufficient stopping decisions ( D). 

Limitations 

CO-GRAFT attributes the performance differ-ence between a pair of policies rather than eval-uating a standalone checkpoint, and its interpreta-tions are subject to the properties of cooperative game-theoretic decomposition and our experimen-tal setup. Attribution shares measure gains rela-tive to a chosen baseline policy (the pre-RL ini-tialization), and Shapley values average marginal contributions across all role permutations; when multi-role interactions are substantial (such as in the Qwen2.5-7B PPO pair), additive Shapley shares alone do not capture joint dependencies and must be interpreted alongside the Möbius interaction terms. In addition, functional boundaries are seg-mented using structural action tags, grouping in-ternal planning, passage synthesis, and structural formatting together inside the intermediate reason-ing role ( R), leaving finer-grained decompositions of R to future work. Our empirical evaluation is also bounded by model scale, inference serving stochasticity, and diagnostic scope. Compute constraints limit our locally trained models to 7B parameters or smaller, requiring us to omit a 14B or 32B model pair, and batched greedy decoding introduces approximately one EM point of serving variance. Furthermore, the fixed-budget and retrieval-swap diagnostics cover only the two Qwen2.5 PPO pairs on NQ and Hot-potQA, and selecting the optimal budget on the evaluation set provides an upper-bound estimate within this noise floor. Finally, training recipes incorporating query-level process rewards, longer optimization schedules, or live web search may redistribute gains differently across roles, which CO-GRAFT can be directly applied to measure.
