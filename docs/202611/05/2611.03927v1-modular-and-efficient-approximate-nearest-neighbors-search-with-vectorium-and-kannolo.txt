A B S T R A C T 

Approximate Nearest Neighbors (ANN) search over neural embeddings powers modern retrieval systems such as Retrieval-Augmented Generation. Embeddings come in three representations (dense, sparse and multi-vector), and efficient search combines an index structure, a similarity function and a quantization scheme. Existing libraries fix one combination, hard-code each one separately, or factor only one side of the problem, and none covers all three representations. We present V ECTORIUM and 

> K

ANN OLO , two open-source Rust libraries that split this design space into a data-management layer and an indexing layer. V ECTORIUM abstracts the vector, the distance function, the quantizer and the dataset independently, and provides 11 encoders, from 32 -bit floats to sub-bit codes. On top of it, 

> K

ANN OLO implements Hierarchical Navigable Small-World (H NSW ) graph and Inverted Vector File (IVF) indexes that are generic over representation, encoder and metric, and a two-stage index that composes any first-stage index with any rescoring dataset. Since every combination is specialized at compile time, this modularity adds no run-time overhead, and the resulting indexes reach state-of-the-art performance: our Product Quantization matches F AISS in accuracy at competitive speed, our RABIT Q and H NSW match or outperform their reference implementations, and our IVF outperforms FAISS . Against six dense and five sparse systems on seven public collections, our indexes give the best memory–throughput trade-off on dense data in 9 of 12 collection–accuracy pairs and perform well on out-of-distribution queries. On sparse data, the systems built on V ECTORIUM lead on all three collections, with S EISMIC and S EISMIC WAVE the fastest and KANN OLO ’s indexes the most compact. 

## 1. Introduction 

Vector search is a core component of many Artificial Intelligence (AI) applications. Retrieval-Augmented Gener-ation (RAG), semantic search, recommendation and mul-timodal retrieval all rely on the same operation: embed a query, then find the items of a collection whose embeddings are closest to it under a similarity function. These systems run on millions or billions of high-dimensional vectors, and at this scale an exhaustive scan is impractical. Approximate Nearest Neighbors (ANN) search therefore trades a small loss in accuracy for orders of magnitude of throughput. Embeddings come in different representations. A dense dual encoder produces a compact real vector per document. A learned sparse encoder produces a high-dimensional vec-tor with only a small number of nonzero coordinates. A multi-vector encoder produces one dense vector per token and scores a document with the MaxSim operator. The three representations offer different trade-offs between memory, effectiveness and efficiency. Which of them is preferable de-pends on the task or on the data modality: for example, sparse representations are often well suited to text, while dense

Serving any of the three efficiently requires two main ingredients: an index data structure for efficient search, and a quantization scheme that reduces the memory occupied by the vectors. Each ingredient involves further choices: the index structure (e.g. a proximity graph or an inverted index), the similarity function (e.g. Euclidean distance or inner product), the quantization scheme, and the optimizations applied on top of them (e.g. compressing the edges of a graph index). Representation, index, metric and compression are largely independent of one another. Together they span a large cross-product of configurations.

Our contribution. We present V ECTORIUM and KAN-NOLO , two Rust libraries that split this design space into two layers. V ECTORIUM (Section 4) is the data-management layer. The compiler specializes each index for the encoding it runs on, so the search loop contains no indirection. A new encoder is therefore available to every index as soon as it is written, and a new index works with every encoding already in the library. The resulting implementations reach state-of-the-art performance: where they implement the same algorithm as a hand-tuned library, they are competitive with it or faster (Sections 4 and 5). In exchange, compilation takes longer and binaries are larger. The distance computations follow the same principle. Each is a few lines of generic code that the compiler vectorizes for the target hardware, with explicit SIMD only for the packed encodings. ## 2. Background and Related Work 

This section introduces the vector representations used in retrieval and the quantization schemes that compress them. It then states the approximate search problem and surveys the index families and adjacency-list compression methods that Sections 4 and 5 build on. It closes with the libraries and systems that cover this design space, and with the relation of V ECTORIUM and KANN OLO to the first version of KANN OLO and to our earlier publications. 

2.1. Vector Representations and Retrieval Models 

To scale, retrieval systems rely on cheap data repre-sentations. These representations are built offline and eval-uated efficiently at query time to gather a set of relevant results, which slower but more accurate methods such as cross-encoders can optionally re-rank. Vectors are the most widespread such representation, since they express a set of features compactly. Before representations were learned, they were engi-neered, from term weighting such as BM25 [15] in text retrieval to local descriptors such as SIFT [16] in computer vision. Neural representations, or embeddings, have super-seded them. They fall into three families: dense, sparse and multi-vector. The last is also called late-interaction, after the MaxSim scoring it relies on. 

Dense embeddings. A dual encoder built on a pretrained transformer [17, 18] maps a text into a single real vector of fixed dimension. The vector typically has afew hundred to a few thousand components, all of them populated, and texts are ranked by inner product or cosine similarity. DPR [19] established the architecture for passage retrieval. D RAGON [20] reaches state-of-the-art zero-shot effectiveness through diverse data augmentation, and SNOW FLAKE [21] is a recent open family of general-purpose text encoders. Most ANN work targets this representation. 

Learned sparse embeddings. A learned sparse encoder maps a text into a vector over the vocabulary in which only a few dozen to a few hundred coordinates are nonzero. The representation is therefore semantically informed and can still be indexed by the inverted-index machinery of lexical retrieval [22, 23, 24]. uniCOIL [25, 26] learns a weight per term over a document expanded by doc2query [27]. SPLADE [28, 29] adds sparsity-inducing regularization and

2.2. Vector Quantization 

Vector search usually relies on in-memory indexes. These indexes store the vectors themselves, so the footprint of the vectors is one of the main bottlenecks of the search. 

Quantization techniques reduce this footprint by mapping the continuous values of a vector onto a smaller discrete set. 

Scalar quantization casts every component into a nar-rower numeric type. For instance, it reduces 32 -bit floating point numbers to the 16 -bit half-precision f16 and bf16 

formats, or to a fixed-point integer under a data-dependent range. The simplest uniform quantizers scale every compo-nent on a single global range. Research has since moved to more sophisticated techniques that keep as much information as possible in the few bits available. Locally-adaptive Vector Quantization (LVQ) [34] takes the range from the minimum and maximum component of each individual vector, so every vector is quantized against its own bounds. LVQ also adds a residual second level to the resulting codes, used for reranking. RABIT Q [35] makes binary quantization, a single bit per component, viable. Each vector is centered by the col-lection’s mean and randomly rotated. This gives a residual 

𝑟 = 𝑃 (𝑥 − 𝜇 ) whose information is spread evenly over the coordinates. The code stores the sign of each component of 

𝑟 , together with two scalars of metadata. From these, the distance is estimated without bias and with a theoretical error bound. The codes can therefore be used for ranking on their own, and not only as a filter. Extended R ABIT Q [36] generalizes the scheme to 𝑏 bits per component: a sign bit and 𝑏 − 1 magnitude bits. In scalar quantization the distance computation is usually an asymmetric distance computation (ADC), meaning that the query is left unquantized. Quantizing the query as well can reduce the computation to bit operations, as in R ABIT Q. Scalar quantization has its limits at low bit budgets. It spends at least one bit on each component, and the points it can represent lie on a regular grid, which the distribution of

Refinements rotate the space before splitting it (OPQ [38]), quantize residuals iteratively [39, 40], drop the independence between subspaces [41], or weight the error by its effect on the inner product [42]. The fast-scan kernels of F AISS shrink a subspace to 16 centroids so that a sub-table fits in a vector register [5]. Quantization compresses the values of a vector. A sparse vector, however, is made of two streams: the weights and the integer identifiers of the components they belong to. The identifiers cannot be quantized in a lossy way, since a perturbed identifier names a different component and not a nearby one. Once the weights are quantized down to a byte, the identifiers account for the larger share of the footprint. They are therefore compressed losslessly, with the integer codes of inverted indexes. D OT PACKING 8 [13] was introduced to cut this identifier overhead. It bit-packs the gaps between consecutive identifiers and computes the inner product directly on the compressed form , so a compressed scan runs close to the speed of an uncompressed one. 

2.3. Approximate Nearest Neighbor Search 

Vector search is usually framed as 𝑘 -Nearest-Neighbor (𝑘 -NN) search. Given a collection  = { 𝑥 1, … , 𝑥 𝑛 } ⊂ ℝ𝑑 

of 𝑛 vectors in dimension 𝑑 , and given a query 𝑞 and an integer 𝑘 , 𝑘 -NN search asks for the 𝑘 elements of  that a similarity function ranks highest [43]. Under squared-Euclidean distance the answer minimizes ‖𝑞 − 𝑥 ‖2. Under 

inner product the answer maximizes ⟨𝑞, 𝑥 ⟩, and the task is also called Maximum Inner Product Search (MIPS). How-ever, 𝑘 -NN commonly serves as an umbrella term for any task that asks for the best-matching vectors under a given similarity function. Answering such a query exactly costs a scan of the collection, and the classical space-partitioning structures of Section 2.3.1 do not avoid that cost in high dimension. Ap-proximate Nearest Neighbor (ANN) search therefore trades a limited loss of quality for a large gain in throughput [44, 45, 46]. An index restricts the scan to a subset of , and indexes are compared by the quality/throughput trade-off they of-fer [47]. Many formulations of ANN exist, distance-bounded and error-bounded among them. The most widely adopted family of ANN techniques, which this paper studies, offers no guarantee at all. Its results are evaluated empirically, by the fraction of the exact top-𝑘 they recover at a given cost (Section 3.2). 

2.3.1. Indexes for Dense Retrieval 

The index families below were developed for dense vectors. The classical ones divide the search space and avoid exhaustive distance computations by pruning irrelevant re-gions: hierarchical partitioning trees [48, 49, 50, 51, 52] and Locality-Sensitive Hashing (LSH) families [44, 53, 54, 45]. Both struggle with the curse of dimensionality. They degrade towards a linear scan, or they need large memory overheads to hold accuracy. 

Inverted Vector File. One partition-based structure nonetheless remains in wide use, at scale and on accelerators [4]. An Inverted Vector File (IVF) index partitions the collection into nlist clusters, the Voronoi cells [55] of a set of k-means centroids, and stores each cluster contiguously. A query is compared against the centroids, and only the nprobe most promising clusters are scanned. This is the geometric analogue of scanning the posting lists of a query’s terms in an inverted index [56]. IVF is attractive for operational as well as algorithmic reasons: construction is one k-means, and a cluster scan is a dense computation over contiguous memory. Two refinements are common. First, the centroid set can itself be indexed, by a graph or by a second level of clustering, once routing to it becomes expensive. Second, the vectors of a cluster can be stored as centroid-relative residuals 

𝑟 = 𝑣 − 𝑐 , which a lossy quantizer reconstructs more accurately. This is the IVFADC scheme introduced with Product Quantization [37]. ScaNN [42] trains the quantizer on the error of the score it estimates, where other quantizers use the reconstruction error. SOAR [57] assigns a vector to several clusters. 

Proximity graphs. Elsewhere the field has shifted towards proximity graph indexes, sometimes using a tree to initial-ize the traversal [58, 59, 60]. Empirical benchmarks place them at the top of the accuracy/throughput trade-off for in-memory search [61, 62, 47]. These indexes represent vectors as nodes and navigate towards a query by best-first traversal. They combine short-range edges for local resolution with long-range edges that let a traversal cross the space. Hier-archical structures such as H NSW [63, 64] and optimized single-layer graphs such as NSG [65, 66] and V AMANA [67] follow this design. Exact 𝑘 -NN graph construction requires a prohibitive quadratic number of distance computations, so these indexes build their graphs approximately, from cheap initializations such as NN-Descent [68]. Modern indexes prune neighbors to reduce redundancy and promote long-range edges [69, 70, 64, 67]. They thus favor diversity of direction over proximity alone, and they bound node degree to balance traversal speed against the memory footprint of the adjacency lists. One of the most widely adopted members of the family is the Hierarchical Navigable Small World (H NSW ) graph [64]. It organizes the collection into a hierarchy of proximity graphs. The lowest level, the ground level , holds every vector. Each level above it holds a decreasing random sample

of the level below and provides long-range links that shorten navigation. A query descends greedily through the upper levels to locate its neighborhood cheaply. Beam search over the ground level then refines the result. Three parameters govern the trade-off. The degree bound 

𝑀 limits how many outgoing edges a node keeps per level (2𝑀 on the ground level). The beam width used to find a new node’s neighbors at insertion is 𝑒𝑓 construction , and the beam width at query time is 𝑒𝑓 search . Neighbor selection uses a robust pruning heuristic that approximates a relative-neighborhood graph. An edge is kept only if no already-selected neighbor is closer to its endpoint than the endpoint is to the node. This diversifies a node’s neighborhood by direction and not by distance alone. HNSW ’s construction is incremental: nodes are inserted one at a time, each running the ordinary search to find its neighbors in the graph. The standard beam search of H NSW halts once the best remaining frontier candidate is worse than the worst element of the current results. Two families of replacement criteria exist. Relaxation-based criteria widen that comparison so the search continues a little past it. Adaptive Beam Search is one of them, with a provable bound on navigable graphs [71]. 

Saturation-based criteria such as Patience-in-Proximity [72] halt once the result set stops changing. A third line of work learns the decision from the state of the search [73]. Today, graph indexes still represent the state of the art in dense retrieval. However, they rely on scattered mem-ory accesses and do little computation, so they are usually memory-bound. To alleviate this problem, a large part of the literature has moved towards two-stage pipelines [34, 74, 8, 35]. A set of candidates is first generated over quantized vectors and then refined against uncompressed or higher-precision representations. The graph is traversed quickly with the cheap representations, and the larger, more expen-sive ones are read only for a small subset of candidates. Another line of research goes further and stores heavily compressed representations of the neighbors directly inside the adjacency lists. This replication gives locality in the graph and trades memory for search speed. The two main examples of this line of research are NGT-QG [75, 76] by Yahoo Japan and S YMPHONY QG [77]. 

2.3.2. Indexes for Learned Sparse Retrieval 

There are two main families of indexes for learned sparse embeddings: one inherited from lexical retrieval, and one borrowed from the dense techniques above. The first is the inverted index . A learned sparse vector is a vector over the vocabulary, so it can be indexed by the machinery that BM25 and its predecessors built for term-weighted documents (Section 2.1). The index holds one inverted list per component, and a query touches only the lists of its own nonzero components. This structure allows pruning at query time without any geometric index over the collection. Block-Max Pruning [78] and Dynamic Superblock Pruning [79] bound the contribution of a block of postings and skip the block when the bound cannot enter the result set. Lightweight Superblock Pruning (L SP ) [80] is a lighter-weight variant of the latter. S EISMIC [10, 11] par-titions each inverted list into geometrically cohesive blocks with summary vectors, and evaluates only the blocks whose summary is promising. S INDI [81] is a recent alternative in the same setting. The other family is taken from the dense world. A prox-imity graph needs only a similarity function that it can eval-uate, so the indexes of Section 2.3.1 apply to learned sparse embeddings without changes. P YANN [82], an approach based on H NSW , won the Sparse Track of the NeurIPS 2023 Big ANN challenge [83]. SEISMIC WAVE combines the two worlds: it augments SEISMIC with a 𝜅 -NN graph over documents [84] to expand the set of results and refine the search. 

2.4. Adjacency List Compression 

In graph indexes the vectors account for only part of the footprint. Adjacency lists carry a significant memory overhead of their own, especially when the vectors are low-dimensional or heavily quantized [85]. Since the order of a list does not matter, it can be sorted and stored as the 

gaps between consecutive identifiers [86], which a variable-length integer code stores in few bytes. Combined with graph reordering [87, 88], S TREAM VB YTE [89] compresses the lists by up to 2.3× with a query time equal to or lower than that of the uncompressed graph [90, 91].

Libraries differ in how much of this cross-product they cover. FAISS [4, 5] is the most widely used and most exten-sively tested of them for dense data. It factors the space through a family of loosely related classes, each with its own construction and search code path: IndexIVFFlat and

DiskANN [3] implements V AMANA [67] with a focus on out-of-core search. P ARLAY ANN [6] provides deterministic parallel builds for several graph algorithms. ## 4. V ECTORIUM 

VECTORIUM is the data management layer beneath every index in the system. It owns how a vector is represented in memory, how two vectors are scored under a given metric, how a collection is compressed, and how that collection is stored and accessed. One set of storage and scoring primi-tives is thus shared across systems and index families, and no index family reimplements it. V ECTORIUM also defines the Index contract that the indexes above it implement, and ships the exact flat index; Section 5.1 describes both together with KANN OLO ’s indexes. 

4.1. Overview and Design Principles 

VECTORIUM is built from four core abstractions, each layered on top of the previous one. A vector view is data in flight. It is a lightweight, zero-copy view over a vector’s storage and describes how a given vector type is laid out in memory (Section 4.2). A Distance is a score together with its ordering. It keeps all generic frontier and heap code inde-pendent of the metric (Section 4.3). A VectorEncoder , paired with its QueryEvaluator , unifies compression and scoring . It maps input vectors to a stored encoding and defines how a query is scored against it, so all distance logic is kept in one place (Section 4.4). A Dataset is storage, parameterized by an encoder. It holds encoded vectors and hands out views of them (Section 4.5). Figure 1 shows how the four are stacked and how the three orthogonal axes of layout, metric and compression cut across them. Three principles govern their design. First, encoders and datasets are type parameters , so every combination of layout, metric and compression is a distinct monomorphized type. Distance kernels inline into the caller, no dynamic dispatch appears on the hot path, and a single generic implementa-tion, such as the H NSW index of Section 5.4, serves every encoding. Second, storage layout (dense, sparse, packed, multi-vector), metric (squared-Euclidean or inner product) and compression scheme vary independently, and any mean-ingful combination is obtained by naming its type, with 

no new code . Third, storage is decoupled from scoring. A  

> Dataset

holds no distance logic, and all scoring is done by the encoder’s evaluator, so one dataset and one index search loop serve every compression scheme.

113 s on our machine, 83 s of it for KANN OLO itself, and produces a 5.3 MiB library. The beam search of Section 5.3 is compiled 164 times, once per combination of graph layout, encoder and metric. These copies make up 3.1% of the generated code. Each copy inlines its distance kernel into the search loop, so scoring makes no indirect call. 4.3. Distance Computation 

The next layer turns two vector representations into a score. A single Distance trait fixes what a score is and how scores are ordered. A flat family of kernels computes the scores, each operating directly on the slices behind a view (Section 4.2). The encoders’ evaluators invoke these kernels (Section 4.4.1). A distance is a thin wrapper around a single f32 . The 

Distance contract only requires it to be totally ordered, and SquaredEuclideanDistance and DotProduct implement it. 

DotProduct reverses its ordering, because a larger inner prod-uct denotes a better match, while for squared-Euclidean distance a smaller value does. “Smaller is better” therefore holds under either metric. Every generic consumer above this layer, including the candidate heaps, is written once and never branches on the metric in use. 

A unified relaxation predicate. Early termination and pruning ask whether a candidate is within a relaxed bound, whose direction depends on the metric. The Distance trait hides the direction behind a single predicate. A minimize metric accepts a candidate when 𝑑 ≤ threshold +|threshold |⋅

𝜆 , a maximize metric when 𝑑 ≥ threshold − |threshold | ⋅ 𝜆 ,and 𝜆 = 0 recovers the exact comparison. The threshold enters in absolute value because an estimated distance can be negative, as the unbiased estimator of R ABIT Q returns for near-duplicate pairs. On such a bound, the multiplicative form threshold ⋅ (1 + 𝜆 ) would tighten the window when it should widen it. 

The kernel family. The scoring work is done by a small set of free functions, one per (metric, layout) pair that occurs. Each comes in a checked form that validates its invariants and an unchecked form that assumes them. Sparse data is scored against a densified query: the evaluator expands the sparse query once into a dense buffer, into which each candidate’s stored components index directly. Sparse-versus-sparse comparisons, needed only during construction, use a sorted merge. The

Portable auto-vectorization. Explicit SIMD in V ECTO -

Hand-written vector code would have to be duplicated for each instruction set and again for every (metric, layout) pair, and each copy would fix the register width and the reduction schedule in the source. Each kernel is a few lines of plain Rust. The compiler, with native CPU optimizations (Section 3.4), lowers it to the vector ISA of the target. Two choices make this possible. First, the reductions use Rust’s algebraic (fast-math) floating-point operators, which let the compiler reassociate and contract the multiply–add chains. A strictly ordered IEEE reduction fixes the sum to one scalar schedule, which can be neither widened into SIMD lanes nor fused. This relaxation applies only to scoring: the ordering and relaxation predicates above compare exactly. Second, the unchecked kernels present loops of known, equal trip count, with no bounds checks that would prevent widening. The packed encodings are scored differently. R ABIT Q (Section 4.4.4) scores by pop-counts and shift-and-mask unpacking, and D OT PACKING 8(Section 4.4.6) decodes bit-packed gaps. These kernels use Rust’s portable SIMD, which fixes the lane width but is still independent of the ISA. Only the packed sparse encodings (Sections 4.4.5 and 4.4.6) call per-ISA intrinsics (AVX2, and NEON on Armv8). Without fast-math the kernels do not vectorize at all. The strict variant lowers to a scalar reduction over a sin-gle accumulator, and the algebraic one to packed fused multiply–adds over several independent accumulators. The algebraic f32 dot-product and squared-Euclidean kernels are 13× faster at 𝑑 =128 and 18 –20× faster at higher di-mensions, and exhaustive top-10 results stay identical on SIFT and D RAGON . The same source vectorizes unchanged when rebuilt for AVX2 alone and on Armv8 with NEON (Appendix C.1). Expert-tuned intrinsics may still outper-form compiled code; Section 4.4.3 measures this difference against F AISS .

Batched evaluation. Every kernel that can exploit batching also comes in a batch-of-six form, exposed on the QueryEvaluator contract (Section 4.4.1). It scores one query against six vectors in a single fused pass. The gain comes from query reuse : each query element is loaded once and used six times. On cache-resident f32 vectors the batched kernel is 1.32 –1.73× faster than six single-candidate calls. Under random access, as among graph neighbors, it is 1.24 –1.59× faster for f32 and f16 (Appendix C.1). The contract provides the batched call as a default fan-out, and an encoder overrides it only where a single-pass kernel is faster. 

4.4. Encoders and Quantization 

A VectorEncoder binds a compression scheme to a layout and a metric. It defines how an input vector is mapped to its stored encoding and how a query is scored against that encoding through the kernels of the previous layer. It is the single unit in which storage, compression and scoring meet. The subsubsections that follow state the contract and then present the concrete encoders along the compression axis, from lossless to increasingly lossy. 

4.4.1. The Encoder and Evaluator Abstraction 

The VectorEncoder is the central contract of V ECTO -

> RIUM

. An encoder supplies everything that a dataset or index needs about an encoding, so the dataset or index never needs to know which encoding it is. This comprises its distance (Section 4.3), its three representations (Section 4.2), the evaluator that computes scores, and the query-side param-eters used to build that evaluator. All of them are resolved at compile time. 

The evaluator. All scoring goes through a QueryEvaluator .It is created once from a query and then scores many can-didates, so the once-per-query work (e.g. building PQ’s dis-tance tables) is amortized over all the distances it computes. Evaluators are constructed in two ways: from a user query at search time, and from a stored dataset vector at build time. In the second case the encoded vector is first decoded back to f32 . One scoring machinery thus serves both search and index construction. 

Query-side parameters. An evaluator may take a param-eter that belongs to the query and not to the stored data. For example, for the 1-bit R ABIT Q encoder (Section 4.4.4) it sets how finely the query is quantized. Because this parameter is not part of the stored encoding, one stored dataset can serve every width concurrently from parallel threads. 

Encoding, decoding and stored-vector pairs. Encoding appends directly into the dataset’s own backing storage, with no intermediate allocation. Decoding reconstructs an f32 

vector for the build-time workflows that need the original space. Graph construction (Section 5) also scores pairs of stored vectors against each other many times. The contract therefore includes a pairwise method, which encoders over-ride to score their stored form directly, without decoding either operand.

4.4.3. Product Quantization 

Product Quantization (Section 2.2) is the first encoder in this section with a learned codebook . ProductQuantizer 

implements it with KSUB = 256 centroids per subspace, so a code is one u8 and a sub-table takes 1 KiB. Narrower codes would have to be bit-unpacked on every lookup. Wider ones would inflate both the codebook and the per-query table with no clear gain. The encoded form is therefore 𝑚 bytes, and the footprint is 8𝑚 ∕𝑑 bits per dimension. The subspace count is a compile-time parameter, so the code length is part of the encoder’s type. The per-vector stride of a PQ dataset is therefore a compile-time constant. The divisibility invariants that the kernels rely on ( 𝑚 mod 4 = 0 and 𝑑 mod 𝑚 = 0 ) are asserted once at construction and assumed in all later code. The 𝑚 codebooks are 𝑚 independent clustering prob-lems, each a k-means over dsub-dimensional projections. Training therefore parallelizes across subspaces with no synchronization, as one task per subspace over the k-means primitive of Section 4.6. The evaluator builds the 𝑚 × 256 lookup table once per query. A small ProductQuantizerDistance trait, implemented once per metric, selects the table builder at compile time, so both metrics share one scoring path. 4.4.5. Blocked Sparse Encoding  

> BlockedSparseEncoder

stores the same u16 components and f16 values as the scalar quantizer, in a layout designed for scoring. Each vector is split into blocks of 16 (component, value) pairs: 32 bytes of components followed by 32 bytes of values, which fill one 64 -byte cache line. A query with at least 33 nonzeros is densified, as with the scalar quantizer. A shorter query is kept as a sorted list and scored with JUMP DOT [13]. The kernel walks the blocks of the candidate in order and skips every block whose last component pre-cedes the current query component. It compares the query component against the 16 components of a block at once. Unlike the kernel family, this kernel uses explicit SIMD (AVX2, and NEON on Armv8). 

4.4.6. Compressed Sparse Encoding: DOT PACKING 8

The scalar quantizer of Section 4.4.2 compresses only the values of a sparse vector. Each nonzero still carries a raw u16 component index, and on learned-sparse embed-dings [28, 29, 99] this index is the largest remaining cost once the values are compressed. DotPacking8Encoder is the only encoder in the library that compresses it. The compo-nent stream is a sorted list of integer identifiers, stored as the gaps between consecutive ones. It is compressed with DOT PACKING 8 [13], the bit-packing code for inner products of Section 2.2. Blocks of eight gaps are packed at the width of their largest gap. A byte shuffle decodes a block into a SIMD register, and a prefix sum over the gaps gives the component identifiers, which gather the query weights. A compressed vector is thus scored without being material-ized. V ECTORIUM integrates the authors’ implementation. It stores the values in 8-bit fixed point, f16 , or per-component 

4-, 8- or 16 -bit scalar codes, so both halves of the vector can be compressed. The gaps between consecutive component identifiers of a vector, and hence its compressed size, depend on how the coordinate axes are numbered. Any permutation 𝜋 of the axes may be applied to the stored components, provided the same 𝜋 is applied to every query. The encoder computes such a 𝜋 at training time, numbering components by decreasing document frequency, and stores it with its inverse. It relabels

components through 𝜋 before packing and undoes the rela-beling on decode, so the permutation is invisible to the rest of the system.

4.5. Datasets 

A Dataset holds a whole collection of encoded vectors. It is the pure storage of Section 4.1, parameterized by a 

> VectorEncoder

. It reports its size, returns a vector by identifier or by storage range, iterates over its vectors, and issues prefetch hints. It knows nothing else about the encoding. Four dataset types cover the four layouts of Section 4.2. Dense data is held in one contiguous buffer. Sparse data uses the compressed-sparse-row form: an offsets array plus parallel component and value arrays. Packed encodings are concatenated into one blob of words with the same offsets table; this is the storage form of D OT PACKING 8 and of the blocked encoder. Multi-vector documents are stored as an offsets table over a flat token buffer. Datasets convert into one another in two ways. A zero-copy conversion keeps the stored bytes and only reinter-prets the encoder. An example is switching a dense or sparse dataset between squared-Euclidean distance and inner product, which share a layout. A re-encoding conversion walks the data once to change its stored form, for example casting f32 to f16 or encoding a plain dataset into Product-Quantization codes. This conversion is also where an en-coder receives the parameters it cannot learn from the data (Section 4.4.4). Every dataset persists and reloads without a rebuild, in a fixed-width little-endian format that is stable across hosts. 

Identifier/range duality. Each stored vector can be fetched by its identifier or by its span in the backing storage. For the sparse layout the difference matters. Fetching by identifier first reads the vector’s boundaries from the offsets table. This is a dependent load, which must complete before the vector itself can be requested. An index that walks a candidate list can resolve each range once, prefetch the vector body, and fetch it by range only when it scores it. The memory latency then overlaps with other work. For dense data the two methods coincide. The prefetch hint touches at most the first 256 bytes of the span. This covers a short or

4.6. Clustering 

The layers above also depend on a primitive that is neither a representation, a metric nor an encoder: k-means. It learns the per-subspace codebooks of the Product Quantizer (Section 4.4.3), the coarse level of the two-level multi-vector quantizer (Section 4.4.7), and the partition from which an IVF index is built (Section 5). It comes in a plain and a spherical variant. The spherical variant 𝓁 2-normalizes the centroids after each update, so an inner-product collection is clustered by angle . 

> KMeans

is generic over the representation. It clusters any dataset that can average a set of its vectors and subtract a centroid from one of them; the dense and sparse datasets currently support both operations. It returns the centroids as a Dataset whose encoder the caller chooses. The metric of that encoder is the clustering metric, which is independent of the metric used later to search the collection. K-means therefore has no distance loop of its own. Assignment is a search over the centroid dataset with V ECTORIUM ’s distance kernels, either an exhaustive scan or a top-1 query against any Index built over the centroids. The training corpus and the centroids may use different storage types. A collection already stored as f16 is therefore clustered directly, with no widening pass, while means accumulate in f32 . The update step groups assignments with a stable counting sort and sums each cluster in parallel, which fixes the summation order. With a fixed seed and exhaustive assignment, the centroids are bit-identical on a given target at any thread count. On sparse data the mean of a cluster is much denser than its members. After every update, each centroid can therefore be pruned to its largest-magnitude components, keeping a given factor times the average number of nonzeros in its cluster.

5.1. System Overview 

VECTORIUM defines the Index contract, and KANN OLO 

is one library that implements it. Any other library built on V ECTORIUM can implement it as well. The contract covers search only: given a query and per-family search parameters, it returns the top 𝑘 results as (distance, id) 

pairs. Construction differs across families, so each family exposes its own. IVF, for example, also chooses the encoding its clusters are stored with (Section 5.5). V ECTORIUM also ships the default implementation, FlatIndex , which makes a dataset searchable by exhaustive scan in Θ( 𝑛 log 𝑘 ) time. Because this index lives in the data layer, V ECTORIUM can work on its own with no dependency on the indexing layer, for example to assign points to centroids during k-means (Section 4.6). 

RerankIndex. RerankIndex is the two-stage composition of Section 2.3.1 written as a single type. It pairs a first-stage index with a companion reranking dataset. The two may use different vector encodings and distance metrics. For example, stage one can be an H NSW index over sparse repre-sentations that retrieves candidates cheaply, while stage two holds multi-vector embeddings and rescores the candidates with that representation, as in [1]. Alternatively, stage one can search a PQ-compressed copy of the same collection that stage two holds at full precision. The only requirement is that the two representations are aligned on the same vector identifiers, and the caller is responsible for it. The pipeline proceeds in two stages. First, the first-stage index retrieves 𝑘 c candidates using its own search param-eters. Then the reranking dataset scores every candidate against the query using the full representation, and the top 

𝑘 final are returned. Two optional optimizations reduce the cost of the second stage [1]: • Candidate Pruning (CP, 𝛼 ). The first-stage scores are read here as similarities, so a higher value is better. Before reranking, candidates whose first-stage score falls below (1 − 𝛼 ) times the score of the 𝑘 final -th candidate are discarded. This exploits the corre-lation between first-stage and second-stage scores to prune clearly suboptimal entries before the expensive reranking step. • Early-Exit Reranking (EE, 𝛽 ). The first-stage re-sults are already sorted by first-stage score. During reranking, if 𝛽 consecutive candidates fail to enter the current top-𝑘 final heap, the reranking loop terminates. The assumption is that the remaining candidates are unlikely to improve the ranking. A third option covers the case where the two stages score complementary parts of one representation, i.e. where the vector is (or is approximated by) the sum of the correspond-ing first- and second-stage vectors. The reranking dataset then holds a residual , and the final score is the sum of the two scores. This decomposition holds only under an additive metric, and the caller must ensure that it is meaningful.

5.2. Graph Index Framework 

> K

ANN OLO abstracts the storage layer of any proximity graph [65, 67, 62, 61] behind a GraphTrait of six primitives: a node’s neighbor list, the node count, the edge count, the degree bound, memory accounting, and a translation from a local graph node ID to the corresponding identifier in the underlying dataset. The identifier translation decouples the graph’s internal node ordering from the external ID space of the application. The graph can therefore be permuted without invalidating any external reference, e.g. to improve cache locality or edge compressibility, or to map the local IDs of H NSW ’s upper levels onto the ground level. When no mapping is configured, the translation is a no-op. A mapping holds one entry per node, bit-packed at ⌈log 2 𝑁 ⌉ bits. The two search kernels, a greedy walk and a beam search, are implemented once on GraphTrait . The greedy walk is the single-best-neighbor descent used for 1-NN search and for traversing the upper levels of H NSW . The beam search is used by every graph index and by the ground level of H NSW .It maintains a max-heap of the current top-𝑘 candidates and a min-heap of the traversal frontier, and processes nodes in best-first order until the termination criterion fires. A graph is built in a growable fixed-degree layout, where each node owns a fixed number of contiguous slots. While the graph is still growing, a neighbor list is located, read and extended with no offsets indirection. This layout needs no locking (Section 5.4.1). Once built, the graph is frozen into one of three search-time backends. The plain backend is a CSR-like layout: neighbor IDs plus an offsets array delimiting each node’s list. The fixed-degree backend pads every list to a common stride and drops the offsets array, which gives direct random access to any node’s list. The third is the compressed layout of Section 5.3.4. Its delta coding needs each list sorted by ID, and the conversion sorts the lists. The plain layout is the default for search, and the rest of this section writes the graph as Graph .

5.3. Graph Optimizations 

The beam-search kernel is implemented on GraphTrait ,so the optimizations below apply to every graph backend and to any graph index added later. We measure all of them on the H NSW index. They are presented in the order in which they act during a query. 

5.3.1. Visited Set Design and Optimization 

A graph index does not partition the search space, so the search has to track which nodes it has already evaluated. 5.3.2. Neighbor Processing 

The innermost loop of the graph beam search reads a candidate’s neighbor list, fetches the bodies of the neighbors not yet visited, and scores them against the query. It executes hundreds to thousands of times per query, so its efficiency governs the throughput of the whole index. Two optimiza-tions act on it, each on a different bottleneck: • Batched distance computation. The expansion buffers up to six unvisited neighbors and scores them in a single pass over six independent accumulators, through the batched kernel of Section 4.3. • Prefetching. Before scoring, the loop issues explicit 

PREFETCHT2 hints, targeting the L3 cache, for the batch it is about to score. The prefetch window is capped 

S. Martinico et al. : Preprint submitted to Elsevier Page 17 of 39 Modular and Efficient Approximate Nearest Neighbors Search with Vectorium and kANNolo 

at the first 256 bytes ( 4 cache lines) of each vector. Any remaining cache lines are left to the hardware prefetcher. Both are built from the Dataset access primitives of Sec-tion 4.5 and neither adds a primitive of its own. We measure them on four collections whose vectors span 

2 to 48 cache lines (Appendix C.5). Enabling both is the fastest or tied-fastest configuration on all four. Prefetching alone gives up to +35% QPS where a whole vector fits in the prefetch window. Batching gives +34% on the largest vectors, where it cuts retired instructions by about a quarter at constant IPC. Hardware counters show that the 4-cache-line cap is the point where two costs balance: the first-access stall that the hints remove, and the issue slots that the hints occupy.

5.3.4. Graph Compression 

The adjacency lists are the other half of the memory cost of a graph index (Section 2.4): with the plain backend, every edge costs 4 bytes and every node an 8-byte offset. The compressed backend implements the scheme of [90], which was originally developed in KANN OLO . After the build, the EGB permutation [88] of the ground level relabels every level and the dataset, so graph-adjacent nodes receive close identifiers. Each sorted list is then stored as small gaps with S TREAM VB YTE [89], and the per-node offsets with Elias–Fano [105, 106]. The permutation can also be applied to an uncompressed graph. It improves the locality of the neighbor accesses, so a compressed index can be as fast as an uncompressed, unpermuted one, or faster. Only the final top-𝑘 results are mapped back to the original identifiers. A STREAM VB YTE block holds at most 256 values, so 𝑀 is capped at 128 .

5.4.1. Index Construction 

Standard implementations parallelize H NSW construc-tion by inserting nodes in parallel, assigning the next node to a free thread. They use locks on neighbor lists to make con-current access and updates safe. The locks are a bottleneck on large collections, and they also make the resulting graph depend on thread timing. KANN OLO follows the lock-free batched scheme of P ARLAY ANN [6], applied to each level of the hierarchy in turn. It departs from P ARLAY ANN in two places: the rule that sets the batch size, discussed at the end of this section, and the way the merge is made race-free. Each batch proceeds in two phases: 1. Phase 1 (parallel search). Every node in the batch searches the current graph for its candidate neighbors. It then prunes them to the degree bound with the pruning heuristic of the original algorithm. The graph is not modified during this phase, so it needs no locks and scales across all cores. 2. Phase 2 (merge and commit). The batch’s edges are written. Forward edges go to slots that belong to the new nodes. Reverse edges, i.e. the links from existing nodes to the new ones, are first merged per target, as described below. 

Reverse-edge insertion. Within a batch, several new nodes often pick the same existing node 𝑣 as a neighbor, so several reverse edges arrive at 𝑣 together. The capacity of 𝑣 ’s neighbor list is fixed, so the edges cannot simply be appended. They have to be pruned against the current content of the list, and against each other. KANN OLO 

therefore prunes per target: all the edges arriving at a node are considered together. The batch’s reverse edges are collected as (target , new node ) pairs and sorted. Each target is then pruned once, with all of its incoming edges in the candidate set. The sort has two effects. First, it makes the merges independent, so they run in parallel over distinct targets. Second, it puts every arriving edge into the single pruning decision that concerns it, so the only edges dropped are those the heuristic rejects. Distances are computed only where the degree bound forces a choice. With this procedure, adding reverse edges cannot cause a data race. Every write is deferred to a commit that runs afterwards over disjoint slots. The reverse targets of a batch are always already-inserted points, while its forward edges go to slots owned by the new points. The Rust borrow checker therefore verifies the absence of data races, and the build-time adjacency needs neither atomics nor interior mutability. 

Reproducible construction. Determinism is a design goal of KANN OLO , because prototyping and testing depend on it. Two things must not depend on thread timing. The first is the set of nodes each insertion sees, which the batch boundary already fixes. The second is the order of a target’s candidate set during the merge. Sorting the reverse edges by target and then by arriving node makes this order canonical. With levels and insertion order drawn from a fixed seed, two builds of the same dataset with the same parameters produce byte-identical indexes on the same target. 

Adaptive batch sizing. The batch size balances two costs. Small batches leave threads idle, because the time a vector takes to find its neighbors varies, much more on sparse collections. Large batches cost accuracy, because nodes in the same batch search a frozen graph and cannot discover each other. The damage depends on the ratio ∕𝑁 between

5.5.1. Validation against FAISS 

We validated KANN OLO ’s IVF against F AISS [5] to check that the generic, single-code-path design has no cor-rectness or performance penalty. In the residual-PQ configuration the gap widens further, from 2.0× to 4.1× as nprobe grows. Here the single-table decomposition above removes F AISS ’s per-cluster combine. On the inner-product D RAGON configura-tions, F AISS ’s own residual formulation is already single-table, and the gap is a flatter 1.3–1.5× . Our decomposition offers no structural advantage on that metric, as the algebra above predicts.

## 6. Experiments 

This section compares KANN OLO end to end against state-of-the-art systems on dense and sparse collections. 

6.1. Experimental Setting 

For the dense domain, the comparison runs inside VIBE [107], a benchmark for vector indexes. V IBE fixes the collections, the ground truth, the parameter grids of every system it ships, and the timing loop. We run V IBE at commit  

> 1b60239

with four patches, each applied to all systems in the same way. The first patch concerns build parallelism. V IBE pins construction to one core, while our builds use 64 threads. Parallel builds make this volume of benchmarks feasible. They also measure construction under realistic conditions: indexes are built in parallel in practice, and making con-struction scale with the number of available threads is part of designing an index. The other three patches concern measurement conventions and are described in Appendix A. For the sparse domain there is no equivalent of V IBE ,so we use our own harness with the same conventions: construction on 64 threads under a per-collection budget, and search on a single core. Two sparse competitors, L SP 

and S INDI , can only be searched through their command-line tools. We therefore time every sparse system in the same way, as one search process that runs over the whole query set (Appendix A.2). Thus, dense and sparse systems cannot be compared with each other.

6.1.3. Measures 

Accuracy@ 𝑘 , throughput and build time are as defined in Section 3.2, with 𝑘 = 10 throughout. A query with no results has zero accuracy. The following conventions differ from those of the design studies. 

Footprint. Footprint is the increase in the unique set size (USS) of the search process across index construction: the private memory the process holds after the build minus the memory it held before. This measure charges an index for what it allocates. For every system here but one, this is the system’s own representation of the collection. The exception is L ORANN, as it does not copy the collection from V IBE , and it re-ranks its candidates against the array of the harness. The difference therefore does not include the memory its queries read. We add the raw f32 collection to its footprint, since any deployment would have to hold it. On sparse data, indexes are built and searched in separate processes, so for sparse data we report the resident memory of the search process. Both measures differ from those of the design studies (Section 3.2), so footprints are compared only within a table or figure. 

Construction time. On dense data, construction time is the time V IBE measures to build an index from the in-memory collection. On sparse data, it excludes the conversion of the collection into the input format of each library. In both cases it includes any processing of the data that an index relies on, such as encoding, pruning or document reordering. 

Throughput per unit of memory. This measure is QPS per GiB of index. A system can gain throughput by using more memory, and Section 6.3 shows that the memory use of the systems here differs by up to an order of magnitude. 

Repetition. Every search configuration is timed over two passes of the query set, and we report the higher QPS of the two. Where a warm-up pass was run, it was discarded. The median spread between the two passes is 0.11 –0.19% 

per collection, except on M SMARCO -E-S PLADE (0.97% ). Our graph builds are deterministic (Section 5.4.1), and re-builds reproduce throughput within 1.0% . We therefore treat smaller differences as parity.

## 7. Conclusions and Future Work 

We presented V ECTORIUM and KANN OLO , two Rust libraries that factorize approximate nearest neighbor search into composable abstractions. Users compose them into indexes for dense, sparse and multi-vector representations, covering many trade-offs between construction time, mem-ory, accuracy and speed. Because the compiler specializes every combination, the factoring adds no run-time overhead and still reaches state-of-the-art performance. Our Product Quantizer matches the accuracy of F AISS at competitive    

> S. Martinico et al. :Preprint submitted to Elsevier Page 29 of 39 Modular and Efficient Approximate Nearest Neighbors Search with Vectorium and kANNolo

speed, our R ABIT Q and H NSW match or outperform their reference implementations, and our IVF outperforms F AISS .End to end (Section 6), our two-stage H NSW is faster than every dense system except S YMPHONY QG on the two large collections, with an index of 0.57 –0.62× the size of the collection. S YMPHONY QG needs about four times our memory. On sparse data, the systems built on V ECTORIUM 

lead. S EISMIC and S EISMIC WAVE are the fastest systems on every collection, our indexes are the most compact on every collection, and our two-stage index is the fastest graph index we measured. Our indexes are not the fastest to build. On dense data they rank in the middle of the field, and on sparse data they take far longer to build than inverted indexes. In KANN OLO , the same components build both the dense and the sparse two-stage indexes, with no index code specific to either representation. This is useful in practice. The first-stage encoder that is sufficient differs from one col-lection to the next, and the rescoring dataset trades accuracy for memory. In both cases, a change requires only a new configuration and no new index. Future work goes in three directions. First, H NSW is so far the only algorithm built on the shared graph layer. We plan to add further graph indexes on it and to sup-port dynamic collections, with insertions and deletions after construction. Second, we will keep both libraries current with new quantization and indexing techniques. We will also evaluate the capabilities that are already implemented but not measured here. Third, and most important, we plan to refine and extend the abstractions themselves, so that they cover designs that currently fall outside them. Specialized indexes such as S YMPHONY QG, S EISMIC and T ACHIOM 

cannot yet be expressed with our framework’s abstractions, even though S EISMIC and T ACHIOM are already built on VECTORIUM . Bringing them inside the framework would let us study and develop these techniques further, and combine their components in ways not explored so far. 

## A. Harness and Measurement Details 

Three of the four V IBE patches of Section 6.1 are mea-surement conventions, applied to every system alike. 

Page placement. Our machine has two NUMA nodes, and the 64 build threads run on both. If an index were allocated on one node only, the threads of the other node would read remote memory at every access. Graph builds consist mostly of such reads and would take roughly 50% longer, while the build of L ORANN would be almost unchanged. This would distort the comparison of build costs across index families. Builds therefore spread their memory over both nodes, and the finished index is then migrated to the node of the core that runs the search. We discard any run whose migration cannot be verified from the process’s page map, and every reported configuration is at least 99% local to its node. With migrated pages, graph indexes lose 1.5% (ours) to 2.5% (hnswlib ) of the throughput they reach with pages allocated locally from the start, so our reported ratio against hnswlib is inflated by about 1% . Only the index of S YMPHONY QG on D PR cannot be migrated (Section 6.3.1). 

Memory measurement and allocator settings. Build and search are recorded in two separate passes, so that the memory we report belongs to the index and not to the benchmark harness. Two settings of the C memory allocator, applied to every system alike, make this measurement reliable. The first caps the number of allocation arenas ( MALLOC_ARENA_MAX ); without it, a multi-threaded build leaves memory reserved but unused. The second ( MALLOC_MMAP_THRESHOLD_ ) serves allocations of up to 

256 MiB from the allocator’s own pools. Without it, the 64 

build threads queue on the lock that guards the process’s memory map, which slowed the construction of G LASS 

on D PR by a factor of 14 . Rebuilt with both settings, the cheapest configuration of every system measured before the change moved by −4 .6 to +1 .6% in build time, so those measurements stand.

C.2. Product-Quantization Table Build 

We time the table build alone. We sweep 𝑚 over the full divisor range at 𝑑 ∈ {128 , 768 , 1 024} for both met-rics. The 256 𝑑 multiply–adds do not depend on 𝑚 , so the sweep changes only the reduction length dsub and the loop nest around it. The natural AoS build has a single IEEE-strict accumulator, which keeps the reduction serial. Besides this build, we compile an AoS variant with the algebraic operators of Section 4.3, which separates the effect of the layout from that of the reassociation. All variants produce the same table up to f32 round-off, ≈5 × 10 −7 (micro-benchmark conventions of Section 3.5). Loops this short are sensitive to code placement. The alignment of branch targets alone changes the time of a kernel by up to 1.65× , through instruction-fetch stalls and not through its arithmetic. Each figure is therefore the minimum over three builds that differ only in code alignment. Table C.2 reports the three deployed shapes, all at dsub = 8, the least favorable end of the sweep (Section 4.4.3 gives their speedups). Across the 36 

shapes, the gain over natural AoS ranges from 4.5× to 12 .5× .Against the fast-math variant it never falls below 2.5× at any dsub ∈ {4 , 8, 16} . Transposing alone, with neither build tiled, gives 3.4–5.1× on the deployed shapes, 3.4–13 .8× 

across the sweep and 2.9–4.1× against the fast-math variant. Tiling with 𝑇 =32 adds a further 1.02 –1.33× , while 𝑇 =8 

is slower than no tiling. Fast-math itself speeds up AoS by 1.26× on 𝓁 2 but only by 1.12× on IP. At dsub = 8

both AoS builds are bound by instruction issue, and the two fast-math kernels run in nearly the same time. The gain therefore measures the extra work of the strict kernel. The strict 𝓁 2 kernel also pays a subtraction per element, which the vectorized form amortizes across lanes. AoS wins only at dsub ≳ 96 , where a long reduction finally amortizes the horizontal sum. This regime means 𝑚 ≤ 8, a code of at most eight bytes for a 768 -dimensional vector, and is of no practical interest. 

C.3. Identifier and Range Addressing 

We isolate the effect on the full C OCONDENSER col-lection stored at f16 . We perform five million accesses in random order, which is the scattered graph-neighbor pattern, and in storage order as a control. Each access is scored by the same QueryEvaluator . The three strategies fetch by identifier, by a range precomputed once, and by range with a prefetch issued 32 vectors ahead of use. We follow the micro-benchmark conventions of Section 3.5. Under random access, an access costs 331 ns by iden-tifier, 226 ns by range and 160 ns by range with prefetch. Range addressing removes the ≈1 .8 last-level-cache misses per access caused by the offsets table ( ≈70 MB), which does not fit in cache. The prefetch removes ≈4 more misses. It executes slightly more instructions in fewer cycles, and IPC rises from 0.18 to 0.43 . The gain therefore comes from hiding latency, and the amount of work does not decrease. In storage order the three strategies tie at 142 ns, so the benefit is specific to scattered access. Section 5.3.2 measures the same prefetch inside the graph beam search.

Four collections span the memory footprints the loop can encounter, from a vector that fits inside the prefetch window to one far larger: S NOW FLAKE compressed with PQ128 ( 2
