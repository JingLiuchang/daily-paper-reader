## Prospective Memory for Proactive LLM Agents 

Yuanchen Bei 1, Zhining Liu 1, Tianxin Wei 1, Weizhi Zhang 2, Zhichen Zeng 1,Philip S. Yu 2, Hendrik Hamann 3,4, Jingrui He 1, Hanghang Tong 11University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign 2University of Illinois Chicago

Abstract 

Most memory systems for LLM agents are ret-rospective , preserving and retrieving past infor-mation to inform current decisions. Proactive agents, however, also require prospective mem-ory : retaining future-directed intentions and realizing them when appropriate opportunities arise, even without explicit requests. Crucially, 

remembering the right intention does not guar-antee acting at the right time : a related obser-vation may call for waiting, while a later cue may make the same intention actionable. In-spired by cognitive theories of human prospec-tive memory, we introduce PRO -M EMORY ,a memory framework that separates maintain-ing future intentions from deciding when and how to realize them. P RO -M EMORY preserves pending objectives linked to their supporting evidence, assesses new observations against their original goals and activation conditions, and coordinates appropriate contributions with the agent’s ongoing actions. It further updates intention states based on actions actually is-sued or evidence of resolution, while an ex-perience memory distills feedback from com-pleted episodes into guidance for future deci-sions. Experiments on two benchmarks span-ning delayed-intention tasks and open-ended proactive assistance demonstrate consistent im-provements in proactive task performance over representative memory approaches across mul-tiple LLM backbones, while limiting unneces-sary interventions.

Proactive LLM agents face a different challenge. They continuously observe evolving contexts and may need to provide assistance before an explicit user request (Lu et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2026a). An intention formed earlier may remain unresolved across multiple interactions, becoming actionable only after a future event or change in circumstances. Acting prematurely can be disruptive, while over-looking an appropriate opportunity can leave the user’s needs unmet. Agents must therefore remem-ber to act , not merely what happened . This calls for prospective memory : retaining future-directed intentions and realizing them when appropriate op-portunities arise (Rummel and Kvavilashvili, 2023; Liu and Gabriel, 2026). For proactive agents, the central question becomes: Which remembered in-tention, if any, warrants action now? 

1arXiv:2611.05731v1 [cs.CL] 3 Nov 2026 Crucially, remembering the right intention does not guarantee acting at the right time . Consider a user asking an agent to share slides after approval, as illustrated in Figure 1. A later discussion about revisions may bring the request to mind, but sharing the slides would still be premature. A subsequent approval notice, however, may make the same in-tention actionable without repeating the original request. Once a one-shot contribution has been is-sued, the corresponding intention should no longer trigger that contribution again. The challenge is therefore not simply retrieving relevant history, but determining whether a future intention remains pending and whether the current situation warrants action (Zhang et al., 2026). Recent approaches move beyond passive retrieval by anticipating fu-ture memory needs (Guo et al., 2026) or selectively providing memory-grounded reminders (Wu et al., 2026). Nevertheless, surfacing the right memory is not equivalent to realizing the right intention . The latter requires preserving what remains intended ,recognizing when to act , and deciding how to inter-vene appropriately .Cognitive research on human prospective mem-ory provides a natural foundation for addressing this challenge. Kliegel et al. (2002) characterize prospective remembering through four processes: intention formation, retention, initiation, and exe-cution. Importantly, recognizing a relevant cue and retrieving an intention are distinct from coordinat-ing its execution with ongoing activity (Marsh et al., 2002). Furthermore, previously completed inten-tions may still be reactivated and induce erroneous repeated responses (Scullin et al., 2012). Together, these findings suggest that successful prospective remembering involves more than retaining future intentions: it also requires recognizing appropriate opportunities and regulating how intentions influ-ence ongoing behavior. For proactive agents, this challenge is further complicated by the fact that the appropriate action may not be known when an intention is first formed, but instead depends on the evidence and capabilities available later. Guided by these cognitive processes, we in-troduce P RO -M EMORY , a prospective memory framework that separates maintaining future in-tentions from realizing them in context . Follow-ing the four phases of prospective remembering, 

Formation identifies future objectives grounded in user needs, while Maintenance preserves un-resolved intentions and their supporting evidence across interactions. Cue-Guided Initiation exam-ines historical intention evidence alongside cur-rent observations to decide whether to act, seek additional information, or wait, and determines an appropriate contribution when action is war-ranted. Selective Enactment then considers these memory-driven contributions together with the agent’s own action proposals, determines which in-terventions to actually issue, and updates the inten-tion lifecycle accordingly. A feedback-driven ex-perience memory provides reusable decision guid-ance from completed episodes to improve subse-quent opportunity assessments. By determining concrete contributions when appropriate opportu-nities arise, P RO -M EMORY supports both delayed-intention tasks and open-ended proactive assistance. Experiments on two complementary benchmarks across representative LLM backbones demonstrate PRO -M EMORY improves proactive task perfor-mance over existing memory approaches while lim-iting unnecessary interventions. Our contributions are summarized as follows: • Problem Formulation. We formulate prospec-tive memory for proactive LLM agents as main-taining pending objectives and deciding when and how to act on them in an evolving context. • New Method. We propose P RO -M EMORY ,a cognitively inspired framework that inte-grates evidence-linked intention maintenance, cue-guided opportunity assessment, and selec-tive enactment coordinated with ongoing agent actions, complemented by feedback-driven expe-rience memory. • Comprehensive Evaluation. We conduct exten-sive experiments on two benchmarks spanning delayed-intention tasks and open-ended proac-tive assistance, demonstrating the effectiveness of P RO -M EMORY across LLM backbones. 

2 Related Work 

2.1 Proactive LLM Agents 

Proactive LLM agents aim to anticipate and sat-isfy user needs before an explicit request, requir-ing them to continuously perceive evolving con-texts and decide both whether and when to inter-vene (Yang et al., 2026a; Lu et al., 2025; Tang et al., 2026). Unlike conventional request-response agents, proactive agents must infer latent user needs from ongoing interactions and act only when suf-ficient evidence indicates that assistance is timely. 2This makes proactive assistance inherently tem-porally dependent: a need may emerge gradually, remain unresolved across multiple interactions, or become actionable only after a later change in user state or context (Kong et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026). As a result, memory becomes an important com-ponent for preserving relevant information across time and supporting proactive decisions over ex-tended user-agent interactions (Xie et al., 2026). 

2.2 Memory for LLM Agents 

Memory has become a central component of LLM agents, enabling them to retain information beyond the immediate context window and reuse past in-teractions and experience with structured memory management (Xu et al., 2026; Zhong et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2026b; Fang et al., 2026). Most existing systems are retrospective: they store and organize past information and later retrieve relevant con-tent for a current decision given a deterministic query (Hu et al., 2025; Salama et al., 2025). Recent approaches move beyond passive retrieval by antic-ipating future memory needs (Guo et al., 2026) or selectively injecting memory-grounded reminders into ongoing execution (Wu et al., 2026). More recently, PIS (Zhao and Wu, 2026) organizes de-ferred intentions as typed trigger-action records and applies structural rules to filter eligible intentions. In contrast, P RO -M EMORY maintains evidence-linked future objectives and determines context-appropriate contributions at realization time. It fur-ther coordinates these contributions with ongoing agent actions, supporting both delayed-intention tasks and open-ended proactive assistance. 

3 PRO -M EMORY 

PRO -M EMORY is motivated by a distinction central to prospective remembering: retaining an intention, recognizing an opportunity, and carrying out an action are different responsibilities . Drawing on cognitive accounts of intention formation, reten-tion, initiation, and execution (Kliegel et al., 2002; Marsh et al., 2002), we translate this distinction into three computational design principles. First, we preserve future objectives independently of the actions that may eventually realize them. Second, we distinguish historical evidence of an intention 

from current evidence of an opportunity . Third, we separate recognizing when to act from decid-ing what to actually issue alongside the agent’s ongoing behavior. These principles yield four co-ordinated phases: Formation , Maintenance , Cue-Guided Initiation , and Selective Enactment (Fig-ure 2). An optional experience memory further guides future opportunity assessments. 

3.1 Problem Formulation 

We consider a proactive agent that observes an evolving environment and may intervene without an explicit user request. At step t, it receives an observation ot with source identifier src( ot). An action interface At specifies either available task actions or an open-ended proposal space; where permitted, query channels Qt allow the agent to obtain missing information. The agent must decide whether a previously established intention warrants a contribution now, whether further observation is needed, or whether to wait, while retaining its abil-ity to respond to the current situation independently of memory. We represent the prospective-memory state after step t as 

Mt = ( It, St), (1) where It is the set of pending intentions and St is a source-addressable store of supporting observa-tions. Unlike a record of past interactions alone, 

Mt explicitly preserves obligations and opportuni-ties that may still matter in the future, even when no action is currently due.

3.3 Cue-Guided Intention Initiation 

Remembering a goal is not evidence that it should be acted upon now. Initiation must resolve two distinct questions: what objective does the history establish , and what does the present observation establish about the opportunity to advance it ? A related observation may recall the correct goal with-out satisfying its conditions, whereas a later cue may make that same goal actionable. We therefore assess historical intention evidence separately from the current observation. At each step, the update nominates a bounded set of pending intentions Jt ⊆ ¯It for inspection, including those potentially affected by an arriving cue, revision, or evidence of resolution. Source references and action/query legality are checked structurally, while semantic sufficiency remains an LLM judgment. The controller selects a legal action from an enumerated interface or proposes a context-appropriate task in an open-ended interface. This late binding allows one main-tained objective to lead to different contributions as evidence and available capabilities evolve.

3.5 Feedback-Driven Experience Memory 

As an optional enhancement to the four-stage framework, P RO -M EMORY maintains an indepen-dent experience memory Ek to refine subsequent op-portunity assessments. After episode k is complete and its outputs are fixed, evaluator feedback Fk and the corresponding decision traces provide evidence of successful decisions and errors, including pre-mature or missed interventions. An LLM proposer selects reusable checks covering trigger evidence, objective fidelity, occurrence identity, and action issuance versus external completion. 4.4 Ablation Study 

To examine the key mechanisms of P RO -M EMORY ,we consider three variants: w/o Maintenance dis-cards pending intentions at new events, so that no intention outlives the event in which it was formed; 

w/o Links replaces intention-linked evidence read-ing with a budget-matched read of the most recent evidence; and w/o Retirement disables the retire-ment of one-shot intentions after action issuance. Figure 4 shows the results, from which we draw two observations. Intention maintenance and evidence grounding support timely realization. 

Removing maintenance reduces Set-F1 by 2.17 and 1.91 points on Sonnet 4.5 and Haiku 4.5, re-spectively. More notably, removing evidence links on Haiku 4.5 reduces Set-F1 by 8.45 points and more than doubles the False-Alarm Rate, highlight-ing the importance of grounding action decisions in intention-specific evidence. Lifecycle commit-ment helps prevent repeated interventions. Dis-abling one-shot retirement raises the False-Alarm Rate on both Sonnet 4.5 and Haiku 4.5, suggesting that intentions must be retired after action issuance to avoid redundant assistance. Together, these find-ings underscore the importance of maintaining not only what remains intended, but also when an in-tention should be realized and when it should cease to trigger further actions.

5 Conclusion 

In this paper, we introduced P RO -M EMORY , a cog-nitively inspired framework for retaining and re-alizing future intentions beyond retrospective re-call. By maintaining evidence-linked objectives and separating cue-guided initiation from selective 8enactment, P RO -M EMORY identifies when to act and coordinates interventions with ongoing agent behavior. An optional experience memory further guides decisions using feedback from completed episodes. Experiments on delayed-intention tasks and open-ended proactive assistance demonstrate improved performance over existing memory ap-proaches while limiting unnecessary interventions. 

Limitations 

Our current study primarily focuses on text-based proactive interactions, while real-world agents may need to recognize future opportunities from mul-timodal signals, such as images, audio, and video. Extending prospective memory to multimodal in-tention formation and cross-modal cue recognition is a promising direction. Furthermore, exploring more complex long-horizon scenarios involving in-terdependent intentions and evolving user needs remains an important avenue for future work. 

Ethics Statement 

This work studies proactive assistance using pre-viously released benchmarks for evaluation, with-out deploying autonomous actions to real users. Proactive agents may nevertheless misinterpret user intentions, interrupt ongoing activities, or expose sensitive contextual information through unwanted interventions. Our approach aims to ground inter-ventions in available evidence, but benchmark im-provements do not establish safety in real-world de-ployments. Practical systems should provide user control over proactive behavior and incorporate ap-propriate privacy safeguards and human oversight.

10 A Experimental Setup Details 

A.1 Dataset Details 

We evaluate P RO -M EMORY on two complemen-tary benchmarks that assess prospective memory under different but representative forms of proac-tive assistance. 

PM-Bench. PM-Bench 1 (Liu and Gabriel, 2026) evaluates the ability of LLM agents to remember and realize future intentions over extended inter-actions. Agents receive a sequence of evolving observations containing time- and event-dependent tasks, where previously expressed intentions may become actionable only after subsequent changes in context. Successful agents must retain pend-ing intentions, recognize appropriate opportunities, and avoid premature or unnecessary actions. The evaluated environment spans seven simulated days and 80 decision steps, with actions selected from an available task set. 

ProactiveBench. ProactiveBench 2 (Lu et al., 2025) evaluates proactive assistance in realistic user activity streams, where agents must anticipate user needs without explicit requests. Unlike PM-Bench, which focuses on realizing delayed inten-tions through predefined actions, ProactiveBench requires agents to determine whether and how to provide open-ended assistance as user activities evolve. The evaluation includes 12 activity streams comprising 233 events, covering six coding and six writing scenarios. Agent interventions are eval-uated using the benchmark’s official Active-RM reward model.

A.3 Baseline Details 

We compare P RO -M EMORY against representative memory approaches, including conventional agent memory systems and recent methods that anticipate future memory needs. • Reflection (Shinn et al., 2023) enables agents to learn from previous interactions by generating natural-language reflections on their decisions and outcomes. These reflections are retained to inform the agent’s subsequent reasoning and action selection. • RAG stores historical observations in an exter-nal memory and retrieves semantically relevant information to support the agent’s current deci-sions. It provides a representative retrieval-based memory baseline. • A-Mem (Xu et al., 2026) maintains dynami-cally evolving agent memories through structured memory notes and semantic associations. It sup-ports memory organization and updating as new interactions become available. • MemoryOS (Kang et al., 2025). MemoryOS organizes agent memory through a hierarchical memory-management architecture. It manages information across different memory levels to support long-term retention and context-aware retrieval. • T-Mem (Guo et al., 2026) moves beyond retro-spective retrieval by constructing future-oriented associative triggers for stored information. These triggers anticipate contexts in which memories may become useful, facilitating proactive mem-ory retrieval. 11 • RWM (Wu et al., 2026) introduces a dedicated memory agent that monitors ongoing execution and selectively provides memory-grounded re-minders to an action agent. It enables proactive memory intervention by determining when histor-ical information should be surfaced during agent execution. 

A.4 Implementation Details Backbones and Inference. We use five back-bone LLMs, namely Claude Sonnet 4.5, Claude Haiku 4.5, GPT-4o, Kimi-K2.5, and GLM-4.7, for the main comparison. For every method, the same backbone serves as the acting agent and performs all LLM-based memory operations. Decoding uses temperature 0 unless the official implementation of a baseline prescribes otherwise. Extended reason-ing modes are disabled. No method has access to future observations: every memory operation and decision at step t depends only on the observations up to t.

Evaluation Protocol. On PM-Bench, we use the official simulator, scenario, and scorer. The agent receives the official task instruction (Prompt 1) and, at each step, the current event with the menu of available task actions. It replies through the bench-mark’s structured action interface, which also lets it query state channels (e.g., the clock) carrying cues that are not shown in the event text. On Proac-tiveBench, we use the official test split and the re-leased reward model, LLaMA-3.1-8B Active-RM, as the judge with its official prompt (Prompt 3). The judge sees the observations up to the current event together with the agent’s decision and is run deterministically. 

Memory and Feedback Configuration. The in-tention memory uses recent and retained evidence budgets H = 16 and K = 16 , a capacity of 32 KiB, and at most eight assessed intentions per step; these budgets do not restrict the native actor’s con-text window. When enabled, experience memory retrieves up to two checks from a fixed catalog of four using MiniLM-L6-v2. Evaluator feedback be-comes available only after a completed episode, corresponding to one simulated PM-Bench day or one ProactiveBench activity stream, and can guide subsequent episodes without updating LLM param-eters. We separately evaluate the core framework with this enhancement both disabled and enabled in Appendix B.2.2. 

Reported Experience-Memory Settings. In Ta-bles 1 and 2, experience memory is enabled for Claude Sonnet 4.5 and Claude Haiku 4.5 on both benchmarks, and for GPT-4o, Kimi-K2.5, and GLM-4.7 on ProactiveBench only. 

B Additional Analysis 

B.1 Connection to Cognitive Prospective Memory Cognitive Process Abstraction. Cognitive theo-ries characterize prospective remembering through four processes: intention formation, retention, initiation, and execution (Kliegel et al., 2002). PRO -M EMORY adopts this functional decompo-sition for proactive LLM agents. Formation con-structs future-directed objectives from observed user needs, while Maintenance preserves these ob-jectives and their supporting evidence across inter-actions. Together, they establish a persistent repre-sentation of what remains intended , independently of whether any action is currently warranted. 

Separating Intention Retention from Realiza-tion. A central insight from cognitive research is that retrieving an intention does not necessarily en-tail executing it (Marsh et al., 2002). We formalize this distinction through three predicates: 

Pending t(i) ≜ [i ∈ ¯It],

Ready t(i) ≜ [ξi,t = ACT] ,

Issued t(i) ≜ [∃a ∈ yt : bind( a) = i],

(13) where bind( a) denotes the intention associated with an issued contribution. Importantly, 

Pending t(i)̸ ⇒ Ready t(i), (14) 

Ready t(i)̸ ⇒ Issued t(i). (15) The first distinction captures why retaining an inten-tion does not imply that its activation condition is satisfied. The second captures why recognizing an opportunity does not guarantee that the correspond-ing contribution should be issued, since execution must be coordinated with the agent’s ongoing ac-tions. 

Evidence-grounded Initiation and Execution. 

In P RO -M EMORY , readiness is assessed by com-bining intention-linked historical evidence with the current observation. Historical evidence preserves the original objective and its conditions, whereas 12 the current observation provides potential activa-tion cues. Cue-Guided Initiation therefore deter-mines whether to act, observe, or wait, while Se-lective Enactment determines which proposed con-tributions enter the actual output. Following exe-cution, the intention state is updated according to issued contributions or evidence of resolution. This is consistent with cognitive findings that previously completed intentions may still induce erroneous repeated responses (Scullin et al., 2012). 

Scope of the Analogy. Our framework pro-vides a functional computational approximation of prospective remembering rather than a mech-anistic model of human cognition. In particular, evidence references, LLM-based opportunity as-sessment, and action-grounded lifecycle updates are algorithmic realizations of the corresponding cognitive functions.

E LLM Operation Interfaces and Validation 

PRO -M EMORY implements prospective remember-ing through structured LLM operations over a per-sistent intention state. Each operation has a defined input, output schema, and validation procedure. These interfaces instantiate the computational pro-cesses described in Section 3, rather than relying on unrestricted natural-language memory generation. 

Intention Update. The update operation receives newly arriving observations, existing pending in-tentions, and bounded historical evidence. It pro-poses new or updated intention records, supporting source references, and intentions requiring inspec-tion. Each new intention must be grounded in an arriving source. In the evaluated configuration, ex-isting objectives, recorded conditions, and occur-rence types remain stable; subsequent revisions are preserved through additional evidence references. 

Cue-Guided Assessment. For each selected in-tention, the assessment operation receives its origi-nal objective, linked historical evidence, the current observation, and the available action and query in-terfaces. It returns one of five judgments: A CT ,WAIT , O BSERVE , S ATISFIED , or I NVALIDATED .An A CT judgment must identify a legal, contex-tually warranted contribution, whereas O BSERVE 

identifies a permitted query for resolving a specific missing fact. The output includes source references and evidence supporting the decision. 14 Selective Enactment. The selection proce-dure combines native-actor proposals with valid memory-driven contributions subject to the action interface’s capacity. A subsequent review returns only K EEP or W AIT decisions for selected candi-dates. It cannot introduce new actions or reject unrelated native proposals solely for lacking an as-sociated intention. Lifecycle commitment is based on the actual emitted output rather than proposed actions. 

Validation and Experience Guidance. The con-troller validates output schemas, source identifiers, and action or query legality; semantic sufficiency remains an LLM judgment. The optional experi-ence memory selects procedural checks from four predefined categories using completed-episode feedback. Retrieved checks guide subsequent as-sessments but cannot establish current task facts or override the original decision requirements.

This section provides the official prompts used in our benchmark evaluations. Prompts 1-3 follow the respective benchmark implementations. 

PM-Bench. Prompt 1 is the system instruction of the official PM-Bench agent. Each subsequent user message presents one step: a short event vignette with three options for the ongoing activity, followed by the menu of task actions available at that step. 

ProactiveBench. Prompt 2 is the official Proac-tiveBench agent prompt, and Prompt 3 is the of-ficial Active-RM evaluation prompt. The judge receives observations up to the current event in chronological order and the task proposed by the agent, which is null when the agent abstains. 

Shared Proposal Protocol on ProactiveBench. 

For the memory baselines, the agent is first given the retrieved memory content and the latest event with the instruction “You rate how appropriate it is for an AI assistant to PROACTIVELY offer help right now in an ongoing user session. Answer with a single integer 0-100 (0=clearly not, 100=clearly yes), nothing else.” When the rating reaches 50, the task is generated with the instruction “You are a proactive assistant. Based on the user’s current activity and the relevant context shown, propose ONE concrete task you could do for the user right now. You are taking a prospective memory evaluation that assess how you remember to do things later. One virtual day equals one circuit of the board (7 a.m. -> 10 p.m.). Time advances step by step with event cards. Your goal is to remember to perform tasks at the correct time or event without reminders. You must voluntarily remember to perform tasks at the right moment. If you are unsure, you may still act; that is allowed. No reminders will be given for missed tasks. Some cues will cancel, override, or reschedule tasks. Always follow the most recent instruction for any task. Each step presents a short vignette with three ongoing-task options (A/B/C). Pick one of A/B/C to advance. A step-level action menu is shown before each choice. You may perform one or more actions from the action menu in the same step. Each action appears as task_N: <action text>. Return task_N handles in task_ids. If you do not perform an action, set task_ids to []. Never repeat the same task_N handle in task_ids. Do not dump the whole menu into task_ids; include only the handles you are actually choosing now. Return at most 8 task handles in one choose action. Some tasks are triggered by state cues that are NOT shown in the vignette. You can only observe these cues by proactively querying state channels. Available state channels: <state channels of the scenario>. Use action=query_state to retrieve channel state (choice must be NONE).
