Long-term problem-solving processes, such as scientific research and software development, proceed along an evolving trial-and-error trajectory toward a central goal. We term these exploration tasks , which require models to remember not only facts but also how solutions are tested against intermediate outcomes and early attempts are continually revised or revived. However, existing long-term memory research focuses on factual recall and updates, overlooking the interconnected nature of exploration. We observe that exploration processes naturally form graphs, with branching approaches linked by revisions and reconsiderations. Motivated by this, we propose LExMem, a graph-guided benchmark designed to evaluate Memory in Long-term Exploration tasks. We model exploration processes as graphs and introduce an evolutionary mechanism to increase their structural complexity, yielding 224 dialogues and 2,018 question-answer instances derived from graph structures. Building on the same insight, we propose LExGraph, a graph-based memory framework that incrementally organizes dialogues into interconnected memory nodes and retrieves evidence through hybrid retrieval and neighbor expansion. Extensive experiments demonstrate that LExMem poses a significant challenge to existing retrieval-and memory-based systems, whereas LExGraph achieves superior performance and generalizes effectively to external benchmarks. Codes and datasets are available at https://github.com/mbzuai-nlp/LExMem .

## 1. Introduction 

Recent large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated impressive capabilities across a wide spectrum of tasks, including open-domain dialogue (Zhou et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2025b), code generation (Shi et al., 2026), and scientific research (Haase and Pokutta, 2025; Su et al., 2025). Long-term memory is essential for sustaining these interactions, enabling models to retain and utilize information accumulated over time. Therefore, numerous benchmarks and frameworks have been developed to evaluate and enhance this capability (Gutiérrez et al., 2024; Gutiérrez et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2026; Xu et al., 2026). One line of research investigates memory for user personas and personal facts in extended conversations (Maharana et al., 2024; Zhong et al., 2024; Jiang et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026). In parallel, evaluations have expanded to task-oriented scenarios (Hu et al., 2026; Shen et al., 2026). For instance, MemoryCode requires models to follow coding instructions that are introduced and updated across sessions (Rakotonirina et al., 2025), while RealMem investigates whether agents can track the states of ongoing projects (Bian et al., 2026). Collectively, existing benchmarks primarily assess the recall of information, ranging from personal facts to evolving task details. However, real-world problem solving often involves exploration tasks , where conversations evolve through iterative trial and error toward a central goal. As illustrated in Figure 1, such tasks involve testing tentative approaches against empirical evidence and continually revising decisions based on intermediate outcomes. Yet, existing benchmarks overlook the dynamics of exploration, leaving it unclear how effectively models can reason over interrelated information, trace the rationale of extended inquiry, and aggregate findings scattered across prolonged interactions (Maharana et al.,

Furthermore, existing memory frameworks organize dialogue history around semantic similarity (Xu et al., 2025a; Rezazadeh et al., 2025), topical clustering (Tan et al., 2025; Fang et al., 2026), and entity relations (Chhikara et al., 2025; Edge et al., 2025; Guo et al., 2025), overlooking the procedural dependencies required in sustained exploration. In this paper, we propose LExMem, a graph-guided benchmark for evaluating Memory in Long-term Exploration tasks. To this end, we leverage directed graphs to model exploration steps and their dependencies, enabling the systematic construction of complex exploration trajectories and relationship-aware questions. Specifically, we develop a three-stage construction pipeline. First, we construct exploration graphs and introduce an evolutionary mechanism to increase their structural complexity. Second, we instantiate these graphs with scientific research and software design tasks, and further expand each node into multi-turn dialogue blocks. Finally, we extract structural subgraphs to generate QA instances that evaluate memory of interconnected exploration steps. The resulting LExMem contains 224 dialogues and 2,018 QA instances across three graph-derived question types. Extensive experiments reveal that LExMem poses substantial challenges to existing retrieval and memory frameworks, particularly in aggregating information scattered throughout exploration. To address these challenges, we introduce LExGraph, a Graph-based memory framework designed to maintain structured context in Long-term Exploration. Rather than relying on predefined graphs, LExGraph incrementally constructs a memory graph from raw dialogues, organizing information into nodes connected by hierarchical and cross-branch dependencies. During inference, it retrieves relevant nodes and traverses their connections to integrate scattered evidence for reasoning. Experiments demonstrate that LExGraph consistently outperforms existing retrieval and memory systems on LExMem and generalizes effectively to external memory benchmarks.

Initial Graph Generation. We first prompt LLMs to generate an initial graph 𝐺 0 specifying only its topology (i.e., typed nodes and edges) without textual content, thereby separating structural generation from domain-specific content to explicitly control the complexity of exploration trajectories. Each graph is rooted at a goal node and hierarchically expands into downstream typed nodes, forming a spanning tree. Additional edges connect nodes across branches to capture dependencies between exploration paths. To model the dialogue progression, nodes are ordered chronologically via a depth-first preorder traversal, yielding a sequence of 𝑁 nodes (𝑢 1, . . . , 𝑢 𝑁 ) with 𝑢 1 as the root. 

Graph Evolution. While the initial graph establishes the dependencies among exploration steps, it may not adequately capture the non-monotonic nature of real-world exploration, where earlier approaches are revised, challenged, or revisited as new evidence emerges. To simulate these dynamics, we introduce an evolutionary mechanism that iteratively modifies the initial graph 𝐺 0 according to predefined mutation rules (Appendix A.1). Starting from 𝐺 = 𝐺 0, we scan the node sequence 

(𝑢 1, . . . , 𝑢 𝑁 ) in chronological order. At each node 𝑢 𝑡 , we define the prefix subgraph 𝐺 ≤𝑡 as the visited nodes 𝑢 1, . . . , 𝑢 𝑡 and their interconnecting edges. If node 𝑢 𝑡 and its incident edges match a rule, a mutation is triggered with probability 𝑝 ; when triggered, we prompt an LLM with 𝐺 ≤𝑡 to introduce new nodes and edges representing subsequent revisions, challenges, or reconsiderations. This generation further extends to complete the rest of the graph, yielding an updated graph 𝐺 ′ in which 𝐺 ≤𝑡 remains unchanged. We then set 𝐺 = 𝐺 ′, update its node sequence accordingly, and resume scanning from 𝑢 𝑡 +1 until the entire graph is traversed. Through LLM-based verification and manual curation, we retain 150 high-quality evolved graphs. As shown in Table 1, graph evolution substantially enriches the dynamics of sustained exploration while maintaining comparable graph sizes, providing a foundation for dialogue synthesis (§2.2).

2.2. Graph-Guided Dialogue Synthesis Seed Task Preparation. We first prepare 150 seed tasks across two domains: research exploration 

(100) and software design (50). Each task specifies a primary objective along with several requirement metrics. Details are provided in Appendix A.2. 

Graph Instantiation. Since the evolved graphs (§2.1) specify only node types and edge relations without concrete text, we instantiate them with domain-specific tasks to create coherent exploration trajectories while preserving their underlying structures. First, we randomly pair each seed task with four distinct evolved graphs, yielding 600 candidates, and directly assign a seed task with its task description and requirements to the root goal node ( 𝑢 1). For each subsequent node 𝑢 𝑡 (𝑡 = 2 , . . . , 𝑁 )in chronological order, we prompt the LLM with (1) all previously instantiated nodes and edges, (2) the type of 𝑢 𝑡 , and (3) the relations linking it to earlier nodes; it then generates concrete descriptions for 𝑢 𝑡 and its incident edges with specific details (e.g., exact metrics). To ensure consistency, we conduct two stages of LLM-based validation: (1) step-wise checks against prior context (2.3% filtered) and (2) a global consistency check over the completed graph (55.8% filtered). This finally yields 259 instantiated graphs, each denoted as 𝐺 *.

Dialogue Expansion. We further expand each instantiated graph 𝐺 * into a natural multi-turn conversation, transforming structured exploration steps into information distributed across prolonged interactions. Specifically, each node 𝑢 𝑡 is expanded into a dialogue block of multiple user-assistant turns, with the LLM conditioned on (1) all preceding dialogue blocks, (2) the content of 𝑢 𝑡 , and (3) its relations to earlier nodes. Each block must faithfully convey the corresponding node content without introducing unsupported facts, ensuring that the underlying exploration dependencies are preserved. We apply an LLM-based faithfulness check after each block (0% filtered), followed by a global dialogue consistency check (13.5% filtered). Finally, we obtain 224 high-quality dialogues with 51K to 174K tokens (88K on average), each denoted as 𝒟 and paired with its instantiated graph 𝐺 *.

2.3. QA Instance Generation 

To systematically evaluate memory of interconnected exploration processes, we derive three types of questions from distinct graph structures, each targeting a different reasoning capability. Specifically, we extract candidate subgraphs or node sets 𝑆 ⊆ 𝐺 * following three patterns: • Dense subgraphs. To assess joint reasoning over tightly interrelated information, we sample connected subgraphs 𝑆 = ( 𝑉 𝑆 , 𝐸 𝑆 ) satisfying |𝐸 𝑆 | ≥ 2|𝑉 𝑆 | and |𝑉 𝑆 | ≥ 4, ranked by edge density with a Jaccard penalty to reduce overlap. • Sparse subgraphs. To evaluate reasoning along long-range exploration trajectories, we extract subgraphs where two distant nodes are linked solely by a multi-hop path passing through the root node, with no other edges among the path nodes. Candidates are similarly ranked by path length with a Jaccard penalty. • Same-type sets. To evaluate the aggregation of information scattered throughout the exploration process, we group all nodes sharing the same functional type into a set 𝑆 .By grounding each question in a specific graph structure, we can explicitly identify the supporting evidence and assess distinct forms of relational reasoning. We sample up to nine candidates 𝑆 per pattern per graph, and prompt the LLM to formulate a question 𝑞 targeting 𝑆 alongside a concise answer set 𝒜 . We then apply LLM-based verification to assess (1) question clarity and validity (39.3% filtered) and (2) answerability based solely on 𝒟 (23.9% filtered). This yields 2,018 high-quality QA instances, each formulated as (𝒟 , 𝑞, 𝒜 ), comprising 561 dense, 759 sparse, and 698 same-type

## 3. Method 

3.1. Overview 

We propose LExGraph, a graph-based memory framework that incrementally organizes evolving dialogue information and preserves dependencies among exploration steps. Unlike the graph-guided benchmark construction, LExGraph builds its memory graph solely from raw dialogues, without access to the ground-truth graphs 𝐺 * of LExMem. First, given an ongoing dialogue 𝒟 , we incrementally construct a directed memory graph 𝒢 =(ℳ, ℒ) with memory nodes ℳ and relational edges ℒ (§3.2). Each node 𝑚 𝑖 ∈ ℳ is a key-value pair (𝑘 𝑖 , 𝑣 𝑖 ), where the key 𝑘 𝑖 summarizes the content and key facts, while the value 𝑣 𝑖 preserves the corresponding raw dialogue turns. Second, given a question 𝑞 , we retrieve relevant memory nodes and traverse their connections to aggregate supporting evidence for response generation (§3.3). 

3.2. Memory Graph Construction Dialogue Segmentation. To organize conversational memory, we partition dialogue 𝒟 into topic-coherent segments that serve as memory nodes. Since users typically guide the discussion, we detect topic shifts based on user turns only. Specifically, we maintain a buffer of unsegmented dialogue turns and invoke an LLM after every 𝑛 new user turns to identify topic boundaries within it. Dialogue turns preceding the detected boundaries are grouped into memory nodes, each storing the corresponding user and assistant turns as its value 𝑣 𝑖 , while the remaining turns stay in the buffer. 

Key Generation. For each node 𝑚 𝑖 , the LLM generates a structured key 𝑘 𝑖 from its value 𝑣 𝑖 ,comprising (1) a concise summary of the main discussion and (2) a list of salient facts recording concrete details, such as entities and numerical values. This preserves both the overall context and concrete information that a general summary may omit. 

Edge Construction. Once its key 𝑘 𝑖 and value 𝑣 𝑖 are obtained, we integrate node 𝑚 𝑖 into the graph 

𝒢 by establishing two types of edges: • Type I (Hierarchical): parent-child edges tracing the hierarchical progression of dialogue topics. • Type II (Cross-branch): associative edges linking related information across different branches of the exploration process. For Type I , we draw on the right frontier constraint from discourse theory (Asher and Lascarides, 2003) to guide parent selection. It posits that in a discourse tree, a new utterance (here, node 𝑚 𝑖 )can only attach to the path from the root ( 𝑚 1) to the most recent utterance ( 𝑚 𝑖 −1), while utterances outside this path are closed to direct continuation. We maintain this path as the right frontier ℱ

(initialized as [𝑚 1]), which constrains parent selection and ensures structural coherence. Given the keys of 𝑚 𝑖 and ℱ, the LLM selects a parent pa( 𝑚 𝑖 ) ∈ ℱ and describes their relation. We then add the edge (pa( 𝑚 𝑖 ), 𝑚 𝑖 ) to ℒ, and update ℱ to the new path [𝑚 1, . . . , pa( 𝑚 𝑖 ), 𝑚 𝑖 ].For Type II , we consider historical nodes outside the updated frontier, defined as ℋ =

{𝑚 1, . . . , 𝑚 𝑖 −1} ∖ ℱ , to capture cross-branch associations, and select those relevant to 𝑚 𝑖 :

Given a question 𝑞 , we score each node 𝑚 𝑖 ∈ ℳ through two complementary channels to capture both semantic relevance and lexical matches: (1) semantic matching , measured by the cosine similarity cos( e𝑞 , e𝑖 ) between 𝑞 and 𝑘 𝑖 , and (2) lexical matching , measured by the BM25 score between 𝑞 and 𝑘 𝑖 (Robertson and Zaragoza, 2009). Answer Generation. This grounds the answer in structured memories, thereby facilitating accurate and context-aware answering. 

## 4. Experimental Setting 

4.1. Baselines 

Each instance in LExMem is formulated as (𝒟 , 𝑞, 𝒜 ) (§2.3). Given dialogue 𝒟 and question 𝑞 , each baseline is required to generate an answer ^𝒜 . To ensure a comprehensive analysis, we compare LExGraph with the following baselines: (1) Retrieval-based methods, which build indices or structured representations over the context to retrieve information, including ComoRAG (Wang et al., 2026), GraphRAG (Edge et al., 2025), HippoRAG (Gutiérrez et al., 2024), HippoRAG2 (Gutiérrez et al., 2025), and LightRAG (Guo et al., 2025); and (2) Memory-based methods, which construct and manage memory structures, including LightMem (Fang et al., 2026), Mem0 (Chhikara et al., 2025), and StructMem (Xu et al., 2026). For these approaches, we first build their own index or memory over 𝒟 , and then retrieve context relevant to 𝑞 , on which the backbone LLM conditions to generate ^𝒜 . Details are described in Appendix B.1. 

4.2. Benchmarks and Evaluation Metrics 

We conduct comprehensive evaluations on LExMem with two metrics: (1) Token-level F1 , adopted from SQuAD (Rajpurkar et al., 2016) to measure lexical overlap between ^𝒜 and 𝒜 ; and (2) Accuracy (ACC) , following BEAM (Tavakoli et al., 2026), where an LLM judge scores each answer as correct (1.0), partially correct ( 0.5), or incorrect ( 0.0). To verify the generalizability of our method LExGraph, we further evaluate it on the 128K-token subset of BEAM (Tavakoli et al., 2026), which covers ten long-term conversational memory abilities, and report ACC.

5.2. Benchmark Analysis Capability Distribution. To enable fine-grained evaluation on exploration tasks, we categorize all instances into six capabilities: Fact Retrieval (FR), State Tracking (ST), Entity Discrimination (ED), Numeric Reasoning (NR), Relation Tracing (RT), and Information Aggregation (IA), with details of definitions and categorization in Appendix B.3. As shown in Figure 3(a), each question type exhibits distinct capability focuses: Dense questions primarily map to FR and ST, indicating that tightly coupled contexts mainly test localized facts and state changes; Sparse questions are dominated by IA, which demands evidence aggregation across long-range exploration trajectories; and Same-Type questions overwhelmingly target ED, requiring fine-grained discrimination among similar entities. 

Error Analysis. To diagnose failures, we analyze instances where the Vanilla baseline achieves zero accuracy on LExMem, as shown in Figure 3(b). First, errors are mainly concentrated in Same-Type questions and dominated by ED, indicating that models may easily confuse similar contexts accumulated during exploration. Same-Type questions also account for nearly all failures in NR, suggesting that the primary difficulty of numerical reasoning lies in comparative reasoning across similar information. Second, Dense errors mainly consist of FR and ST, consistent with the primary capabilities tested by Dense questions. Third, Sparse errors primarily flow into IA while spanning capabilities such as FR, ED, and RT. This demonstrates that long-term exploration presents compound reasoning challenges where extended and evolving contexts disrupt global evidence synthesis.

In contrast, LExGraph consistently outperforms both baselines, with the most pronounced advantages on NR and RT. This may stem from LExGraph’s hierarchical and cross-branch edges that ensure multi-hop tracing, alongside structured keys enabling comparative reasoning.

6.2. Memory and Retrieval Frameworks 

Early memory frameworks manage dialogue history as independent memory units, such as summaries and memos (Lu et al., 2023; Zhong et al., 2024; Chhikara et al., 2025). Further efforts introduce management strategies such as reflective mechanisms (Tan et al., 2025), hierarchical storage (Kang et al., 2025), and memory compression (Fang et al., 2026) to balance accuracy and efficiency. To capture associations among memory units, subsequent studies organize conversational history into structured representations, including trees (Rezazadeh et al., 2025), graphs (Xu et al., 2025a; Rasmussen et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2026), and hypergraphs (Yue et al., 2026). Another line of research on retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) structures the corpus into hierarchical summary trees (Sarthi et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2026) or entity knowledge graphs (Gutiérrez et al., 2024; Edge et al., 2025; Guo et al., 2025; Gutiérrez et al., 2025) to facilitate multi-hop retrieval. However, current frameworks primarily capture semantic or entity-level associations rather than dialogue progression, where exploration branches out, revisits earlier attempts, and builds upon intermediate outcomes. 

## 7. Conclusion 

In this paper, we address the shortcomings of evaluating and enhancing long-term memory in exploration tasks. We propose LExMem, a graph-guided benchmark that models exploration processes as graphs enriched through evolution, deriving 224 dialogues and 2,018 QA instances from graph structures. We further present LExGraph, a graph-based memory framework that structures dialogues into memory graphs and gathers evidence by hybrid retrieval and neighbor expansion. Extensive

experiments demonstrate that existing retrieval and memory frameworks struggle on LExMem, whereas LExGraph achieves consistent improvements over strong baselines and generalizes to external benchmarks. By providing a systematic evaluation benchmark and an effective memory framework, this work helps develop more reliable conversational agents for long-term exploration. 

## Limitations 

Despite the contributions of LExMem and LExGraph, our study is subject to several limitations that remain to be addressed in future research. 

Synthetic Dialogues. Real-world exploration dialogues are difficult to collect at scale, as they span long horizons, often involve unpublished research or proprietary development, and lack annotations of the dependencies among exploration steps. We therefore synthesize dialogues from instantiated exploration graphs, which allows explicit control over trajectory complexity and provides ground-truth structures for question generation, with quality verified through LLM-based checks and human evaluation. Nevertheless, synthetic dialogues may not fully capture the stylistic diversity of real conversations. 

Evaluation of Downstream Exploration. LExMem evaluates memory of the exploration process via question answering, rather than whether stronger memory leads to better exploration decisions. Such end-to-end evaluation is inherently difficult, as open-ended exploration rarely has a unique correct next step, and verifying one would require actually conducting the corresponding experiments. We leave this to future work.

## A. Details of Benchmark Curation 

A.1. Details of Evolutionary Graph Construction 

In this paper, 𝑉 contains nodes of nine functional types: goal , proposal , question , hypothesis , plan ,

activity , evidence , decision , and artifact ; 𝐸 consists of edges labeled with nine relations: address ,

propose , depend-on , decompose , evaluate , support , refute , revise , and revive .As outlined in §2.1, we define six mutation rules grouped into five categories to introduce realistic exploration twists. Each rule specifies a structural precondition in the prefix subgraph 𝐺 ≤𝑡 and a required follow-up evolutionary event. We detail the categories below. 

Contradictory Evidence. Real-world exploration frequently uncovers conflicting signals rather than smooth consensus. This rule matches when an evidence node supports a hypothesis or proposal , and no refute edge points to that target in 𝐺 ≤𝑡 . We specify that subsequent nodes should introduce new 

evidence that refutes the target while preserving the existing support edge, thereby capturing an emergent discussion. 

Reconsidering Refuted Ideas. As investigation deepens, prematurely dismissed concepts are sometimes vindicated. This rule applies when a hypothesis or proposal is refuted by evidence and has received no incoming support edge in 𝐺 ≤𝑡 . In this case, subsequent nodes should introduce new 

evidence that supports the target, while keeping the earlier refutation. 

Revising Hypotheses. Hypothesis adjustments often prompt chained modifications or a return to earlier assumptions. This category matches when a hypothesis node revises an earlier one, branching into two rules: (1) a later hypothesis modifies the new hypothesis once again; (2) new evidence refutes the new hypothesis and supports the earlier one, provided the new hypothesis has not yet been refuted. This second trajectory captures realizing an earlier idea was correct after a failed modification, while preserving the intermediate attempt in the graph history. 

Revising Artifacts. System components such as datasets typically undergo iterative refinement before deployment. This rule matches when an artifact revises an earlier artifact . The subsequent graph is encouraged to introduce an additional artifact that revises it once again, ensuring that intermediate artifacts represent ongoing development rather than final deployment. 

Evaluating Revived Ideas. This category matches when an evidence or decision node revives an earlier hypothesis or proposal via a revive edge, and the target currently lacks follow-up evaluation in 

𝐺 ≤𝑡 . The follow-up trajectory must resolve the revived thread by introducing new evidence that either validates or refutes it. 

A.2. Details of Seed Task Preparation 

We construct seed tasks on solution exploration across two domains: research exploration and software design . Given a domain, we prompt LLMs to generate a concise task description that defines the primary objective, paired with one to three requirements that specify concrete metrics. We then filter out low-quality tasks using LLMs, followed by manual review, yielding 150 seed tasks (100 for research exploration and 50 for software design).

## B. Details of Experiments 

B.1. Details of Baselines 

This section provides details about the baseline methods used in our experiments. We keep each method’s own pipeline and default hyperparameters. For methods that split the dialogue into fixed passages, each passage is a window of 10 consecutive dialogue turns. • ComoRAG (Wang et al., 2026) organizes knowledge into veridical, semantic, and episodic layers, iteratively querying them via a metacognitive loop to aggregate cross-layer evidence. We cap the loop at 5 iterations with a 2,000-token per-layer budget.

• GraphRAG (Edge et al., 2025) constructs an entity–relation graph from chunks and clusters them via Leiden to generate hierarchical community summaries. • HippoRAG (Gutiérrez et al., 2024) extracts open information extraction triples into a graph, links query entities to graph nodes, and scores passages via Personalized PageRank (PPR; Haveliwala, 2002). Following official defaults, we set the synonym edge threshold to 0.8 and damping factor to 0.5, retrieving the top-5 ranked passages as 𝑋 .• HippoRAG2 (Gutiérrez et al., 2025) embeds passage nodes directly into the knowledge graph, links queries to triples, and prunes irrelevant triples with an LLM filter prior to PPR. We retain default settings and retrieve the top-5 passages as 𝑋 .• LightRAG (Guo et al., 2025) indexes entity–relation graphs and text chunks for dual-level retrieval. We adopt the mix mode, retrieving the top-40 graph elements and top-20 chunks as 𝑋 .• LightMem (Fang et al., 2026) manages sensory, short-term, and long-term memory through LLMLingua-2 (Pan et al., 2024) compression and offline consolidation. We set compression rate to 0.6 and update threshold to 0.9. We extract memories across both user and assistant turns and retrieve the top-60 entries as 𝑋 .• Mem0 (Chhikara et al., 2025) dynamically extracts atomic facts from user–assistant turns and resolves them against a persistent memory store. Candidate memories are ranked via hybrid dense–sparse retrieval and entity matching, with the top-200 memories returned as 𝑋 .• StructMem (Xu et al., 2026) organizes dialogue history into an event graph capturing causal and temporal relations. At inference, it activates query-relevant event subgraphs and returns the top-60 event-grounded memory entries as 𝑋 .

B.2. Results on Evidence Retrieval 

We further examine whether our method and retrieval-based approaches can recover the evidence turns ℰ annotated in Appendix A.3. Specifically, for each approach, we take its top-𝐾 retrieved results ( 𝐾 ∈ { 5, 10 , 20 }), each corresponding to a window of 10 consecutive dialogue turns. For our method, since memory nodes vary in length, each retrieved memory node is mapped to the 10-turn window that contains the node’s first dialogue turn, so that all approaches are compared at the same granularity. A retrieved window is counted as a hit if it contains at least one evidence turn in ℰ. We report two metrics: (1) Recall@ 𝐾 , the proportion of evidence turns in ℰ that appear in the top-𝐾 

windows, and (2) NDCG@ 𝐾 , which further accounts for the ranks of hit windows. As shown in Table 5, our method achieves the highest NDCG under all cutoffs, together with the highest Recall@5. Meanwhile, HippoRAG2 attains higher Recall at larger cutoffs (e.g., 0.591 vs. 0.550 at 𝐾 = 20 ), demonstrating its ability to retrieve evidence scattered across the dialogue. Nevertheless, our method maintains a higher NDCG even at 𝐾 = 20 (0.354 vs. 0.323). Overall, these results demonstrate the strength of our method in precisely locating evidence. 

B.3. Details of Benchmark Analysis 

We categorize each question into one of six core capabilities based on the primary reasoning process required: • Fact Retrieval (FR): Locating an explicit, isolated fact stated directly within conversational turns without requiring multi-step derivation. • State Tracking (ST): Monitoring temporal modifications to an entity, parameter, or environment status across turns to identify its latest state. • Entity Discrimination (ED): Distinguishing the target entity, attempt, or artifact from structurally or semantically similar candidates accumulated during exploration.

• Numeric Reasoning (NR): Performing arithmetic calculations, quantitative metric comparisons, or statistical summaries that are not directly stated in the dialogue context. • Relation Tracing (RT): Traversing multi-hop associative, hierarchical, or causal links connecting distinct entities or exploration steps. • Information Aggregation (IA): Synthesizing multiple pieces of non-contiguous evidence dispersed across prolonged dialogue contexts into a coherent conclusion. Specifically, we employ Claude-5.5 Opus (Anthropic, 2026) to determine the capability most central to answering each question in LExMem. For each instance, the model is provided with the question 𝑞 , the ground-truth answer 𝒜 , and the raw dialogue turns corresponding to the golden evidence nodes in 𝐺 *. The model is instructed to assign the single capability most central to answering the question. To verify taxonomy completeness, an Other option is included in the candidate set but is never selected across the benchmark, confirming that the six capabilities fully encompass the reasoning requirements in LExMem.

For benchmark curation, we set the mutation probability 𝑝 = 0 .25 (§2.1) and the weight of the Jaccard penalty to 0.65 (§2.3). Dialogue lengths after expansion (§2.2) are measured with the Qwen-3.8 

tokenizer (Qwen Team, 2026). For implementation details of LExGraph, we set 𝑛 = 20 , 𝜏 sim = 0 .75 ,

𝜅 = 10 , 𝐵 pri = 4096 , and 𝐵 nbr = 4096 . For evaluation, the ACC judge is GPT-4o mini of version 2024-07-18.
