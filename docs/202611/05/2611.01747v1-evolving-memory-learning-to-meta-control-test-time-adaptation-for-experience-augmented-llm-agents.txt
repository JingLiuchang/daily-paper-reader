Abstract 

Self-evolving agents accumulate experience from task streams, but existing frameworks typically rely on fixed pipelines to retrieve and utilize this experience, neglecting when and how to deploy it. We introduce Online Meta-Control of Experience (OMCE ), a frame-work that updates experience memory and learns a meta-control policy from task feed-back during test-time adaptation. OMCE em-ploys a lightweight contextual-bandit meta-controller that uses compact retrieval signals to select among four cognitive actions—DIRECT, EPISODE, GUIDE, and DISTILL. This rout-ing mechanism operationalizes three coupled decisions: whether to invoke experience, which memory format to utilize, and when to con-solidate new guidance. OMCE evolves online, leveraging task feedback to update the mem-ory bank and refine the routing policy via con-textual bandit updates. Experiments on four online adaptation benchmarks across four back-bones show that OMCE improves first-attempt success by up to 20.5 percentage points over the strongest compared baseline, while reduc-ing per-task token use by a factor of 5.7–62.5 relative to ACE with GPT-5.2. Controlled ab-lations further support the benefits of learned routing and continued routing policy adapta-tion. 

1 Introduction 

Experience-oriented self-evolving LLM agents turn interaction feedback into reusable experience for later tasks (Gao et al., 2025; Fang et al., 2025a; Chen et al., 2026). This experience can preserve concrete task cases or take more abstract forms, in-cluding reasoning guidance, reusable skills, and context playbooks (Fang et al., 2025b; Ouyang et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2025a). Its availability, however, does not guarantee its util-ity for the current task: semantic similarity alone

may retrieve unhelpful experience (Zhang et al., 2026b). Even when experience is useful, concrete cases retain execution details but may not transfer readily to different tasks, while distilled guidance captures reusable principles but can omit details needed for execution (Fang et al., 2025b; Ouyang et al., 2025). Extending this memory also poses a further choice: distilling guidance from accu-mulated episodes may help future tasks, but also incurs additional computational cost and can lead to excessive memory growth(Zhang et al., 2025a). These trade-offs leave open how experience should be used and expanded as tasks arrive. We argue that scheduling accumulated experi-ence is a learnable dimension of self-evolution, and make it precise through three requirements. (1) 

Adaptive invocation : whether experience is needed at all (Fang et al., 2025b; Ouyang et al., 2025). (2) 

Selective form : which of its forms to use (Fang et al., 2025b; Zhang et al., 2025a). (3) Learnable consolidation : when to dis-till transferable guidance from episodic evidence (Zhang et al., 2025a; Shinn et al., 2023). Meta-control research on adaptive tool use (Li et al., 2025) and cognitive-demand monitoring (Scoville et al., 2026) suggests that adapting rea-soning effort to task demands can be more effective than uniformly allocating computation (De Sabbata et al., 2024).These frameworks, however, control external, fixed resources such as a tool set or a rea-soning budget. Accumulated experience differs: the agent produces it itself, in heterogeneous forms, in a store that grows and shifts, so scheduling it requires a decision over use and consolidation, not a single invocation flag. To this end, we propose Online Meta-Control of Experience (OMCE ), an agent framework that learns to control experience use and consolidation through a lightweight meta-controller over episodic and guidance memory. 2 Related Work 

Experience Memory and Self-Evolving Agents. 

Experience-augmented reasoning has advanced LLM-agent memory systems from case-level trajec-tory retrieval (Fang et al., 2025b; Shinn et al., 2023) to structured agentic knowledge bases (Tang et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2025). Other work stores reusable plans, skills, or heuristics for cross-task transfer (Wang et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2025; Allard et al., 2026), or distills experience into general principles, evolving playbooks, and reusable reasoning memories through generation, reflection, and refinement (Zhao et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025a; Ouyang et al., 2025). MemRL (Zhang et al., 2026b) improves retrieval by scoring memory candidates with a non-parametric Q-value function, and Evo-Memory (Wei et al., 2025) benchmarks retrieval, integration, and update in continuous task streams. Overall, these efforts show that experience can be accumulated, abstracted, and maintained in multiple forms. However, most frameworks still use a fixed retrieve-inject-reflect pipeline, with lim-ited control over whether memory is useful for the current task, which type of experience to consult, or whether a new outcome should be consolidated (Majumder et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2025). 

Meta-Control in LLM Agents. Learned meta-control decides whether to invoke a resource and how much to spend on it: a tool (Li et al., 2025), a deliberation budget (De Sabbata et al., 2024; Ji-An et al., 2025), test-time compute (Snell et al., 2024), a learning mode (Dupoux et al., 2026), or a verifier (Wan et al., 2026); related work also edits the agent’s program directly (Zhang et al., 2026a). Dual-process agents separate fast execu-tion from slower deliberation (Evans, 2011; Evans and Stanovich, 2013; Liu et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2025b), and feedback-driven methods improve these meta-level policies with reward signals (Wu and Qu, 2025). Closest to our setting are controllers selecting among reasoning strategies at inference time: Meta-Reasoner (Sui et al., 2026) learns with a contextual bandit when to backtrack or restart within one problem, and MetaScale (Liu et al., 2026) evolves meta-thoughts as the sampling bud-get grows. Both focus on controlling predefined resources, but OMCE instead learns to manage the experience an agent accumulates over time, jointly deciding when to use existing experience and when 2to distill new guidance. 

3 Online Meta-Control of Experience 

Given a sequential stream of queries Q =

{q1, . . . , q N }, OMCE equips an LLM agent with Episodic Memory, Guidance Memory, and a Meta-Controller (Fig. 2). For each query, the controller uses meta-state to select an action that determines whether and which experience to inject into the task prompt, including whether to distill new guidance before execution. The resulting augmented context is supplied to the agent. Task feedback then updates the stored expe-rience and the controller’s policy, allowing both to adapt across the stream.

ηi = ( τi, r i, Ωi) contains the task trajectory τi,the immediate reward ri ∈ [0 , 1] , and a small set of case-specific insight entries Ωi = {ωi,m }Mi

For retrieval, both case-specific insights and guidance entries use the same two-part format: a compact description of the applicable scenario and a detailed principle. The description serves as the cue for query-to-memory matching, while the prin-ciple supplies the content for injection into the task prompt.

4 Experiments 

We evaluate all methods in an online adaptation setting, where tasks arrive and must be solved se-quentially. After completing an instance, the agent observes task feedback and may update its mem-ory, controller, or context before processing the next instance. 

4.1 Experimental Design 

We organize the evaluation around five questions. 

RQ1 : does learned routing raise first-attempt suc-cess across benchmarks and backbones? RQ2 : how should the routing policy be determined, in partic-ular is a fixed form of experience use sufficient, and does a learned controller beat fixed and hand-crafted alternatives? RQ3 : do the gains come from the routing policy, or from the memory content and the update schedule? RQ4 : does the controller keep improving as the stream proceeds? RQ5 : what is the token cost of these gains? Section 4.2 addresses RQ1, Section 4.3 addresses RQ2 to RQ4, and Sec-tion 4.4 addresses RQ5. The evaluated methods span four test-time adap-tation regimes: no external memory ( Base LLM ), within-instance retry ( Reflexion (Shinn et al., 2023)), cross-task adaptation ( MemP (Fang et al., 2025b)), and cross-task adaptation with multi-attempt inference, including ACE (Zhang et al., 2025a), ReasoningBank (Ouyang et al., 2025) and our OMCE , which differs from these baselines in learning when to invoke experience and which form to use rather than following a fixed routine. Against OMCE , we additionally compare policies that always commit to one form of experience and a hand-crafted rule over retrieval scores (Section 4.3). We report two variants of OMCE : OMCE -TS and 

OMCE -UCB, which implement meta-evolution with two contextual-bandit action-selection algorithms: Thompson Sampling (TS) (Agrawal and Goyal, 2013) and LinUCB (Chu et al., 2011; Abbasi-Yadkori et al., 2011), respectively (detailed in Ap-pendix B). We evaluate on four complementary benchmarks spanning distinct task domains under this shared setting: BigCodeBench (Zhuo et al., 2024) and SWE-bench Verified (Jimenez et al., 2023) for code generation and repository-level software engi-neering, MultiChallenge (Deshpande et al., 2025) for realistic multi-turn conversation, and BBEH (Kazemi et al., 2025) for advanced general reason-ing. Across all benchmarks, we use four LLMs as backbone models: GPT-5.2 (OpenAI, 2025), DeepSeek-V3.2 (Liu et al., 2025), DeepSeek-V4-pro (DeepSeek-AI, 2026), and Kimi-K2.5 (Team et al., 2026). We use one-shot success rate (OSR) as the pri-mary metric, also called first-attempt success: it measures whether the current policy and accumu-lated experience solve an instance on the first at-tempt. For methods with retry mechanisms, we additionally report cumulative success rate (CSR), defined as success within a shared budget of P = 4 

total attempts, including the initial attempt (Sec-tion G.3). Reflexion does not transfer experience across instances, so by construction its first at-tempt matches the Base LLM under this proto-col; we therefore report its CSR only, while OMCE ’s CSR combines within-instance retries with cross-instance adaptation. Full details on benchmark construction, trial protocol, baseline implementa-tions, metrics, and controller variants are provided in Appendix G. Both variants share one configu-ration across all benchmarks and backbones; the values and the selection procedure are given there.

5 Conclusion 

In this work, we presented OMCE , a framework that recasts experience-augmented reasoning from an always-on memory pipeline into a learnable meta-cognitive control problem. Instead of treating re-trieval, context injection, reflection, and consolida-tion as a fixed routine, OMCE employs a lightweight meta-controller based on compact retrieval features and linear reward models to govern the full life-cycle of experience: choosing among DIRECT, EPISODE, GUIDE, and DISTILL. Crucially, this meta-controller evolves online via task feedback, progressively improving the agent’s judgment over its own cognitive processes. Evaluations across four online adaptation benchmarks demonstrate that OMCE establishes state-of-the-art OSR and a favorable performance-token trade-off. Ultimately, our findings suggest that as self-evolving agents mature, the primary bottleneck of online adaptation shifts from merely accumulating richer experience to learning exactly when and how that experience should be deployed and updated. 8Limitations 

OMCE inherently relies on timely feedback. In en-vironments with sparse or delayed rewards, the contextual-bandit updates can become noisy, and abrupt task distribution shifts may render both the accumulated experience and the learned meta-policy stale. Moreover, due to the partial observ-ability of the bandit formulation, the controller lacks counterfactual signals regarding unchosen cognitive routes, potentially slowing its recovery from early suboptimal decisions. Additionally, as the memory banks expand indefinitely, retrieval may suffer from redundancy or semantic collisions. To address these limitations, future work could integrate established memory-maintenance tech-niques (e.g., explicit aging or eviction of stored memory entries). More fundamentally, future it-erations could leverage the LLM’s reflective capa-bilities to simulate counterfactual rewards for un-chosen actions (potentially accelerating bandit con-vergence), or complement the current meta-control framework with explicit memory auditing and prun-ing, advancing towards a fully autonomous lifelong-learning agent. Our controlled comparisons of memory use and controller updates focus on GPT-5.2, while the rout-ing comparison spans all four backbones. Testing the same interventions on other backbones would help assess whether the observed effects generalize.

G Details for Experimental Design 

G.1 Task Setting. 

Our experiments follow the online adaptation eval-uation setting (Zhang et al., 2025a), where each method processes the test stream in a single sequen-tial pass. For every incoming instance, the agent first produces its prediction using the current pol-icy, memory, or context, observes the task feedback, and then updates the corresponding memory or con-troller state before moving to the next instance. To make the comparison controlled, all methods use the same task order on each benchmark, the same backbone LLM, and the same evaluation pipeline. Each method is evaluated in a single run over each benchmark–backbone configuration due to the cost of LLM API calls. Reported success rates aggre-gate outcomes across benchmark instances. For methods supporting retries, each instance allows up to four total attempts, including the initial at-tempt; these are within-instance retries rather than independent experimental runs. Methods that sup-port multi-attempt inference use a shared budget of 

P = 4 total attempts, following Section G.3, and 

OMCE may stop early once a task is solved. 

G.2 Benchmarks. 

Under this setting, we consider four comple-mentary benchmarks spanning code generation, repository-level software engineering, realistic multi-turn conversation, and advanced general rea-soning. English is the language used for all bench-marks. 

Code generation and software engineering. Big-CodeBench (Zhuo et al., 2024) is a function-level code generation benchmark emphasizing complex instructions and diverse function or tool calls, with unit-test-based automatic evaluation to reflect prac-tical programming behavior. SWE-bench Verified 

(Jimenez et al., 2023) is a human-validated subset of SWE-bench designed to assess whether agents can resolve real-world GitHub issues in repository-scale codebases. 

Multi-turn conversation. MultiChallenge (Desh-pande et al., 2025) measures whether a model can handle realistic, challenging user interactions over extended dialogue contexts, with particular empha-sis on instruction-following, context allocation, and in-context reasoning. We use GPT-4o-mini (Ope-nAI, 2024) as the judge model, to judge whether the answer meets the hidden task requirement. 

Advanced general reasoning. BIG-Bench Extra 

18 Hard (BBEH) (Kazemi et al., 2025) is a substan-tially more challenging successor to BIG-Bench Hard (BBH) (Srivastava et al., 2023; Suzgun et al., 2023), covering a broad range of general reasoning skills. We use a shuffled 6-task subset comprising 1,200 instances: causal_understanding (Nie et al., 2024; Kıcıman et al., 2023), dyck_languages (Tyen et al., 2023), temporal_sequence, web_of_lies (White et al., 2024), word_sorting (Tyen et al., 2023), and zebra_puzzles (Shah et al., 2024). These instances are mixed and randomly shuffled to con-struct the evaluation stream. 

G.3 Evaluation Metrics. 

We adopt one-shot success rate (OSR ) as the pri-mary evaluation metric, defined as the fraction of test instances solved correctly on the first attempt during online adaptation. This metric directly mea-sures the agent’s immediate response quality un-der its current policy and accumulated experience. When multiple attempts are allowed, we addition-ally report cumulative success rate (CSR ), defined as the fraction of instances solved within a maxi-mum budget of P = 4 total attempts, including the initial attempt. This corresponds to at most three retries after the initial attempt. For methods with-out multi-attempt inference, the unavailable metric is marked as “-” in the result tables. Since Reflex-ion (Shinn et al., 2023) improves later attempts through within-instance self-reflection rather than cross-task experience transfer, we report it under CSR in the main comparison. To measure inference efficiency, we report tokens per task , the average total LLM tokens consumed per instance. This count includes all LLM calls made throughout the full online adaptation pipeline for a task, rather than only the final task-solving call; in particular, it includes token usage from auxiliary stages such as memory update and other adaptation-related inference, as well as additional token consumption introduced by test-time scaling in baselines that use it. The average is computed by dividing the total token consumption by the total number of tasks. Token accounting includes the cost of solving tasks and the cost of extracting and refining experiences, but excludes evaluator or judge-model calls. 

G.4 Baselines and Methods. 

For baselines and compared methods, we include a Base LLM and four representative test-time im-provement approaches that enhance performance without parameter updates. These baselines sepa-rate test-time scaling alone, cross-task experience transfer alone, and methods that combine cross-task adaptation with multi-attempt inference. 

Base LLM. Base LLM is evaluated on each bench-mark without any external memory, i.e., the model is queried with the benchmark’s default prompt template (as provided by the dataset authors) and produces an answer directly. 

Reflexion (Shinn et al., 2023). Reflexion instanti-ates test-time scaling through within-instance self-reflection over repeated attempts. After an attempt, the agent converts task feedback into a natural-language self-reflection and conditions subsequent attempts on these reflections. In our comparison, Reflexion does not transfer experience across dif-ferent test instances. 

MemP (Fang et al., 2025b). MemP transfers expe-rience across tasks but makes only a single attempt per instance. It therefore represents cross-task ex-perience transfer without multi-attempt inference. 

ACE (Zhang et al., 2025a). ACE treats prompts/-contexts as evolving playbooks and improves them through a modular generate-reflect-curate loop with structured, incremental updates designed to pre-vent context degradation across iterations; ACE supports both offline prompt optimization and on-line adaptation via memory/context updates driven by execution feedback. 

ReasoningBank (Ouyang et al., 2025). We evalu-ate ReasoningBank in its sequential MaTTS setting, where the method combines cross-task adaptation with multi-attempt inference. 

OMCE (ours). OMCE combines memory evolution with online meta-evolution. OMCE -TS uses Thomp-son Sampling (Agrawal and Goyal, 2013), while 

OMCE -UCB uses an optimistic LinUCB-style rule (Chu et al., 2011; Abbasi-Yadkori et al., 2011). Appendix B gives the formal specifications, and Appendix E analyzes the optimistic variant.

H Detailed Efficiency Results 

Figure 5 reports tokens per task , as defined in Section G.3, across four backbone LLMs (GPT-5.2, Deepseek-V3.2, Deepseek-V4-pro, and Kimi-K2.5) on four benchmarks. Lower values indicate better inference efficiency. The figure reveals a clear and consistent pattern across all four backbone models. Base LLM and MemP are the most token-efficient methods be-cause they eschew test-time scaling, and therefore add limited overhead beyond direct inference. Rea-soningBank and OMCE incur a moderate additional token budget due to retrieval, reflection, and struc-tured online adaptation, but remain far more effi-cient than large-context prompting. By contrast, ACE is dramatically less efficient: rel-ative to OMCE , it requires about 62.5 × more tokens per task on BigCodeBench, 32.5 × more on SWE-bench Verified, 5.7 × more on MultiChallenge, and 21.8 × more on BBEH. This inefficiency is con-sistent with ACE’s strategy of unconditionally in-jecting the entire playbook into the prompt, which substantially inflates the context length as adapta-tion proceeds. Taken together with Figure 3, these results place 

OMCE between the two extremes on every bench-mark and backbone: more accurate than the token-cheap baselines, and far cheaper than ACE.

J Broader Impact 

This work introduces Online Meta-Control of Ex-perience ( OMCE ), a framework designed to improve the efficiency and reliability of self-evolving LLM agents. The ability to selectively ignore misleading or redundant past experiences helps mitigate the propagation of errors in memory-enhanced agent systems. This contributes to the development of more robust AI assistants. However, like all frame-works that enhance the autonomy and efficiency of AI agents, OMCE could potentially be repur-posed for malicious activities. While OMCE offers a pathway toward more intelligent and resource-efficient agents, its deployment should be accompa-nied by continuous monitoring and safety protocols to ensure that its evolutionary capabilities remain aligned with ethical standards and human values. 22
