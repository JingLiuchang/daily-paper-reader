# Abstract 

Despite advances in retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), suppressing hallucina-tions during complex reasoning remains a persistent challenge. We identify that existing RAG pipelines suffer from a textual bottleneck: by passing only raw text to the generator, they discard discriminative signals of the retriever. Furthermore, recent attempts to unify retrieval and generation within a single LLM induce severe objective conflict and exacerbate the rank collapse of decoder-only architectures, degrading performance on both tasks. To address these limitations, we introduce ShiftRAG, a decoupled RAG framework that employs a single LLM backbone but isolates discriminative retrieval learning from autoregressive generation. To bridge these decoupled modes, we project retrieval alignments into continuous soft tokens, establishing a high-bandwidth interface that infuses the generator with retrieval-side signals. Concurrently, our soft orthogonality objective mitigates rank collapse, while layer-wise relevance dynamics preserve the fine-grained latent space required for highly discriminative evidence separation. Extensive evaluations demonstrate that ShiftRAG significantly improves retrieval and question answering performance over state-of-the-art baselines. ShiftRAG preserves foundational capabilities of the backbone while maintaining a favorable efficiency-performance trade-off on general text encoding and generative tasks. Our code is available 2.

# 1 Introduction 

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) [37] has emerged as the definitive framework for grounding LLMs with external knowledge across diverse knowledge-intensive domains, including multi-hop question answering (MHQA) [17, 43, 69] and knowledge graph reasoning [39, 42, 49, 57]. Current research has advanced RAG through three avenues: (i) learning dense representations specifically for retrieval [30, 31, 36, 52], (ii) adapting decoder-only LLMs into text encoders [2, 35, 41], and (iii) sup-porting both retrieval and generation within a single LLM backbone [45, 68]. Meanwhile, researchers have proposed to augment reasoning over retrieved evidence through explicit Chain-of-Thought [40, 61] and latent reasoning [55, 64]. Despite these sophisticated architectural enhancements, suppressing hallucinations [4, 25, 27, 28, 51] during retrieval-based reasoning remains a persistent challenge. We claim that this limitation stems from three factors. First, overcoming the rank collapse inherent to decoder-only LLMs [12, 53] while preserving generative capacity remains a fundamental bottleneck. In deep layers, token representations naturally concentrate into a low-dimensional subspace, blurring the fine-grained semantic distinctions necessary to separate true evidence from distractors. Current paradigms fail to resolve this trade-off: while contrastive adaptation in deep encoders [2, 35, 41] mitigates rank collapse, it severely degrades autoregressive generation. Unified LLMs [45, 68] preserve generation via backbone sharing, but inadvertently exacerbate rank collapse, exhibiting higher covariance and lower eRank than the base model (Gemma 2) 5. Sharing a backbone or relying on standard attention mechanisms is insufficient to maintain both generative fluency and the discriminative representations for retrieval. Second, standard RAG frameworks [5, 36, 52] suffer from a textual bottleneck, i.e., they transfer only raw document text to the LLM, discarding the implicit signals (the semantic alignment between question and document embeddings) from the retriever. Consequently, the generator is deprived of the retriever’s underlying discriminative confidence. Figure 1(c) highlights this limitation: while an evidence-sensitive generator [63] should exhibit distinct predictive entropy gaps between correct and incorrect outputs, existing state-of-the-art baselines show small entropy differences 6. This indicates that raw text alone fails to convey the strength of the supporting evidence. While Chain-of-Thought [40, 61] and continuous latent reasoning [55, 64] attempt to extract deeper rationales, they still derive these representations exclusively from explicit text. Lacking a mechanism to directly inject the embedding-level alignment of the retriever, these methods remain constrained by the textual interface. Third, unifying retrieval and generation under shared adaptation parameters induces severe objective conflict. Discriminative retrieval requires a latent topology optimized for contrastive separation and fine-grained diversity by aligning the representation of a question with its supporting documents and separating it from distractors, whereas autoregressive generation organizes the representation space for sequential next-token prediction. In unified architectures [45, 68], forcing these fundamentally divergent tasks to share parameters creates competing optimization pressures, leading to representation instability and negative transfer. Even if adaptation parameters are decoupled, simultaneous joint training remains suboptimal; the generator is forced to optimize against a continuously shifting retrieval-conditioned interface, destabilizing retrieval convergence and downstream reasoning. To address these issues, we introduce ShiftRAG , a novel RAG framework that employs a single LLM backbone across decoupled encoder and decoder modes. ShiftRAG transcends the standard textual interface by translating high-dimensional retrieval knowledge directly into latent reasoning signals. Our contributions are threefold. First, we cultivate a highly discriminative embedding space by exploiting layer-wise relevance dynamics, effectively mitigating the deep-layer rank collapse typical of decoder-only LLMs. Second, we project question–document embeddings and relevance scores into generation-friendly soft knowledge tokens. This latent bridge allows implicit retrieval alignments and explicit document text to jointly guide the reasoning of the generator. Third, by training the encoder and decoder modes independently and bridging them only at inference, we circumvent the severe objective conflict that plagues joint parameter optimization. Extensive evaluations demonstrate that ShiftRAG achieves state-of-the-art retrieval and end-to-end QA performance across multi-hop (HotpotQA, 2WikiMultihopQA, MuSiQue) and single-hop (NaturalQuestions) benchmarks. Furthermore, auxiliary evaluations on text encoding and general generative tasks confirm that our decoupled approach preserves foundational capabilities of the backbone while maintaining a favorable efficiency-performance trade-off, establishing a robust new paradigm for retrieval-augmented reasoning.

# 2 Related Work 

RAG. Traditional dense retrieval maps queries and documents into unified vector spaces [30, 36, 46], while subsequent methods capture finer-grained interactions [31, 52] or exploit layer-wise representations [33]. Recently, LLMs have been adapted as powerful text encoders [2, 35, 41] and unified models capable of both retrieval and generation [32, 45, 58, 68]. However, despite these architectural advances, the RAG pipeline remains fundamentally disjointed. Because both LLM-based encoders and unified models interface with the generator exclusively via raw retrieved text, they discard the rich and latent discriminative signals formed during the retrieval process, limiting the ability of the generator to weight evidence effectively. 

Latent Chain-of-Thought. While Chain-of-Thought (CoT) methods [10, 40, 61, 67] enhance reasoning transparency, they suffer from high generation latency and sensitivity to retrieved noise. Latent-CoT approaches [18, 55, 64] address this by compressing intermediate reasoning into con-tinuous latent vectors. However, because these continuous representations are generated only from autoregressive prediction, they are structurally isolated from external knowledge modules, heavily restricting their integration into RAG systems. 

Rank Collapse of LLMs. As LLMs increase in depth, hidden token representations tend to converge directionally, concentrating within a narrow subspace. This phenomenon, known as rank collapse, reduces the effective rank and representational diversity of deep-layer tokens, impairing fine-grained semantic discrimination. Theoretical studies [11, 47] demonstrate that this contraction accumulates across repeated self-attention layers. While architectural components like attention masks and LayerNorm can influence the geometry of this collapse [65], the degradation is particularly detrimental in decoder-only Transformers, where it severely weakens token-level expressivity [1, 6, 12, 53]. Despite this established limitation, recent LLM-based text encoders [2, 35] optimize for retrieval without explicitly mitigating the rank collapse of their pretrained backbones, hindering the formation of a semantically well-separated embedding space. This bottleneck becomes even more acute in unified LLMs [45, 68], where forcing generation and representation learning to share the same collapsed hidden space creates severe objective conflict. 

# 3 Method 

To enable a single LLM to master both retrieval and reasoning, we fine-tune a pre-trained causal LLM with two task-specific LoRA adapters. In encoder mode , we relax the causal mask to allow bidirectional self-attention, adapting the model to capture deep question-document semantic relevance. In decoder mode , the LLM reverts to its native autoregressive decoding to generate answers, leveraging the discriminative insights gained from the encoder mode. Figure 2 illustrates the overall inference framework of ShiftRAG, which consists of three stages: retrieval, semantic signal translation, and reasoning. For offline indexing, the encoder-mode LLM maps every document into an embedding space. In the retrieval stage at inference, the encoder mode embeds the input question into this shared space to retrieve top-K relevant documents. In the semantic signal translation stage, we then translate the question-document embedding pairs and their corresponding relevance scores into soft knowledge tokens. These latent guidance vectors bridge the gap between the discriminative encoder and the generative decoder by mapping retrieval signals directly into the token embedding space of the decoder. Finally, in the reasoning stage, the LLM 3Figure 3: Overall framework of ShiftRAG which separately trains the encoder and decoder modes. is switched to decoder mode, generating a grounded answer based on the question, the retrieved document text, and the injected soft knowledge tokens. 

3.1 Gap-aware and Collapse-resilient Deep Encoder 

To repurpose the decoder-only LLM as an encoder, we enable bidirectional self-attention (as illustrated in Figure 3), allowing the model to aggregate global context. To capture deep question-document relevance, we exploit the layer-wise representation gap (Section 3.1.1), which captures the semantic maturation of the input from shallow to deep layers. To mitigate rank collapse, we introduce a soft orthogonality constraint that regularizes the hidden representations of all tokens within a sequence (Section 3.1.2). This ensures that the model preserves discriminative feature diversity, preventing the ‘narrow cone’ effect often seen in deep transformer layers. 

3.1.1 Layer-wise Gap-aware Contrastive Learning

3.3 Soft Knowledge-guided Reasoning 

In the reasoning stage, the LLM generates a grounded answer by integrating retrieved document texts with their corresponding soft knowledge tokens. To bridge the gap between retrieval-side discriminative signals and generative reasoning, we optimize the decode-mode adapters LoRA dec 

via a dual objective: (1) rationale-conditioned self-distillation, where a student LLM with LoRA dec 

learns to mimic the output distribution of the same frozen LLM backbone conditioned on explicit rationales; and (2) next-token prediction (NTP) on the target answer to preserve linguistic coherence and factual grounding. As illustrated in Figure 3, this configuration ensures that the generator treats the soft knowledge tokens as a latent blueprint for the final response. 4.1 Experimental Setup 

We evaluate our framework mainly on five datasets: NaturalQuestions [34] for single-hop QA, HotpotQA [66], 2WikiMultihopQA [22], and MuSiQue [59] for multi-hop QA, and SimpleQA [62] for factual QA. Each dataset contains 1,000 test questions. For the multi-hop QA benchmarks, we follow the test split configuration of RaLa [33] and compare all retrieval-based baselines under identical conditions. We optimize the encoder-mode LoRA adapter ( r = 16 ) on the E5 [60] dataset by following LLM2Vec [2]. The decoder-mode LoRA adapter is trained with a higher rank ( r = 64 )on a hop-aware mixture constructed from both single- and multi-hop QA benchmarks. We use Gemma2-9B [14], LlaMA3.1-8B [16], Mistral-7B [29], and Qwen2.5-7B [50] as backbones, apply the SOA loss to the last four layers, and use five soft knowledge tokens for each question–document pair. Further details regarding the experimental setup and baselines are described in Appendix A.

Joint learning with shared parameters exhibits severe optimization instability, leading to a large degradation in QA 8performance, e.g., EM drops precipitously to 0.1300 on HotpotQA and 0.1270 on 2WikiMultihopQA. These results demonstrate that soft tokens do not merely act as verbalized scores; instead, they serve as an effective interface that transfers complex, high-dimensional discriminative signals that are lost during textualization. 

4.6 Geometric Intuition for Efficacy of Semantic Signal Translator 

Figure 5: t-SNE visualization of anchor token embeddings (red), anchor token embeddings + offset embeddings (green), and original document token embeddings (orange) for each benchmark. Figure 5 visualizes the anchor token embeddings E, the translated embeddings E + ∆ Ek, and the decoder-mode document token embeddings ( Doc. Tokens ) in a shared t-SNE space. The anchor embeddings E form a localized set of reference points that are poorly aligned with the distribution of document tokens. However, upon incorporating the offset embeddings ∆Ek, the resultant vectors migrate toward the document token clusters, exhibiting a substantial distributional overlap across all datasets. This shift suggests that ∆Ek effectively projects the anchor-token basis into a vocabulary-aligned region that is semantically congruent with the retrieved evidence. Consequently, the soft knowledge tokens enable SST to infuse the generator with retrieval-side knowledge effectively. This visualization confirms that our anchor-plus-translation design serves as a robust bridge between the retriever’s latent space and the generator’s reasoning manifold.

# 5 Conclusion and Limitation 

We introduce ShiftRAG, a decoupled RAG framework that isolates discriminative retrieval learning from autoregressive generation. By proposing the semantic signal translator (SST), we successfully shift implicit high-dimensional retrieval alignments into latent soft knowledge tokens, providing an effective bridge between the retriever and generator. Furthermore, the soft orthogonality objective effectively mitigates rank collapse, while layer-wise gap-aware contrastive learning preserves the fine-grained latent topology required for robust evidence discrimination. Empirical evaluations demonstrate that ShiftRAG achieves superior retrieval accuracy and downstream QA quality. Despite its strong empirical performance, our framework presents two limitations. Unlike explicit CoT pipelines, the soft knowledge tokens are continuous and inherently uninterpretable. Because our architecture relies on extracting and translating internal embedding states, it requires white-box access to the model weights; it cannot be applied to black-box LLMs.

Datasets. We train encoder mode on the E5 [60] dataset following LLM2Vec [2]. For decoder mode, we construct a hop-aware mixed training set by sampling instances from the training splits of NaturalQuestions [34], HotpotQA [66], 2WikiMultihopQA [22], and MuSiQue [59] according to hop count. Table 8 shows the training-set statistics. The final training set contains 10,000 examples: 2,000 1-hop, 6,000 2-hop, 500 3-hop, and 1,500 4-hop examples. Table 9 shows the test-set statistics for HotpotQA, 2WikiMultihopQA, MuSiQue, and SimpleQA [62]. Each benchmark contains 1,000 questions, and Documents denotes the size of the retrieval pool. We follow the test split configuration of RaLa [33] and compare all baselines under identical conditions. 

Baselines. We evaluate ShiftRAG against four categories of baselines. Encoder-based retrievers include Contriever [36], GTR [46], ColBERTv2 [52], and RaLa [33]. LLM-based retrievers include GTE-Qwen2-7B-Instruct [41], LLM2Vec [2], and NV-Embed-v2 [35]. Unified LLM baselines include OneGen [68] and GritLM [45]. Latent-CoT baselines include Coconut [18], CODI [55], and Sim-CoT [64]. For fair comparison, we fine-tune LLM2Vec and OneGen using the same three backbones as ShiftRAG: Gemma2-9B [14], LlaMA3.1-8B [16], and Mistral-7B [29]. We use Gemma2-9B as the default backbone because it achieves the strongest retrieval and QA performance for both ShiftRAG and the fine-tuned baselines among the tested LLM backbones. Unless otherwise specified, we report results using Gemma2-9B. Since GritLM requires full fine-tuning, we use the open-weight checkpoint released on Hugging Face without additional training. Latent-CoT methods are not originally designed for retrieval-based QA; therefore, their adapted results are reported separately in Appendix C.6. 

Evaluation Metrics. For evaluating ShiftRAG, we select the most commonly used retrieval, QA, embedding and generative benchmarks. For the retrieval task, to measure the ability to identify relevant information within a large-scale corpus, we use Recall@ K (R@ K) as the primary metric, where K ∈ { 2, 5, 10 }. It measures the proportion of instances where the ground-truth document is ranked within the top-K retrieved results. This metric focuses on the capacity of the model to narrow down the search space accurately. For the question answering task, to measure the precision of the model in generating correct answers based on provided contexts or internal knowledge, we employ two standard metrics: Exact Match (EM) measures the percentage of predictions that exactly match the ground-truth answers. F1-score evaluates the harmonic mean of precision and recall based on the word-level overlap between the predicted and ground truth answers, providing a broader assessment of semantic alignment. Following the setup of GritLM [45] and the MTEB [44] benchmark, we also evaluate the quality of the learned embedding representations through three different tasks: (1) Clustering (Clust.) : We perform K-means clustering on the text embeddings and assess the alignment between the cluster assignments and ground-truth labels using the V-measure .(2) Semantic Textual Similarity (STS) : We compute the cosine similarity between sentence embeddings and report the Spearman correlation with human-annotated similarity scores to verify how well the embeddings capture semantic relatedness. (3) Summarization (Summ.) : We measure the cosine similarity between the embeddings of original texts and their summaries. The performance is scored via Spearman correlation against human quality ratings to evaluate the model’s ability to encode core information. To assess reasoning and multilingual proficiency, we follow the generative evaluation framework of GritLM [45], utilizing the following benchmarks: (1) MMLU : We evaluate the model’s broad world knowledge across various disciplines (humanities, social sciences, etc.) in a Zero-shot (0 FS) setting, using Exact Match (EM) as the evaluation metric. 

18 (2) GSM8K : To test multi-step mathematical reasoning, we apply 8-shot (8 FS) prompting with Chain-of-Thought (CoT). The performance is measured by the Exact Match (EM) of the final numerical answer. (3) TyDiQA : We evaluate multilingual QA capabilities using the Gold Passage (GP) and zero-shot (0 FS) setup. Given the linguistic diversity of the tasks, we report the F1-score to assess the accuracy and robustness of the generated responses across different languages. 

Implementation Details. We train ShiftRAG on four NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPUs and run inference on a single GPU. Both encoder mode and decoder mode are trained with LoRA [23] adapters. For the encoder, we employ a global batch size of 16 and a LoRA rank of r = 16 ; for the decoder, we use a global batch size of 8 and r = 64 .The LGC loss is calculated with a bucket size of four layers and a temperature of τ = 0 .05 . The SOA loss is applied to the final n = 4 candidate layers with a coefficient of λSOA = 1 × 10 −3. In decoder mode, the knowledge distillation weight is set to λKD = 0 .75 . SST is configured to generate five soft knowledge tokens per question–document pair. Experiments on MTEB [44], MMLU [21], GSM8K [9], and TyDiQA [8] follow the protocol of GritLM [45]. We evaluate our approach across several backbone LLMs, including Gemma2-9B [14], LlaMA3.1-8B [16], Mistral-7B [29], and Qwen2.5-7B [50].

B.2 Anchor Token Initialization 

In our decoder-mode training, we initialize the anchor token embeddings as the pretrained latent vectors [72] to align soft knowledge tokens with the token embedding space of the generator. For a question q and a retrieved document dk , SST constructs soft knowledge tokens Tk ∈ RM ×h by combining anchor token embeddings E ∈ RM ×h with document-conditioned translation embeddings and positional embeddings, as described in Section 3.3. Since the document-conditioned translation embeddings are derived from encoder-mode representations, the resulting soft knowledge tokens may not be well aligned with the token embedding space used by the generator. Following SPRING [72], we freeze the student LLM and optimize only the anchor token embeddings E, which serve as the trainable basis of Tk .For each training question-document pair (q, d k ), we use an offline-generated target rationale that explains whether dk helps answer the question and how it contributes to the answer. The rationale construction procedure and the prompt for anchor token initialization are provided in Appendices H.1 and H.2, respectively. Given dk ,

q, and E, we form the initialization context as 

XATI = [ Instruction , d k , q, E] (13) The frozen student LLM then autoregressively predicts the target rationale from XATI using the standard next-token prediction (NTP) objective. During this stage, gradients are back-propagated only to E, while all other 

19 parameters remain fixed. This procedure therefore does not tune the student LLM itself; it only learns a small set of anchor embeddings that can be plugged into the generator’s token space. This initialization is performed once before the decoder-mode training. By optimizing E with rationale supervision, this stage provides a vocabulary-aligned basis for SST-generated soft knowledge tokens and stabilizes the later translation of retrieval-side signals into the decoder. The initialized anchor embeddings do not encode document-specific knowledge by themselves; rather, they act as reference points that allow the document-conditioned translation embeddings to produce generation-friendly soft knowledge tokens. The target rationales are strictly reserved for the training pretraining anchor token embeddings and for teacher-side supervision employed by knowledge distillation. Empirically, the optimization of anchor token embeddings E

takes less than one hour.

24 D.2 Complexity Comparison with RaLa 

We analyze the computational complexity and training memory of RaLa-style contrasting compared with our gap-aware relevance scoring. Following the notation in Section 3.1, let |q| and |d| denote the sequence lengths of a question and a document, respectively, h denote the hidden dimension size, and |C| denote the number of candidate layers used for layer contrast. In the unoptimized RaLa formulation, layer contrast is computed over token-level MaxSim across all question–document token pairs and layers from each bucket. Therefore, its scoring complexity is O(|C|·| q|·| d|· h). The optimized RaLa formulation reduces the layer-contrast computation to a [CLS] -based gap-aware weight, but its main matching score still relies on token-level average MaxSim. Thus, its asymptotic complexity becomes O(|q| · | d| · h), which is asymptotically identical to ColBERT-style late interaction. In contrast, our gap-aware relevance scoring computes layer-wise semantic alignment gaps over mean-pooled question and document representations. Once the pooled representations are obtained, the scoring complexity is O(|C| · h), and even when mean pooling is counted, the cost grows only linearly with the sequence length, rather than bilinearly with |q|| d|. Thus, our method removes the token-token interaction term required by average MaxSim while using the layer-wise semantic alignment gap itself as the contrastive similarity score in the LGC loss. This difference is particularly important for LLM-based retrieval encoders. Since LLM hidden states have much larger hidden dimensions than conventional encoder backbones, applying average MaxSim to LLM hidden states substantially increases peak memory usage. In our preliminary training with a Gemma2-2B backbone, average MaxSim either caused out-of-memory errors or required reducing the per-device batch size from 8 to 2. To maintain the same global batch size, the gradient accumulation step had to be increased by 4×, resulting in roughly 16 × longer training time in our setup. Our mean-pooled gap-aware relevance scoring avoids this token-level MaxSim computation, allows training with the original per-device batch size, and more directly reflects layer-wise semantic alignment in the retrieval objective. We further speculate that token-level average MaxSim may be less suitable for decoder-only LLM encoders whose late-layer token representations already exhibit rank-collapse tendencies, because MaxSim assumes sufficiently discriminative token-level representations.

Figure 7: Average token entropy over the generated answer on MHQA benchmarks, grouped by answer correctness. Red values indicate the entropy gap between incorrect and correct cases. 

As shown in Figure 7, for LLM2Vec[2], retrieval is performed using the LLM2Vec encoder, while answer generation is conducted by the backbone LLM. Across all three datasets, ShiftRAG exhibits the clearest entropy separation between correct and incorrect answers. On HotpotQA, the entropy gap is 0.8760 for ShiftRAG, compared with 0.3967 for GritLM[45], 0.1291 for OneGen[68], and 0.0415 for LLM2Vec. A similar trend is observed on 2WikiMultihopQA, where ShiftRAG again shows the largest entropy gap of 1.0004, substantially exceeding those of GritLM (0.3324), OneGen (0.2174), and LLM2Vec (0.0664). On MuSiQue, ShiftRAG also achieves the largest gap at 0.8933, compared with 0.3196 for GritLM, 0.1768 for OneGen, and 0.0626 for LLM2Vec. These results indicate that existing baselines remain relatively overconfident even when their answers are incorrect, as reflected by their limited entropy separation between correct and incorrect cases. In contrast, ShiftRAG assigns substantially higher entropy to incorrect decodings while maintaining lower entropy on correct ones, yielding a much clearer separation by answer correctness. This supports our claim that transferring retrieval-side knowledge through SST and soft knowledge tokens leads to more correctness-aware decoding behavior and better mitigates hallucination-prone overconfidence.

F.1 Impact of Batch Size 

We conduct experiments on encoder mode and decoder mode to evaluate the impact of batch size. Figure 11 (a) illustrates the R@2 metrics for batch sizes of 4, 8, 16, 32, and 64. The results show that a batch size of 16 yields the highest R@2, while performance drops sharply when the batch size exceeds 32. This trend can be attributed to the dynamics of training with in-batch negatives; smaller batch sizes increase the relative proportion of hard negatives within the batch, thereby facilitating the optimization of the layer-wise gap-aware contrastive (LGC) loss. However, when the batch size is reduced below 16, the absolute number of negative samples becomes insufficient, hindering the overall training process. In contrast to the retrieval performance, the decoder mode shows no significant variation in EM as the batch size increases, as illustrated in Figure 11 (d). This is because the objective functions for decoder mode do not depend on the number of negative samples, unlike the encoder mode objective. While the remaining figures focus on the 2WikiMultihopQA dataset, other datasets, including HotpotQA and MuSiQue, follow a similar trend. 

F.2 Impact of LoRA Rank 

We conduct experiments in both encoder and decoder modes to analyze the impact of the LoRA rank r. As illustrated in Figures 11 (b) and 11 (e), the model exhibits highly stable performance across various rank settings. Specifically, while R@2 and EM both peak at r = 64, the overall variance remains marginal. In encoder mode, R@2 shows only minor fluctuations as r increases from 4 to 64. Similarly, in decoder mode, EM remains relatively consistent across rank values, with only a negligible decline observed at r = 128. While remaining figures focus on the 2WikiMultihopQA dataset, other datasets, including HotpotQA and MuSiQue, follow a similar trend.

# G Case Study 

G.1 Knowledge Comparison across Different Attention Policies 

Figure 14 illustrates how the attention policy used in encoder-mode training affects the retrieved evidence. For the question “Where was the place of death of Abdul-Aziz Bin Muhammad’s father?”, the bidirectionally trained retriever returns both Abdul-Aziz bin Muhammad and Muhammad bin Saud, where the latter provides the key evidence that the father’s place of death was “Diriyah”. In contrast, the unidirectionally trained retriever still retrieves “Abdul-Aziz bin Muhammad”, but fails to retrieve Muhammad bin Saud and instead returns lexically similar yet non-supporting documents such as Abdul Aziz bin Fahd and Abdullah ibn Muhammad. This comparison shows that the two attention mechanisms rely on different signals. Bidirectional retrieval is better at capturing global relational semantics needed to connect father and place of death, whereas unidirectional retrieval is more prone to lexical-overlap bias over surface entity names. As a result, the bidirectionally trained retriever identifies more answer-supporting evidence, while the unidirectionally trained retriever tends to retrieve documents that are locally similar in wording but less useful for reasoning.
