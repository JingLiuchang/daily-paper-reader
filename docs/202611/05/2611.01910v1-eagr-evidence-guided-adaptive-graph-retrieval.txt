Abstract 

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) for multi-hop reasoning requires retrieval to adapt as intermediate evidence reveals new infor-mation needs. Existing approaches support such retrieval through explicit graph structures or iterative query reformulation, introducing graph-construction or query-time overhead. We present Evidence-Guided Adaptive Graph Re-trieval (EAGR), a framework in which accumu-lated evidence dynamically determines which semantic directions to explore next, and which requires no dedicated graph database and no explicit graph materialization. EAGR operates over an implicit semantic graph induced by compact passage metadata pre-ingested into a standard VectorDB. Starting from dense-retrieval seed passages, an LLM iteratively as-sesses accumulated evidence, identifies promis-ing semantic directions, expands the retrieval frontier through metadata-based neighborhood lookup, and terminates once sufficient evidence has been collected. By precomputing semantic metadata, EAGR enables evidence-conditioned graph exploration without materializing an ex-plicit graph, maintaining a dedicated GraphDB, or iteratively rewriting the query. Across three widely used multi-hop QA benchmarks, EAGR achieves the highest LLM-based answer accu-racy among evaluated methods, outperforming the vanilla RAG by 17% and strongest graph-aware baseline by 4% percentage points av-eraged across three popular benchmarks Hot-PotQA, 2WikiMultiHopQA, and MuSiQue. 

1 Introduction 

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) grounds Large Language Models (LLMs) in external knowl-edge (Lewis et al., 2020). Conventional RAG re-trieves a fixed set of passages using dense similarity and delegates reasoning to the LLM (Karpukhin et al., 2020). This works for localized evidence, but

often struggles when an answer requires combining information across documents through dependent retrieval steps (Xiong et al., 2021; Trivedi et al., 2023). Such questions require retrieval to evolve as evidence accumulates. The full retrieval path is rarely apparent from the original query. For example, a question about a person’s mother-in-law may require first finding a passage about their spouse , then using that ev-idence to retrieve information about the mother .The next useful retrieval direction thus emerges from intermediate evidence, rather than from the query alone. Recent work tackles multi-hop retrieval using graph-based structures, including hierarchical sum-mary graphs, knowledge graphs, associative mem-ory graphs, and relation-free passage graphs (Edge et al., 2025; Gutiérrez et al., 2024, 2025; Wu et al., 2026a; Wang et al., 2024; Li et al., 2025). These methods demonstrate the value of semantic connec-tivity, but commonly add a graph representation or indexing stage to the passage index. Another line of work treats retrieval over explicit knowl-edge graphs as a planning problem. Systems such as StructGPT, Think-on-Graph, and Plan-on-Graph use LLMs to explore graph neighborhoods and re-fine their decisions (Jiang et al., 2023a; Sun et al., 2024b; Chen et al., 2024a). They assume a sym-bolic graph whose entities and relations are known 

a priori .We study a different setting: evidence-conditioned traversal over unstructured pas-sages (Li et al., 2026b). Enterprise VectorDBs typi-cally store passages and metadata, not explicit sym-bolic graphs. We derive passage connections from shared keyword and semantic descriptors in the ex-isting index, then use metadata lookups to retrieve connected passages. This enables GraphRAG-style traversal without a separate graph store or a sepa-rately materialized graph. The central challenge is to discover which semantic directions to explore as arXiv:2611.01910v1 [cs.IR] 1 Nov 2026 Figure 1: Comparison of various Retrieval Approaches 

evidence accumulates, rather than to execute a plan over a known graph. We propose Evidence-Guided Adaptive Graph Retrieval (EAGR) , which views retrieval as an adaptive search process. Each passage is asso-ciated with LLM-generated semantic descriptors stored as metadata; shared descriptors induce im-plicit connections without explicit graph edges. Re-trieval starts with dense-retrieved seed passages. At each iteration, an LLM assesses the accumu-lated evidence, selects promising directions, and ex-pands the retrieval frontier through metadata-based neighborhood lookup. Unlike planning-based ap-proaches, EAGR neither executes an explicit rea-soning plan (Chen et al., 2024a, 2026) nor repeat-edly rewrites the query: its traversal adapts to the evidence gathered so far. Our contributions are: 1. Evidence-guided adaptive retrieval. We formulate retrieval decisions around both the original query and accumulated evidence, en-abling iterative LLM-guided exploration with-out repeated query rewriting. 2. GraphRAG over a standard VectorDB. 

We derive implicit passage connections from LLM-generated semantic descriptors stored as metadata, enabling neighborhood lookup without materialized graph edges or a separate graph store. 3. EAGR traversal strategy. We introduce an iterative procedure that selects semantic direc-tions, expands the retrieval frontier, and stops when sufficient supporting context has been collected. 2 Related Work 

Adaptive retrieval. Dense retrievers rank pas-sages once against the query (Karpukhin et al., 2020). Multi-hop methods instead condition later retrieval on earlier passages or the model’s rea-soning state (Xiong et al., 2021; Trivedi et al., 2023; Shao et al., 2023; Jiang et al., 2023b), and re-cent work uses intermediate knowledge, sufficiency judgements or identified information gaps to steer subsequent steps (Zhuang et al., 2024; Jeong et al., 2024; Asai et al., 2024; Su et al., 2024; Fang et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026b,a). Most of these methods adapt by issuing new queries against the entire cor-pus index. EAGR keeps the original query fixed and adapts through accumulated evidence instead, restricting each step to the neighbourhood of the passages already explored. 

Graph-structured retrieval. Graph-based RAG organizes corpora into summary hierarchies, entity– relation graphs or associative networks (Sarthi et al., 2024; Edge et al., 2024; Gutiérrez et al., 2024, 2025; Zhu et al., 2025; Guo et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2026b), and lighter designs reduce construction cost with incidence links instead of triples (Huang et al., 2025; Zhuang et al., 2026). These structures expose cross-passage connections, but they must be built and stored, and query-time retrieval typi-cally follows community hierarchies, graph scores such as personalized PageRank, or fixed expansion rules. EAGR builds no graph and replaces fixed rules with evidence-guided search. 

LLM-guided graph traversal. Think-on-Graph (Sun et al., 2024a), Plan-on-Graph (Chen et al., 2024b) and ToG 2.0 (Ma et al., 2025) let an LLM navigate symbolic knowledge graphs, and GraphReader (Li et al., 2024), GeAR (Shen et al., 2025) and ReMindRAG (Hu et al., 2025) navigate graphs extracted from text. All assume a graph built in advance. Closest to EAGR are passage-level methods: KGP links passages via shared keywords and selects neighbours from the retrieval history (Wang et al., 2024), GNN-Ret propagates relevance over keyword graphs with a graph neural network (Li et al., 2025), HopRAG links passages through pseudo-queries (Liu et al., 2025b), and CuriousLLM uses accumulated evidence to generate follow-up queries and decide when to stop (Yang et al., 2025). EAGR differs from these approaches in three ways. (i) No materialized graph: adjacency is induced on demand from descriptors stored as ordinary VectorDB metadata, so no edge set, graph index, GNN or pseudo-query index is built. (ii) Evidence-guided expansion without query rewriting: the evidence pooled across explored passages decides which descriptors of the local frontier to follow next, rather than generating new queries over the whole corpus or ranking a fixed candidate pool. (iii) Verified stopping and evidence-based ranking: traversal stops only when the LLM’s reasoning chain is supported by the collected evidence, and the retrieved passages are ranked by that chain, rather than by query simi-larity alone. 

3 Methodology 

Figure 2 illustrates our overall pipeline, consisting of an offline stage that induces an implicit graph over the corpus (Fig. 2a) and an online stage that traverses this graph via evidence-guided frontier search (Fig. 2b). 

3.1 Problem Formulation 

Let C = {p1, . . . , p N } denote a corpus of passages and q denote an information-seeking query. Stan-dard retrieval in RAG systems typically formulates retrieval as a mapping from the query to a ranked set of passages, 

Rstd (q, C) → P ,

where P denotes the retrieved passages. Under this formulation, the query serves as the primary condi-tioning signal throughout retrieval. For multi-hop queries, however, the evidence needed to answer 

q is spread across several passages, and what ear-lier retrieval uncovers often determines what must be retrieved next. For example, the passage that identifies a film’s director is what reveals which biography to retrieve. Query-only retrieval does not model this evolving context. We therefore treat adaptive retrieval as a first-class retrieval problem , in which each retrieval step is conditioned jointly on the original query q and the evidence accumulated so far. Let Mt denote the evidence accumulated after t retrieval iterations. The passages retrieved next depend on both: 

Pt+1 = Radapt (q, Mt, C),

Mt+1 = Mt ∪ Evidence( Pt+1 ),

where Evidence( ·) extracts the facts in the newly retrieved passages that bear on q. Retrieval thus al-ternates between retrieving passages and updating the evidence state, and Radapt need not search all of C at every step: in EAGR it searches only the neighbourhood of the passages explored so far. The objective is to acquire sufficient evidence to answer q while avoiding unnecessary retrieval. Given a retrieval budget B, defined as the maxi-mum number of nodes that may be explored, the process terminates when the accumulated evidence is sufficient to answer q, or when the exploration budget is exhausted, and returns a relevance-based reordered list of passages. Unlike iterative methods (Yao et al., 2023) that issue new model-generated queries at each step, our formulation keeps the original query q fixed throughout retrieval. Adapta-tion happens instead through the evolving evidence state Mt, which determines what to seek next with-out altering the original information need. In the following sections, we instantiate this formulation through an implicit semantic graph abstraction 3.2 and an evidence-guided adaptive graph retrieval procedure 3.3. 

3.2 Offline Stage: Implicit Graph Construction (Figure 2a) 

To enable adaptive retrieval beyond the initial set of retrieved passages, we introduce an implicit se-mantic graph abstraction over the corpus. Rather than explicitly constructing and storing a graph, we attach to each passage pi ∈ C a set of semantic descriptors Ki = {ki1, . . . , k im i }, the entities and key concepts it mentions, and store them as meta-data alongside the passage in the VectorDB. Descriptor Extraction and Adjacency. A single LLM call per passage extracts the named entities it mentions (people, organizations, locations, titles of works, events, products) and a few key concepts. Descriptors are lowercased, stripped of accents and punctuation, and kept only if they occur in the passage, which removes hallucinated descriptors. Dates are not used as descriptors, since they con-nect many unrelated passages. Finally, because the same person is often named differently across pas-sages (e.g., Clarence Brown in a film’s passage and 

Clarence Leon Brown in his biography), a corpus-level step links shorter forms of a person’s name to the full name, so that such passages become neighbours. Appendix E gives the prompt and the complete procedure. 

3.3 Online Stage: Evidence-Guided Frontier Search (Figure 1b) 

Given a query q, EAGR first retrieves the top-k

passages by dense passage retrieval (DPR). Each passage is represented as vi = ( pi, K i), with con-tent pi and semantic descriptors Ki. These seed passages form the initial frontier V0, and the accu-mulated evidence is initialized as M0 = ∅. EAGR then proceeds frontier by frontier: it assesses ev-ery node of the current frontier Vt, adding the evi-dence they contain to Mt, and uses this evidence to decide whether to stop and, if not, which direc-tions of the implicit graph to explore next. Each iteration comprises five stages: Node Assessment and Evidence Accumulation , Edge Selection , Suf-ficiency Check , Evidence-Guided Edge Reranking 

and Graph Expansion . Node Assessment and Edge Selection are performed jointly in a single LLM call per node; the prompts are given in Appendix G. 

Node Assessment and Evidence Accumulation. 

Nodes of the frontier are assessed in order of their similarity to q. For each node vi, EAGR decides whether it is relevant to the query and extracts the evidence it contributes, conditioned on the query and the evidence accumulated so far: 

(ri, E i) = Assess( q, v i, Mt).

A node is relevant ( ri = 1 ) if it helps answer any step of the query: it states information the query asks for, or identifies an entity on the path to the answer, such as the director of a film named in the query. It need not contain the final answer. Ei is the set of facts from pi that fill a step of the query, each written as a short, self-contained sentence; facts about the same entities that would not change the answer are left out. Since the LLM may introduce information that is not in the passage, a fact is kept only if it is grounded in pi, and facts already in 

Mt are skipped (Appendix G). The evidence of relevant nodes is accumulated, 

Mt ← M t ∪ Ei if ri = 1 ,

so each subsequent node, within the same frontier or a later one, is assessed against all evidence found so far. Irrelevant nodes contribute neither evidence nor directions for exploration.

Sufficiency Check. Once all nodes of the frontier have been assessed, and if they added new evidence, EAGR checks whether Mt is sufficient to answer 

q:

st = Sufficient( q, Mt).

Rather than asking for a bare yes/no judgement, which we found prone to premature termination, the LLM writes out the chain of steps the query requires, each as an ( entity , attribute , value ) triple with the value taken from Mt, or left empty if the evidence does not state it. For example, for 

“Which team did Joe Buck’s father broadcast for?” ,the chain is (Joe Buck, father, Jack Buck) followed by (Jack Buck, team, St. Louis Cardinals). EAGR accepts st = 1 only if the LLM judges the evidence sufficient, every value is filled, and every value ap-pears in Mt, which rejects answers the LLM fills in from its own knowledge. If st = 1 , retrieval ter-minates; otherwise EAGR continues with the edges selected in this frontier. Retrieval also terminates when the number of explored passages reaches the budget B.

3.4 Retrieval Reordering 

After traversal terminates, EAGR orders the ex-plored passages using the evidence collected along the way, in three groups: (i) relevant passages whose extracted facts mention the entities or val-ues in the reasoning chain of the last Sufficiency Check, i.e., the evidence for the answer; (ii) other relevant passages that contributed facts; and (iii) all remaining passages, with those judged irrelevant last. Within each group, passages keep their order from the traversal. This ordering matters most for multi-hop queries, where passages reached after one or more hops (e.g., the biography of a film’s director) are needed to establish the answer but may share little vocab-ulary with the query. Ranked by query similarity alone, such passages would often fall outside the top-ranked results; ranking by the evidence chain instead places them first, while passages judged irrelevant are moved to the end.

5 Results and Analysis 

Generation accuracy (Q1). Table 1 shows that EAGR achieves the highest average accuracy, 67.1 LLM-Acc., 3.9 points above the strongest baseline (LinearRAG, 63.2) and 7.3 above vanilla RAG. Its main strength is consistency: the strongest graph-based baselines each excel on some benchmarks but fall below vanilla RAG on others (HippoRAG2 and LinearRAG on MuSiQue, LinearRAG also on HotpotQA), whereas EAGR improves over vanilla RAG on all three. It matches the best baseline on HotpotQA (77.4 vs. 77.9) and 2Wiki (78.1 vs. 78.2) and gives the best result on MuSiQue (45.8 vs. 44.2), the benchmark with the longest reason-ing chains. Giving the genertor the accumulated evidence instead of passages raises MuSiQue accu-racy further to 51.7, suggesting that the evidence collected during traversal is a useful condensed context for long chains. 

Retrieval (Q2). Table 4 shows the largest re-trieval gain on 2Wiki, where EAGR reaches 95.5 Recall@5 against 91.0 for HippoRAG2 and 76.7 for dense retrieval; its questions mostly follow ex-plicit entity chains (e.g., a film’s director, then the director’s birthplace), which descriptor edges fol-low directly. On HotpotQA, EAGR is on par with HippoRAG2 (95.4 vs. 95.9). On MuSiQue, its re-call equals dense retrieval and trails HippoRAG2 (71.5 vs. 76.2), yet its answer accuracy is 7.1 points higher, indicating that recall of all gold passages alone does not fully determine downstream accu-racy. Figure 3 shows how early stopping criteria in EAGR makes a significant number of queries stop at less than 5 retrieved chunks, this filtering out noisy context and providing better QA accuracy. 

Adaptive retrieval. Because traversal stops once the accumulated evidence is verified to answer the query, EAGR spends retrieval effort where it is needed (Figure 3). On HotpotQA and 2Wiki, about 81% of queries stop within 10 passages, and on Hot-potQA 71% need at most 5, i.e., the four seed pas-sages and at most one more; only about 4% reach the budget. MuSiQue, whose questions require up to four hops, shows a different profile: about 46% of queries stop within 10 passages, while 31% ex-plore the full budget. The traversal thus scales with question difficulty rather than retrieving a fixed number of passages per query, which keeps the av-erage cost low on benchmarks dominated by short reasoning chains. 

5.1 Ablation Study 

Each ablation removes one component while keep-ing the rest of the pipeline unchanged (Table 2). Two components matter consistently. Removing accumulated evidence, so that assessment, edge selection and reranking see only the query and the current passage and traversal never stops early, low-ers average accuracy by 4.3 points, and by 6.3 and 5.4 on 2Wiki and MuSiQue: conditioning on ev-idence is the main source of EAGR’s gains. Re-moving retrieval reordering costs 3.9 points (7.9 on 2Wiki), showing that passages found through multi-hop expansion need to be ranked by the evidence chain to reach the reader. Edge reranking has a small average effect (0.8) but matters on MuSiQue (3.2), where longer chains make the order of explo-ration more consequential. Relevance assessment has the smallest effect: it helps on 2Wiki (1.7) and is neutral on HotpotQA and MuSiQue. Edge se-lection is the most critical component: following every descriptor of a relevant passage instead of the selected ones lowers average accuracy by 23.3 points, below even vanilla RAG. Without selec-tion, the budget is spent within the first expansion on passages that share incidental descriptors with the seeds, so the passages of later hops are rarely reached, and the many loosely related passages ex-plored compete with the actual evidence for the top-ranked positions. 

5.2 Qualitative Analysis 

To examine how evidence guides traversal, we an-alyze a 3-hop MuSiQue query where all baselines fail (Table 3, Appendix C). While dense, graph, and agentic baselines fail to bridge intermediate entities ( Rodney Crowell → Warner Bros. Records 

→ Warner Music Group ) due to localization bias, hallucination, or looping, EAGR’s Node Assess-ment iteratively accumulates evidence across hops and successfully recovers the complete reasoning chain.

κ = 0 .854 ; Appendix D). 

6 Conclusion 

We presented EAGR, an adaptive retrieval ap-proach in which accumulated evidence, rather than a rewritten query, determines which directions to explore next. EAGR traverses an implicit graph induced from descriptors stored as ordinary Vec-torDB metadata, selecting and ranking edges by the evidence collected and stopping once a verified reasoning chain answers the query, all without ma-terializing a graph or rewriting the query. Across HotpotQA, 2WikiMultiHopQA and MuSiQue, it achieves the highest average answer accuracy, 3.9 points above the strongest graph-based baseline, and is the only graph-based method that improves over dense retrieval on all three benchmarks. Ab-lations show that conditioning on accumulated ev-idence drives these gains, supporting a view of multi-hop retrieval as an adaptive search process in which accumulated evidence and not the original query alone, determines how the retrieval frontier evolves. 7 Limitations 

All components of EAGR are currently used off-the-shelf without task-specific training. Future work could explore component-specific fine-tuning and stronger foundation models to further improve both retrieval quality and practical viability.

A Appendix 

A.1 Evidence-Guided Adaptive Graph Retrieval (EAGR) Algorithm 

Algorithm 1 provides the complete procedural de-scription of EAGR. The algorithm consists of an offline semantic graph abstraction stage and an on-line evidence-guided retrieval stage. During the of-fline stage, each passage is associated with a set of semantic descriptors Ki, which are stored as meta-data in the VectorDB. During inference, EAGR first retrieves k seed passages using DPR and initializes the retrieval queue and accumulated evidence with these seeds and an empty evidence context, respec-tively. The online retrieval procedure follows the four stages described in Section 3.3: Node Assessment, Edge Selection, Evidence-Guided Edge Reranking, and Graph Expansion. Relevant but insufficient nodes contribute evidence and selected semantic descriptors to the traversal, while irrelevant nodes are excluded from further expansion. Candidate descriptors proposed across the explored nodes are subsequently reranked using the query and accumu-lated evidence, after which at most Nmax neighbor-ing passages are retrieved through metadata lookup. Already explored nodes are excluded from subse-quent expansion. The procedure terminates when a relevant node is assessed as sufficient or when the exploration budget B is exhausted. Finally, the retrieved pas-sages are reordered using the relevance decisions made during traversal, while preserving their rela-tive retrieval order.

Step 4: Person-name normalization. Using the extracted entity types, we collect person names across the corpus and group them by last name and suffix (e.g., Jr. , II ). Within each group, a name is considered a shorter form of another if its words occur in the same order in the longer name, with initials matching words that begin with the corre-sponding letter (e.g., Clarence Brown and Clarence Leon Brown ; Robert A. Stemmle and Robert Adolf Stemmle ). First names must match, suffixes must be identical, and the shorter name’s middle part must match the first middle part of the longer name. Thus, George W. Bush is not merged with George H. W. Bush . Names containing particles or epithets (e.g., de , van , the , of ) are excluded from merg-ing. If a shorter form matches at most three full names, all matching full names are added as de-scriptors to every passage containing that shorter form. If it matches more than three, it is consid-ered ambiguous and left unlinked. Finally, single-word fragments of a person’s name (e.g., Clarence ,

Brown ) are removed from passages containing the full name to prevent spurious connections between different people.

EAGR uses three prompts, all sent to the same LLM with greedy decoding and thinking disabled. The prompts below are reproduced verbatim; only line breaks are added for layout. 

Joint Node Assessment and Edge Selection. 

One call per explored passage (Figures 9 and 10). In the prompt, Previously Retrieved Facts is the accumulated evidence Mt, Current Passage is pi

Sufficiency Check. At most one call per fron-tier (Figure 11). The prompt receives the query and the full accumulated evidence Mt and returns the reasoning chain ( required ) and a judgement (is_sufficient ), which is verified as described below. 

Evidence-Guided Edge Reranking. At most one call per frontier, only when the evidence is not yet sufficient (Figure 12). The prompt receives 

q, Mt and Kcand and returns the ordered list K∗.

Implementation details. 

• Candidate edges. The Edge Labels shown for a passage are its descriptors that have not yet been expanded. When a person’s name is selected, other spellings of the same name in that passage (e.g., robert a. stemmle and 

robert stemmle ) are expanded as well, since a neighbouring passage may use only one of them. • Parsing. Selected labels are matched to the candidates ignoring case and whitespace, and labels that are not among the candidates are discarded. If the joint prompt’s output can-not be parsed, the passage contributes no evi-dence or edges, and its relevance is treated as unknown during reordering. • Fact grounding. Since the LLM may introduce information that is not in the passage, a fact is kept only if every number in it appears in the passage and at least 60% of its content words do (words of five or more letters may match on their first five letters, so that, e.g., 

married matches marriage ). A fact is skipped if an existing fact in Mt already states it, i.e., contains its numbers and at least 85% of its content words. • Sufficiency verification. st = 1 only if the LLM answers Yes , the chain has at least one step, and every value (every item of a list value) appears in Mt: its numbers must match exactly, and either the whole value or all of its content words must occur. • Reranking fallback. Labels missing from the reranker’s output are appended in their selec-tion order; if the output cannot be parsed, the selection order is kept. • Evidence chain for reordering. A passage sup-ports the reasoning chain if one of its facts contains a chain entity or value as a phrase, or contains all its numbers and at least 75% of its content words.
