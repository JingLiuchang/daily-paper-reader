# RAGCompass: Generalizable Retriever Routing via Reranking 

Valentin Six 1, Mufei Li 2, Dongqi Fu 3, Yinglong Xia 3, Hong Li 3, Pan Li 21University of Virginia, 2Georgia Institute of Technology, 3Meta Different retrievers exist for retrieval-augmented generation, yet none performs best for all queries. Therefore, the key question is: given a query and retrieved passages from different retrievers, which one is most suited for that query? Existing routers usually make predictions from query-specific features and a fixed pool of retrievers; such routers have no output for an unseen retriever and are incapable by design on a new corpus. To fill these gaps, RAGCompass reads the retrieved passage sets themselves: passages from an unfamiliar retriever or corpus stay directly comparable to other passages, so routing stays well designed where a query-only map would fail. We contrastively fine-tune a reranker against the answer each evidence set produces, which accounts for most of the gain; a small comparison head adds a moderate improvement, and neither component has retriever-specific parameters. RAGCompass 

improves answer F1 by 28% relative to the best fixed retriever, and outperforms fusion baselines, three query-only routers, and a prompted 8B language model. Generalization experiments show that 

RAGCompass keeps most of its gain under an unseen corpus, retriever, or reader, while query-only baselines fall to the best fixed retriever or below. Code is made available at https://github.com/Graph-COM/RAGCompass .

# 1 Introduction 

In retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), a retriever first selects passages from a corpus, and a reader (e.g., a language model) then writes an answer from them. RAG is widely used because it gives a language model access to knowledge beyond its training data and makes its answers traceable to their sources (Lewis et al., 2020). A deployed RAG system usually fixes one retriever for all queries, yet sparse lexical matching, learned sparse expansion, dense embeddings, late interaction, and graph-based linking (Robertson and Zaragoza, 2009; Formal et al., 2021; Lassance et al., 2024; Karpukhin et al., 2020; Lewis et al., 2020; Santhanam et al., 2022; Gutiérrez et al., 2024, 2025) each define a different notion of relevance for retrieval, and each one wins on different types of queries. Averaged over various datasets, the retrievers we study are close in performance (0.22 to 0.28 answer F1), but an oracle that picks the right one for each query reaches 0.75 F1, almost 3× the best fixed retriever (Figure 1). The opportunity is therefore not necessarily to design a better mechanism, but rather a way of choosing among existing ones given a specific query. This is the problem of retriever routing , posed as learning to rank retrievers by Kim and Diaz (2025): given a query and several structurally different retrievers, which retriever should one prefer? What makes routing hard in practice is that a deployed system changes: a text corpus evolves, retrievers are added or replaced, the reader is upgraded... Adapting the router after each of those changes means re-running the reader over every training query to regenerate its labels, so a routing method is usable only if it holds on a corpus, mechanism pool, or reader it was not trained on. This practical necessity for routing robustness is left untouched by most prior work. Indeed, most existing routers choose a retriever from query-specific features, without looking at any retrieved evidence: query performance prediction and learned routers predict which mechanism will do well from the query’s wording, hand-designed features, or a retriever’s own confidence scores (Meng et al., 2023; Chifu et al., 2026; Tian et al., 2026; Lee et al., 2025; Kalra et al., 2025; Kim and Diaz, 2025; Zhao et al., 2026). We instead let the decision depend on the evidence coming from each retriever. Whichever mechanism or corpus produces the evidence, retrieved passages stay comparable to one another. Deciding which passages best answer the query should therefore be less sensitive to shifts in the query distribution, the retrievers, or the corpus. One disadvantage is that it requires running every candidate retriever first. We argue that the cost of retrieval is small, as the retrievals can run concurrently, and the decision adds about 0.2s to a 

5 − 6s per query pipeline. To that end, we pose routing as selection among evidence sets , which fixes two design choices: the scorer reads the passages themselves, so a reranker is the natural building block; and it is trained on the answer the evidence yields rather than on document relevance, since the two often disagree and relevance-based training leads to lower answer quality (Section 5). Our method, RAGCompass , fine-tunes a reranker (Yang et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025) so that its score for a mechanism’s passages tracks the answer they produce, and adds a small comparison head that reads the reranker’s representations of all candidates jointly. The fine-tuned reranker accounts for most of the accuracy gain; the head adds 1.4pp on top. Neither the reranker nor the head holds retriever-specific parameters, so the router applies to a retriever it was not trained on. Trained on four question-answering datasets and routing among five retrievers, RAGCompass achieves a 28% 

relative improvement over the best fixed retriever and beats a weighted mixture of retrievers, the strongest baseline we run. It also improves retrieval quality over the best fixed retriever ( +5 .0 points of recall@5, +1 .3

of nDCG@5). 

RAGCompass also generalizes by design: held out from an entire dataset and its corpus, RAGCompass 

retains more than 80% of its in-distribution gain on some datasets (Figure 4).

# 2 Related Work 

Methods for choosing among retrievers differ in what they condition on. We organize prior work around those differences; Table 1 summarizes. 

Routing that never reads the evidence Reciprocal Rank Fusion (Cormack et al., 2009) sums a rank-based score across systems without training: a nearly free baseline that is unable to identify the best retriever per query. MoR (Kalra et al., 2025) fuses heterogeneous retrievers through a weighted sum of their scores, with per-query, per-retriever weights computed zero-shot from the geometry of each retriever’s embedding space; the static-weight variant we run as a baseline (Appendix I) is query-independent. Query performance prediction conditions on the query, predicting a retriever’s effectiveness from query features or retrieval-score statistics (Meng et al., 2023); Chifu et al. (2026) find its accuracy highly collection-dependent across the sparse, hybrid and dense families we route over, with marginal downstream gains, and Tian et al. (2026) extend it to RAG with QPP-style, reader-perplexity and document-quality features. Among learned routers, LTRR (Kim and Diaz, 2025) learns to rank retrievers against downstream utility gain as we do, but reads hand-engineered query and score features rather than the passages. ROUTERRETRIEVER (Lee et al., 2025) routes over domain-specialized experts of a single architecture by training-free nearest-centroid similarity between the query and each expert’s own embeddings, whereas we route across structurally different paradigms. All of these methods decide from signals other than the retrieved set whose quality is at issue. 

Utility-supervised routing without evidence R3AG (Zhao et al., 2026) is closest to our method in spirit: an LLM’s assessment of retrieved documents decides which retrievers count as training positives, but the router never reads those documents. Each retriever is a single learned identity token fused with the query through attention. The routing score, therefore, depends on the query and a fixed per-retriever profile, not on the retrieved evidence. A retriever added after training will get a token, so the method stays defined, but that new token never receives a gradient, and in our reimplementation the router does not select an unseen retriever more often where it is the right choice (Section 5.3). What separates it from our formulation is what is read at inference; we reimplement R 3AG, with the details listed in Appendix I, and test it under both shifts (Section 5). To the best of our knowledge, none of these formulations have been tested under a shift. Rank fusion and MoR learn no mapping from queries to retrievers, so a corpus or mechanism change cannot break one. QPP and the learned routers do build one, but stays undefined for a retriever it never saw and untested on a new corpus. How routing behaves in such new conditions is the question that makes the paper’s central test.

This is the reranking problem announced in Section 1: the candidates to be ranked are the evidence sets D1, . . . , D R, and the ranking criterion is yi.Two properties of Equation 1 guide our method design. First, content grounding : yi is a property of Di, so a router that never reads Di predicts yi from a proxy. Second, utility supervision : yi is defined through the reader, not through retrieval relevance, since a passage set can be topically relevant and still yield a wrong answer. The two diverge in practice: nDCG against gold passages and answer F1 select different retrievers on roughly 43% of queries, so a router trained on relevance optimizes toward the wrong retriever on close to half the queries. Per-retriever encoding and scoring We encode each retriever’s passages in its own forward pass through a shared backbone, Qwen3-Reranker-0.6B (Yang et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025), and read a scalar score from the logit of the token yes under a routing-oriented instruction:

Similar to the scorer, the head has no parameters tied to particular retrievers, so the full router stays valid for a candidate pool it was not trained on. A variant that adds a learned per-retriever identity embedding to hi loses this property (Section 5). The head costs under 1% of the routing decision’s time; Section 5.1 reports its contribution to accuracy. We consider one pooled vector per retriever or one hidden state per passage for hi.

Training the head We first fine-tune the backbone alone against Equation 3, then train the head on its frozen representations. This two-stage recipe matches joint training of head and backbone at three seeds (Section 5), and it is the configuration behind every generalization result. 

# 4 Experimental Setup 

Datasets and retrievers We jointly train and evaluate on four single-hop and multi-hop QA datasets: Natural Questions (Kwiatkowski et al., 2019), MuSiQue (Trivedi et al., 2022), HotpotQA (Yang et al., 2018), and 2WikiMultihopQA (Ho et al., 2020). Each query is routed among R=5 retrievers from deliberately different paradigms, which is what makes their confidence scores incommensurable and distinguishes our setting from routing among same-architecture experts (Lee et al., 2025): a dense retriever (Karpukhin et al., 2020; Lewis et al., 2020), BM25 (Robertson and Zaragoza, 2009), SPLADE-v3 (Formal et al., 2021; Lassance et al., 2024), ColBERTv2 (Santhanam et al., 2022), and HippoRAG2 (Gutiérrez et al., 2024, 2025). Each supplies k=5 

passages to a fixed Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct Grattafiori et al. (2024) reader. Queries on which all retrievers reach near-identical utility give a nearly uniform target u, carrying no routing signal but adding gradient noise. 

Metric and splits We report test-set answer F1 from the selected retriever’s passages; this corresponds to the utility of Equation 1, consistent with the RPP/GPP framing of Tian et al. (2026). We additionally report the recall@5 and nDCG@5 scores of the routed choice. We use a 70/30 train/validation resplit. We train and evaluate only on queries with a top-two utility gap ≥ 0.05 . We average the main result, both ablations, and generalization protocols over three seeds.

. Non-learned combinations are RRF (Cormack et al., 2009) (unsupervised rank fusion, k=60 )and a MoR -style mixture (Kalra et al., 2025) (static retriever weights fit on validation utility). Learned routers condition on the query but never read passages when routing; all three are our reimplementations, adapted to our retriever pool and compute budget, and Appendix I lists how each differs from its original: the LTRR-style ranker (Kim and Diaz, 2025) (query and retrieval-confidence features, trained on our utility targets, model family chosen on validation F1), R.RETRIEVER (Lee et al., 2025) (training-free nearest-centroid routing on training queries where each retriever’s F1 is highest), and R3AG (Zhao et al., 2026) (two contrastively trained Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B encoders with one learned identity token per retriever). Two answer-level baselines complete the set: majority vote over the R generated answers, and LLM-as-router , where Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct picks zero-shot among the R evidence sets or answers. 

Generalization protocols We test three shifts, each changing one component of the pipeline while the rest stays fixed. Unseen corpus : leave-one-dataset-out trains on three datasets and evaluates on the fourth, whose queries and passages are never seen in training. This gives one fold per dataset; the control trains on all four and is evaluated on the same rows, so the difference between the two isolates the cost of the shift. 5Unseen mechanism : leave-one-retriever-out removes one retriever from the training pool, together with its utility labels, but keeps it among the candidates at test time. This gives one fold per retriever; the control trains with all five. In both protocols the backbone and the head are retrained for each fold, so neither component has seen the hidden dataset or retriever, and the compared baselines are run under the identical protocol. Unseen reader : the router, trained on utility labels from Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct Grattafiori et al. (2024), and its evidence are left unchanged, and Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct Qwen et al. (2025) answers in place of Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct. These three shifts mirror how a deployed RAG system changes over time: the corpus evolves, retrievers are added or replaced, and the reader is upgraded. Since collecting utility labels requires the reader to answer from every candidate’s evidence, retraining the router after each such change is costly, and a router is only practical if its gains survive them.

Unseen mechanism Our identity-free head costs 0.16 –1.17 pp when a retriever is held out of training (Table 4) and still selects that retriever 14 –26% of the time, and on 20 –40% of the queries where it is the oracle choice. The backbone and the head are both retrained without the held-out retriever, so no part of the pipeline has seen its passages or labels. Naturally, adding a learned identity embedding makes the head degenerate on the retriever it never saw during training: it almost never picks BM25 or RAG ( 0.1–0.2% ), nearly always picks ColBERTv2 or SPLADE-v3 ( 75 –99% ), and its ColBERTv2 fold falls to 0.2706 , below the best fixed retriever (0.2782 ). An R-way classifier cannot express this protocol, since it never predicts a class absent from training, so we re-pose the LTRR-style ranker as a shared scorer over (query, retriever) pairs with no per-retriever parameters: it gains nothing in distribution ( −0.51 ± 0.80 pp) and is below the best fixed retriever on all five folds; with a retriever-identity feature it is level in distribution and below on every fold. R 3AG, whose token for a new retriever is defined but untrained, is also below on every fold. None of these baselines discriminates between queries (Figure 5): their pick rates for the held-out retriever range from 0% to 61% across folds and stay nearly unchanged on the queries where it is the oracle choice, whereas our head picks it more often where it is right. 

Unseen reader Leaving the router and its evidence untouched and re-answering with a new reader (Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct in place of Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct), the router retains 71% ± 2% of its advantage; the best fixed retriever’s identity changes with the reader (SPLADE-v3 to HippoRAG2), so this is a different reference point as well as a different reader. A fourth shift points the same way: evaluated at retrieval depth k=3 rather than 

k=5 (training was done at that k = 5 setting) with no retraining, the router still beats that depth’s best fixed retriever by +3 .64 pp and captures a comparable share of a smaller headroom (Appendix G). 

# 6 Discussion 

Our experiments show that routing signals matter more than score coupling: utility supervision beats relevance supervision by 2.11 pp, while coupling the candidates’ scores through joint normalization moves the result by less than seed noise (Section 5.1). This sharpens our disagreement with R 3AG (Zhao et al., 2026): the substantive difference is its representation of each retriever as a fixed identity token, where we use the evidence retrieved for the particular query; its per-retriever scoring is not the issue. Comparison design also matters less for accuracy than expected: comparing representations rather than scalars adds little (Section 5.1), and reading all retrievers in one context underperforms per-retriever encoding (Appendix A). Reading the evidence is also what allows generalization: a passage set stays comparable to its rivals whatever produced it, whereas a map from query-side features or retriever identity has no output for a mechanism it was not fitted to. Consistent with this, none of the tested query-only routers exceeds the best fixed retriever under either shift, and our own head degrades once it is given a per-retriever identity embedding (Section 5.3). The query-only routers do not exceed the best fixed retriever in distribution either (Section 5.1), so the 9comparison supports the claim that a query-only signal is weak in our pooled setting more than a claim about those systems as originally published. A large oracle gap remains ( 0.7508 against 0.3552 ). Section 5.2 shows: the router matches the oracle on about a third of the queries, and most of its misses still beat the best fixed retriever. 

# 7 Conclusion 

In this work we study retriever routing: given a query and the passage sets returned by several retrievers, deciding which set the reader should see. We pose this retriever-routing problem as a content-grounded comparison supervised by downstream utility, rather than as predicting each retriever’s quality from other signals. RAGCompass uses a fine-tuned reranker as the scorer and a comparison head, neither with parameters tied to any retriever. RAGCompass outperforms every router we compare against on answer F1, at 3.9% of the cost of the generation pipeline; most of that gain comes from the utility-supervised reranker, and the head adds a small further improvement. More importantly, RAGCompass keeps most of its advantage on a corpus, a retriever, or a reader it never saw in training, while the query-only routers do not exceed the best fixed retriever. Narrowing the remaining gap to the oracle is left for future work. 

# Limitations 

Utility (Equation 1) is defined through one reader (Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct) and one retrieval depth ( k=5 ,fixed at retrieval time), and the unseen-reader protocol tests exactly one alternative (Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct), so we can neither separate evidence quality from evidence–reader fit nor claim the reader result holds beyond that single swap. Depth is tested in one direction only: the stored retrievals are five deep and each row’s utility is the answer the reader gave from a five-passage context, so going deeper would mean re-running retrieval for every retriever, dataset and split and regenerating every label – how routing behaves as retrieval 

deepens is untested. Every result uses one backbone scale (Qwen3-Reranker-0.6B), so whether the properties of Section 3.1 hold at a different parameter count is unmeasured. The three learned query-only baselines (LTRR, R 3AG (Zhao et al., 2026) and ROUTERRETRIEVER (Lee et al., 2025)) are our reimplementations, and they may differ from the original implementation by the authors; Appendix I lists the differences. Our MoR baseline is likewise a simplified variant: it uses one validation-fitted weight per retriever in place of the original’s per-query weights. The 3.9% inference overhead covers the routing decision only, on one device (L40S) at one batch size: routing also requires running all R retrievers rather than one, a real cost that is parallelizable but not included in that figure, which should be read as an order of magnitude rather than a portable constant.

(+1 .4 over the scalar scorer alone), so a joint, non-linear comparison does recover signal that the linear probe misses. We swept roughly twenty further configurations against the same frozen representations (listed in Appendix C), and none exceeded ∼0.353. The only change that moved the number at all was training the head jointly with the backbone (Table 7), and even that tops out at 0.3623 , inside the range we would call noise if it came from a seed change rather than an architecture change. Given Table 6, this is the expected 14 outcome: most of the routing signal is already in the score, so a more elaborate comparison has little more to find. 

An expected effect of scalar supervision The pattern in Table 6 is what one should expect from supervising a single scalar per retriever: the objective asks for a score, and the model is optimized for that score. It is a property of how the scorer is trained and not a defect of it. The scalar scorer is already content-grounded and utility-supervised, so its large share of the gain (Section 5.1) is evidence for the formulation.

# I Baseline Implementation Details 

All three learned query-only baselines are our reimplementations, following experimental details in the corresponding papers. They run on the same filtered population, splits and seeds as our router. Each differs from its original system, because our retriever pool, reader and compute budget differ from the ones the methods were designed for. Table 9 summarizes the main differences. 

R3AG We follow Zhao et al. (2026) wherever the paper specifies a choice: a single trainable identity token per retriever, never a description or its outputs (their Appendix B); two Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B encoders with only the word embeddings and the last two layers trainable, learning rate 2 × 10 −6, 10 epochs (their A.5); a top-2 multi-positive contrastive loss with τ =0 .1; utility EM + 0 .2 F1 + 0 .2 σ(R), with σ the retriever’s mean training EM; attention fusion of each candidate’s two capability vectors conditioned on the query; cosine routing; binary cross-entropy on the sigmoid of that cosine plus a squared-ℓ2 regularizer. We depart from it in five places. (i) The pool excludes their null retriever, so R 3AG is scored on exactly the population of Table 2: adding an action changes which queries pass the utility-gap filter. (ii) The retrieval-quality judge is Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct rather than an 80 B model, using their five-dimension rubric; its averaged integer scores tie at the top-2 boundary on about 64% of training queries, and we break ties at random. (iii) We train with batch size 16 rather than an effective 512 , which on our 5,009 training queries would allow about 100 

optimizer steps in total. (iv) The fusion stage’s learning rate ( 10 −4), epochs ( 10 ) and regularizer weight are not given; we select the stage-1 learning rate, regularizer weight and γ from a five-point grid on validation F1, which returns the paper’s learning rate and γ with a regularizer weight of 0.1, and pick the fusion epoch on validation. (v) Our reader’s answers are long, so EM is 4–10% against roughly 36% in their setting; the EM term of the utility is mostly zero and the per-retriever prior σ often decides the second positive. On validation, no configuration in the grid beats the best fixed retriever; the one selected routes nearly every query to it. 

LTRR-style ranker We keep LTRR’s formulation, a learned ranker over retrievers supervised by downstream utility and fed query and retrieval-confidence features. The original trains pairwise XGBoost on normalized utility gain, using a PCA-reduced query embedding, query length and type, and six embedding-similarity 18 features of the retrieved documents (e.g., MaxSim, VarSim, Moran). The five-way classifier and the shared per-retriever scorer each fit several model families (logistic regression, random forest and gradient boosting, plus two regression targets for the shared scorer); the family is chosen per run on validation F1 and refit on training plus validation for the test number. Its in-distribution result depends on the gradient-boosting fit more than on the formulation: with the model family fixed to gradient boosting and seed 42 , the same code and data give 0.2903 under scikit-learn 1.6 and 1.7 but 0.2782 and 0.2785 under 1.5 and 1.9, while logistic regression is identical across versions. We report three seeds under a single library version (1.9) with validation selection. This sensitivity, up to 1.2pp, is larger than the ranker’s margin over the best fixed retriever. 

R.RETRIEVER Lee et al. (2025)’s routing has no learned parameters: each expert’s “pilot embedding” is the centroid of a shared query encoder’s embeddings for that expert’s own training queries, and a query routes to the nearest centroid by cosine similarity. Their experts are domain-adapted instances of one embedding architecture, so “that expert’s own training queries” is domain membership, independent of any other expert. We route across five structurally different retrievers instead, with no such shared notion of a retriever’s “own” queries, so we substitute the closest available analogue: with Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B as the shared encoder, retriever i’s centroid is built from the training queries where retriever i’s own F1 is highest – a per-retriever, non-comparative signal, unlike the argmax label every other baseline in this paper uses. The one hyperparameter, the fraction of training queries contributing to each centroid, is chosen per run from {0.1, 0.2, 0.35 , 0.5, 1.0} on validation F1 ( 1.0 is a naive full-mean centroid, ignoring F1 entirely). Being training-free, it has nothing to overfit to the training corpus, but that is not the same as being accurate: it is below the best fixed retriever in distribution ( −1.74 pp) and on every leave-one-dataset-out fold ( −2.07 to 

−8.01 pp, worst on MuSiQue). We do not report a leave-one-retriever-out number: retriever i’s centroid needs retriever i’s own F1 outcomes to be built at all, comparative or not, which is exactly what that protocol withholds, so its score for a held-out retriever cannot be constructed without reusing information the protocol is meant to deny it, the same wall the plain LTRR classifier hits. 

Fusion baselines RRF uses the standard constant k=60 (Cormack et al., 2009) and no training. The original MoR (Kalra et al., 2025) is zero-shot: it weights each retriever per query using embedding-geometry signals (distance to corpus cluster centroids, Moran coefficient) with fixed mixing coefficients, and also fuses over several index granularities. Ours instead fits one weight per retriever on validation utility and applies it to every query, with no per-query signals or multi-granularity indices. 

Implications for the comparison The differences above go in one direction: our versions use smaller models and less compute than the originals, and two of the methods are applied to a pool of structurally different retrievers that they were not designed for. We therefore expect our numbers to understate what the original systems achieve in their own settings, and they should not be compared with the numbers in the corresponding papers. Our MoR variant differs in kind and not in scale: it is not zero-shot and does not adapt to the query. R3AG’s near-collapse onto a single retriever (Section 5.1) may partly reflect these conditions, in particular the weaker judge and the near-zero exact-match term, and not only the method.

# K An LLM as Router, and the Cost of Routing 

An LLM as router Prompting Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct (roughly 13 × our router’s parameters) with the query and all five evidence sets and asking it to choose zero-shot gains 0.20 pp over the best fixed retriever; asked instead to choose among the five already-generated answers, it gains 3.20 pp. Our 0.6B router, fine-tuned against downstream utility on the same queries, gains 7.70 pp. The evidence-judging variant is also strongly position-biased, picking whichever evidence set comes first on 51 .8% of queries against a 20% unbiased rate, and it costs more: 0.358 s/query against 0.222 s for our router, which generates nothing. We attribute the gap to the training signal rather than to model capacity, and it is not bought with compute. 

Cost Reading the evidence means running all R retrievers before deciding, which a single-retriever system does not pay. That cost is small compared to the cost of the reader: the decision itself takes 0.222 s/query (corresponding to five 0.6B forward passes) against 5.687 s for one retriever’s full retrieve-and-answer pipeline, an overhead of 3.9% , and the R retrievals are independent and parallelizable. Generation, the expensive step, happens once either way. We estimate the reported experiments at roughly 700 GPU-hours on NVIDIA L40S GPUs: about 475 for running the five retrievers and the reader over all 60 ,000 queries to obtain utility labels, and about 200 for router training, generalization folds, ablations and baselines; index construction and preliminary experiments are excluded. 20
