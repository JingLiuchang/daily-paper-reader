## Abstract 

Disk-resident approximate nearest-neighbor search (ANNS) scales vector search beyond DRAM, yet state-of-the-art sys-tems remain I/O-bound, even with fast SSDs and sophisti-cated I/O execution. To understand this bottleneck, we con-duct an I/O-centric analysis that breaks each query down into an approach phase that locates a query-near region and a 

converge phase that resolves the final top-k. This analysis reveals complementary I/O tradeoffs: cluster-based search circumvents approach-phase I/O through in-memory routing but reads coarse-grained, full clusters during convergence, whereas graph-based search employs fine-grained, vector-level I/O throughout, enabling efficient convergence at the cost of inevitable I/O during the approach phase. To combine the complementary I/O benefits of the two granularities, we present TriVS , a new paradigm that re-architects disk-resident vector search as a trilogy: approach coarsely, distill progressively, and converge exactly . Its key in-novations come in two parts. First, it decouples the approach and converge phases from a single search granularity, em-ploying cluster-based search to avoid approach-phase I/O and leveraging vector-level I/O for efficient convergence. Second, to bridge the two granularities, it introduces a new in-memory 

distill phase between the original two, progressively reduc-ing routed clusters to a vector-level shortlist using compact quantized vectors before disk access. Moreover, coarser clus-tering shrinks the centroid-routing index, allowing TriVS to reallocate existing memory to distillation rather than expand its memory budget. Our evaluation on five datasets spanning diverse vector types and scales shows that, at Recall@10=0.9, TriVS achieves on average 3 .42 × PipeANN’s throughput, 51.2% lower latency, and 63.7% less disk I/O, while using less memory and storage than PipeANN. 

## 1 Introduction 

Modern search and retrieval systems represent images, text, and other objects as high-dimensional vectors. Because exact nearest-neighbor search is prohibitively expensive over large high-dimensional collections, approximate nearest-neighbor search (ANNS) trades a small loss in recall for substantially less work [26]. As datasets grow to billions of vectors, disk-resident ANNS has become a de facto standard for cost-efficient vector search: it offloads full-precision vectors and their large indices (each often requiring hundreds of giga-bytes [10]) to SSDs while retaining only compact search state in DRAM. Based on how they organize and access the index and disk-resident data, existing disk-resident ANNS systems generally fall into two categories: 1) cluster-based ANNS that manages vectors at a coarse-grained cluster granularity (with SPANN [9] as a state-of-the-art baseline) and 2) graph-based ANNS that organizes vectors into a proximity graph at vector granularity (exemplified by the representative DiskANN [28] and PipeANN [20]). While both (cluster-based and graph-based) search paradigms have seen widespread adoption and extensive opti-mization, we nevertheless observe that even with faster SSDs, they remain inherently I/O-bound, especially under high con-currency. Specifically, our measurements on a high-end PCIe 5.0 NVMe SSD show that SPANN, DiskANN, and PipeANN stop scaling as query concurrency increases: they all approach the SSD’s I/O capacity, causing exposed I/O wait to account for 87–93% of total query latency at high concurrency. This bottleneck persists even for PipeANN despite its optimized pipelined execution. These results identify I/O volume as the primary bottleneck to scalability, establishing a key de-sign principle that scalable disk-resident ANNS systems must pursue: minimize per-query disk I/O .To identify what drives this per-query I/O volume, we break each query down into an approach phase , which locates a re-gion likely to contain the nearest neighbors, and a converge phase , which examines candidates within that region to deter-mine the final top-k. Our analysis and experiments reveal that 

fine and coarse search granularities provide complementary I/O benefits across the two phases . Specifically, cluster-based search avoids approach-phase I/O through in-memory cen-troid routing, but it reads coarse-grained, full clusters during 1arXiv:2611.29948v1 [cs.DB] 23 Nov 2026 convergence, incurring excessive I/O volume. By contrast, graph-based search performs fine-grained, vector-level I/O across both phases: this granular retrieval effectively limits the I/O volume during the convergence phase, but it incurs unavoidable I/O overhead during the approach phase. Aiming at combining the identified, complementary I/O benefits of the two granularities,we present TriVS , a new paradigm that re-architects disk-resident vector search as a 

tri logy: approach coarsely, distill progressively, and con-verge exactly . Specifically, TriVS adopts cluster granular-ity to avoid approach-phase I/O and employs vector gran-ularity for efficient convergence. Meanwhile, to bridge the huge gap between the two granularities, TriVS introduces an in-memory distill phase that progressively reduces routed clusters to a vector-level shortlist using compact quantized vectors before disk access. This three-phase workflow de-fines a third path beyond the existing fixed-granularity graph-based or cluster-based approaches: it adaptively leverages progressively-shrinking granularities as the search advances and the query space narrows, establishing a novel search-aware methodology to fundamentally minimize per-query disk I/O. Making this design practical requires balancing I/O re-duction with in-memory compute overhead. To meet this requirement, we develop TriSearch, a vector search algorithm that progressively increases quantization precision as the can-didate set shrinks. It combines the progressive refinement with early termination and lower-bound pruning to limit in-memory computation and exact-vector I/O. In addition, TriVS reallocates rather than expands memory. By enabling coarser clustering, in-memory distillation reduces the number of cen-troids and shrinks the routing index, freeing DRAM for the quantized vectors needed by the distill phase. We comprehensively evaluate TriVS across datasets span-ning diverse vector types and scales. Across five datasets at Recall@10=0.9, TriVS achieves, on average, 3 .42 × Pi-peANN’s throughput, 51.2% lower latency, and 63.7% less disk I/O, while using less memory and storage than PipeANN. This paper makes three contributions: • We present an I/O-centric analysis that identifies disk I/O as the scalability bottleneck in existing disk-resident ANNS and reveals how fine and coarse search granularities shift I/O between the approach and converge phases (§3). • We design TriVS as a trilogy that decouples search gran-ularity through in-memory distillation, and develop the TriSearch algorithm and an efficient implementation to re-alize this design (§4). • We comprehensively evaluate TriVS across diverse work-loads and scales, showing that it achieves higher throughput and lower latency than state-of-the-art disk-resident ANNS systems by reducing disk I/O (§5). 

## 2 Background 2.1 Disk-Resident ANNS 

Given a vector dataset X and a query vector q, vector search retrieves the k vectors in X closest to q under a chosen dis-tance metric. Because exact search is prohibitively expensive on high-dimensional datasets [26], modern vector search sys-tems [11, 24, 36] rely on approximate nearest-neighbor search (ANNS) to significantly reduce search cost at the expense of a small, acceptable accuracy loss. ANNS accuracy is typically measured by Recall@ k, the fraction of true top-k neighbors retrieved. As datasets grow to hundreds of millions or billions of high-dimensional vectors, the full-precision vectors alone can occupy hundreds of gigabytes, and the index can require comparable or greater capacity [10,28,60]. Large-scale vector search therefore commonly uses disk-resident ANNS, storing the bulk of the vectors and index on SSD while retaining compact search state in DRAM [9, 20, 25, 28, 48]. Existing disk-resident ANNS systems can be classified by the granularity at which they organize the index and disk-resident data. Cluster-based systems such as SPANN [9] use coarse-grained cluster granularity, whereas graph-based sys-tems such as DiskANN [28] and PipeANN [20] use fine-grained vector granularity. The chosen granularity shapes index size, data placement, query workflow, and disk access, as Figure 1 summarizes. 

Cluster-based Disk-Resident ANNS. Cluster-based systems such as SPANN [9] organize vectors at cluster granularity: nearby vectors are assigned to a cluster, stored together on disk, and represented by its centroid (Figure 1(a)). Because one centroid represents many vectors, the routing index con-tains far fewer entries than a per-vector graph and can remain in memory. At query time, the system searches the in-memory index for the centroids closest to the query, then reads the cor-responding clusters from disk in a batch and computes the exact distance to each retrieved vector. This coarser organi-zation eliminates navigation I/O and enables batched reads, but fetching at cluster granularity can amplify disk I/O by transferring every vector in each selected cluster. To maintain recall while limiting cluster probes, SPANN [9] replicates boundary vectors across neighboring clusters, trading storage overhead for lower per-query I/O. 

Graph-based Disk-Resident ANNS. Graph-based systems such as DiskANN [28] and PipeANN [20] organize vectors into a proximity graph at vector granularity: each vertex repre-sents a vector, and edges connect nearby vectors (Figure 1(b)). Because each vector links to multiple neighbors, the graph edges can collectively rival the raw vector data in size [10,60]. These systems commonly use best-first search [3, 49], which repeatedly expands the closest unvisited vector in a candidate list. The state-of-the-art PipeANN [20] pipelines reads across search steps and adapts I/O parallelism to overlap I/O with computation, but retains the same per-vector disk layout and access pattern. 

## 2.2 Vector Quantization 

Vector quantization maps a high-dimensional vector to a com-pact code that approximates its distance to a query. Common methods include scalar quantization (SQ), product quantiza-tion (PQ) [32], OPQ [19], LVQ [1], and RaBitQ [16, 18]. For high-dimensional vectors, SAQ [37] segments PCA-projected vectors and allocates more bits to high-impact dimensions, while DADE [13], ADSampling [17], and LeanVec [42] re-duce or adapt the dimensions used for distance evaluation. Across these methods, different code widths, quantizer config-urations, and dimension subsets provide different trade-offs among accuracy, memory, and computation. Disk-resident graph systems such as DiskANN [28] and PipeANN [20] keep PQ codes in memory to guide vector-level navigation. After reading an expanded vector’s neighbor list from disk, the system uses quantized codes to estimate each neighbor’s distance to the query and updates the ordered candidate list which it then uses to select the next graph records. This in-memory distance estimation avoids fetching every neighbor’s full-precision vector from disk. Among these methods, RaBitQ [16, 18] is a prominent state-of-the-art quantizer that provides multiple precision lev-els and theoretical error bounds. Given a reference center c,typically a cluster centroid, RaBitQ normalizes the residuals of the data vector and query, applies a shared random rotation, and represents each residual dimension with one or more bits. A one-bit code provides an initial estimate, while additional bit planes progressively refine it and reuse the earlier compu-tation. During index construction, RaBitQ stores each vector’s code together with a few precomputed terms derived from that vector and its center. At precision level ℓ, search evaluates the code against the transformed query residual and combines the result with these terms to obtain an estimated distance Dℓ. Ra-BitQ also uses a stored error term and the query residual norm to derive an error width Eℓ, which bounds the exact distance within [Dℓ − Eℓ, Dℓ + Eℓ] at the specified confidence level. We denote the corresponding lower bound as LB ℓ = Dℓ − Eℓ.

## 3 Observation and Motivation: An I/O-Centric View of Disk-Resident ANNS 

In this section, we take an I/O-centric view of disk-resident ANNS. We first show that existing systems scale poorly as query concurrency increases, even on a high-end PCIe 5.0 NVMe SSD, and identify disk I/O as their shared bottleneck. To understand where this disk I/O arises, we break down the vector search process into approach and converge phases and examine the granularity at which each phase operates. This analysis reveals that existing systems apply one granularity throughout the vector search process, thereby determining the amount and distribution of disk I/O across its phases.

## 3.2 Different Search Granularities Determine Disk I/O 

Having established disk I/O as the shared bottleneck, we next examine how it arises during vector search. Following prior analyses of vector-index traversal [20, 58, 60], we break each query down into two analytical phases: the approach phase 

and the converge phase . The approach phase moves the search from its entry point into a region likely to contain the query’s nearest neighbors. The converge phase examines candidates within that region to determine the final top-k. We apply this analytical lens to cluster- and graph-based vector search and study the disk I/O incurred in each phase. Our analysis and experiments reveal a common source of excessive I/O: existing architectures couple both phases to a single search granularity, optimizing one phase at the expense of the other. This mismatch arises because the two phases favor different granularities: cluster-level routing keeps the index compact enough to reside in memory, eliminat-ing approach-phase I/O, whereas vector-level access restricts converge-phase I/O to selected vectors. 

Cluster-based disk-resident ANNS eliminates approach-phase I/O at the cost of amplified converge-phase I/O. 

SPANN [9] applies cluster granularity to both phases, as Fig-ure 4(a) illustrates. During the approach phase, its compact centroid index selects query-near clusters entirely in DRAM, avoiding routing I/O. However, the converge phase fetches each selected cluster in full, although only a small fraction of its vectors can contribute to the final result. ## 4.1 Decoupling Search Granularity with In-Memory Distillation 

Decoupling search granularity could capture the I/O benefits of both existing paradigms: a cluster-level approach phase avoids vector-level graph-traversal I/O, while a vector-level converge phase preserves selective reads rather than fetching full clusters. However, these granularities cannot be composed directly: the cluster-level approach phase identifies promising clusters, while the vector-level converge phase must select a small subset of the vectors within them. Without an in-memory mechanism to select this subset, the system must read and scan the selected clusters in full, recreating cluster-level convergence and its coarse-grained I/O. We observe that compact quantized vectors, which can be kept in memory, provide the missing bridge between cluster-level approach and vector-level convergence. Even within query-near clusters, most vectors remain sufficiently far from the query to be pruned using approximate distance estimates computed from these in-memory representations. After clus-ter routing, TriVS can therefore scan the quantized vectors entirely in memory, discard unlikely candidates, and retain only a vector-level shortlist for the converge phase. Distillation reallocates rather than expands memory. Anatural concern is whether using in-memory quantized vectors for distillation increases memory usage. It does not: TriVS reallocates existing memory to support distillation rather than expanding its total memory footprint. On the one hand, com-pared with graph-based systems, distillation adds no new form of per-vector evidence: these systems already keep compact quantized vectors in memory to guide search. PipeANN [20] pairs these codes with a sampled entry graph, while TriVS pairs them with a centroid index of comparable size. On the other hand, compared with cluster-based SPANN [9], TriVS can scan more and larger clusters because evaluating their in-memory quantized codes does not incur additional full-cluster disk reads. This flexibility elegantly enables coarser cluster-ing and removes the need for boundary replication. Coarser clustering produces fewer clusters and therefore fewer cen-troids, shrinking the centroid-routing index and making room for one compact code per vector. Assigning each vector to one cluster further prevents those codes from being replicated. Together, these reallocations let distillation change the search workflow without increasing memory usage. Our evaluation in §5.2 confirms that TriVS uses less memory than PipeANN at both 100M and 1B scales. 

## 4.2 Index Organization 

With in-memory distillation providing the missing cluster-to-vector interface, TriVS separates its serving state by phase: memory-resident structures support the approach and distill phases, while full-precision vectors remain on disk until dis-tillation finalizes the shortlist. Figure 7 shows how offline construction produces this layout and how queries use it at serving time. 

Offline index construction. Given a vector dataset, TriVS first applies size-bounded hierarchical k-means with a tar-get cluster size T (128 vectors in our implementation), as shown at the top of Figure 7. Specifically, it recursively ap-plies k-means to any cluster containing more than 2 T vectors. TriVS deliberately adopts much coarser clustering: its cluster count is only about 0.7–0.8% of the vector count, compared with roughly 10–12% for SPANN [9]. This coarser clustering is both practical and beneficial: in-memory distillation can scan larger clusters without full-cluster disk reads, while the smaller centroid index leaves more memory for quantized vectors. Notably, in TriVS, each vector belongs to exactly one cluster, avoiding boundary replication and thereby reducing memory and storage overhead while simplifying construc-tion. For datasets that exceed available construction memory, TriVS processes the hierarchy one subtree at a time. After clustering, TriVS builds an in-memory routing index (e.g., HNSW [38]) over the final cluster centroids. Within each cluster, it quantizes vectors relative to the cluster cen-troid using RaBitQ [18]. The per-cluster reference reduces residual spread and improves distance estimation during the distill phase. Finally, TriVS stores the quantized vectors con-tiguously by cluster in memory and packs the full-precision vectors in the same cluster order on disk. 

Serving-time data layout. At serving time, TriVS keeps the centroid index, vector IDs, exact-vector locations, and RaBitQ codes in memory, with per-vector state laid out contiguously by cluster. The cluster-contiguous RaBitQ representation sup-ports incremental refinement while occupying only two bits per dimension. Consequently, the approach and distill phases access only DRAM; disk reads begin only after distillation fi-nalizes the shortlist and resolves each candidate’s exact-vector location.

Figure 7 provides an overview of this three-phase workflow; Algorithm 1 details its complete execution. 

Cluster-level approach phase. Given a query q, TriSearch first searches the DRAM-resident centroid index using stan-dard HNSW [38] to locate query-near clusters (Algorithm 1, Line 2). The search returns an ordered set C of at most P clus-ters, where P is the cluster-probe budget, and uses a search width of e f = 1.2P. These routed clusters become the input to in-memory distillation. At this point, the query has accessed only centroids; no disk-resident vector has been read. 

Progressive in-memory distillation. After the approach phase, the routed clusters still contain a broad set of vec-tors. Granularity decoupling shifts cluster-to-vector candidate selection from disk reads to quantized-distance evaluation over the routed vectors in DRAM. This additional compu-tation must remain small enough to avoid replacing the I/O bottleneck with a CPU bottleneck. Evaluating every routed vector with a more accurate quantized representation would be too costly, whereas using only a coarse representation would leave too many candidates for exact-vector I/O. To balance these costs, TriSearch progressively increases quantization precision as the candidate set shrinks: it first screens every routed vector using 1-bit RaBitQ, then reads one additional bit plane only for competitive candidates to evaluate them at mid precision (2-bit RaBitQ). To implement this progression, TriSearch visits the routed clusters in order and calls ProgressiveScan on their com-pact vector codes (Lines 5–6). For every vector block, the 1-bit RaBitQ evaluation computes a low-precision distance es-timate for each vector (Lines 17–18). TriSearch maintains an 

R-entry max heap H ordered by mid-precision distance (2-bit RaBitQ). The capacity R bounds the refined candidate pool passed to exact-access selection. Notably, RaBitQ pairs each estimate with a confidence-controlled lower bound, which TriSearch uses to decide whether the vector merits 2-bit re-finement. Before H is full, every vector remains eligible for refinement; afterward, the worst mid-precision distance in H

becomes the admission threshold. TriSearch computes the mid-precision distance for a vector only when its 1-bit Ra-BitQ lower bound falls below this threshold, then uses the refined distance to update the R-entry heap H if it enters the current top R (Lines 20–22). Every successful refinement tightens the heap threshold for subsequent vectors, making later screening more selec-tive. Across clusters, the absence of heap updates provides an early-termination signal: TriSearch stops after W consec-utive routed clusters fail to update H, where W is derived from P (Lines 5–7). Together, within-cluster promotion and cluster-level early termination reserve increasingly precise computation for increasingly smaller candidate sets. 

Exact-access selection. When the scan terminates, H contains at most R candidates with mid-precision distances and lower bounds. TriSearch seeds the exact-access shortlist S with the approximate top-k and uses their kth-best distance as a threshold for the remaining candidates. TriSearch then retains any remaining candidate whose mid-precision lower bound does not exceed this threshold and adds candidates stored on pages already selected for S, since their exact evaluation requires no additional page read (Lines 9–12). 

Vector-level converge phase (exact re-ranking). After the shortlist S is finalized, TriSearch maps its vectors to disk pages, deduplicates and coalesces the reads, and computes ex-act distances from the full-precision vectors as the requested pages arrive (Lines 13–14). Generality across quantizers. TriSearch is not tied to Ra-BitQ. Its low- and mid-precision stages can use different code widths, quantizer configurations, or dimension subsets from different quantization methods. TriSearch uses RaBitQ’s the-oretical lower bounds to enhance candidate promotion and pruning, but quantizers without such bounds can rely on esti-mated distances, fixed refinement ratios, or quantizer-specific pruning criteria. Different quantizers may affect accuracy and efficiency but preserve TriSearch’s three-phase workflow. 

## 4.4 Implementation and Optimizations 

TriSearch bounds the work performed in each phase of the search process; we further optimize the complete workflow to reduce computation, I/O, and memory footprint. 

Efficient progressive distillation. For distillation, each clus-ter stores a 1-bit-per-dimension RaBitQ base code plus one additional bit plane, forming a nested 2-bit-per-dimension representation. The codes are arranged in 32-vector blocks for an AVX-512 FastScan kernel [14]. The kernel computes the base-precision estimate and lower bound together; refine-ment reuses this work and evaluates only the additional bit plane. The final partial block stores only valid lanes and is reconstructed in thread-local scratch space for aligned access, avoiding per-cluster padding. 

Compact in-memory state. The centroid HNSW stores 8-bit scalar-quantized (SQ8) centroids, a configuration supported by Faiss [14] and suited to identifying a broad set of promising clusters. Within each cluster, TriVS also encodes the per-vector RaBitQ estimator fields in SQ8. Using each cluster centroid as the RaBitQ reference gives its distance-estimation factors and bound-error terms a narrow local range, enabling SQ8 encoding with little accuracy loss. Conservative rounding of the error terms preserves valid pruning bounds. 

Page-efficient vector-level convergence. The full-precision vector file densely packs vectors in cluster order, allowing one page read to cover multiple candidates from the same cluster. After the mid-precision gate, TriVS includes candidates on already selected pages at no extra read cost, then deduplicates page IDs and coalesces adjacent pages into larger reads. 

Inline and pooled re-ranking. The exact re-ranking step can run either inline in the query thread or in a dedicated thread pool. In pooled mode, query threads pass finalized shortlists to dedicated re-ranking threads, which continuously issue new exact reads as earlier requests complete. This continu-ous refill can drive SSD utilization closer to its IOPS limit. Pooled re-ranking helps when I/O remains the bottleneck after distillation, but introduces handoff and queueing overhead. Accordingly, TriVS uses pooled re-ranking for workloads that remain I/O-bound after the distill phase and inline re-ranking when the resulting I/O reduction from the distill phase shifts pressure toward computation. 

## 4.5 Discussion 

TriVS focuses on search optimization, while online-update support is orthogonal to its core design. Prior work treats on-line updates as a dedicated design problem: SPFresh [53] sup-ports updates in cluster-based disk-resident ANNS systems; FreshDiskANN [41], IP-DiskANN [52], and OdinANN [21] support updates in graph-based disk-resident ANNS systems. TriVS could adapt SPFresh’s local rebalancing: an insertion assigns a vector to its nearest cluster, stores its cluster-local quantized representation, and records its exact-vector loca-tion, while a deletion invalidates the vector’s unique entry. This approach would preserve the existing query workflow and simplify update handling because TriVS assigns each vector to exactly one cluster without replication, avoiding replica-version tracking and cleanup. 85 Evaluation 

We evaluate TriVS against state-of-the-art disk-resident ANNS systems across diverse workloads and scales. Our key findings include: • At Recall@10=0.9, TriVS achieves on average 3 .42 × Pi-peANN’s throughput, 51.2% lower latency, and 63.7% less disk I/O across five datasets. • TriVS has memory and storage footprints comparable to or, in most cases, smaller than those of existing disk-resident ANNS systems. • TriVS progressively narrows query work; the saved I/O time outweighs distillation overhead, and its advantage grows with query concurrency.

## 6 Related Work 

Disk-resident ANNS systems. Cluster-based systems such as SPANN [9] use an in-memory centroid index to select disk-resident clusters but fetch and scan each selected cluster in full. Helmsman [25] augments SPANN with learned cluster pruning (not publicly released) and specialized SSD-array execution, but retains a single cluster granularity. On the other hand, graph-based systems retain vector-level selectivity but read graph records from disk during proximity-graph traversal, as in DiskANN [28]. Starling [48] and MARGO [57] reorder graph records to improve page locality but require extra construction time and memory. PageANN [35] builds a page-aligned graph, letting one read examine multiple nearby vectors. These locality gains depend on multiple useful vectors sharing a page and diminish as higher dimensionality reduces page capacity [56]. Cache-oriented designs include GoVector [62], which combines hybrid node caching with similarity-aware reordering, and GPS [56], which targets high-dimensional vectors by prior-itizing adjacency lists over full vectors in memory and on disk. PipeANN [20] relaxes strict best-first-search ordering to pipeline dependent graph reads with computation, aligning graph search with SSD characteristics to achieve state-of-the-art performance. Together, page-aware layouts, caching, and pipelined execution reduce graph I/O or hide its latency but cannot eliminate approach-phase I/O. In contrast, TriVS eliminates this I/O through its in-memory approach and dis-till phases, reading only shortlisted full-precision vectors from disk during convergence. Page-aware placement and caching are orthogonal and could further optimize TriVS’s convergence-phase I/O; our evaluation shows that TriVS out-performs PipeANN despite its pipelined execution. 

Other ANNS systems. Conventional in-memory ANNS spans inverted-file [4, 34], graph-based [38], tree-based [47], hash-based [12], and hybrid indexes [8]. Beyond DRAM-only platforms, several ANNS systems build on emerging memory and storage technologies, including persistent memory [40], CXL-attached memory [27], processing-in-memory [51], and computational storage [43]. Accelerator-based systems use GPUs and FPGAs to exploit specialized parallelism for vec-tor search [23, 29, 30, 39, 44, 45, 59, 60]. Distributed ANNS 12 systems scale search across machines [46,61], while disaggre-gated systems separate compute and memory [15, 55]. TriVS focuses on disk-resident vector search using the widely avail-able single-node CPU, DRAM, and SSD platform. 

## 7 Conclusion 

We present TriVS, a new paradigm for disk-resident ANNS that decouples the approach and converge phases from a single search granularity through an in-memory distill phase. TriVS organizes vector search as a trilogy that approaches coarsely, distills progressively, and converges exactly, combining the complementary I/O benefits of cluster and vector granularities to reduce per-query disk I/O. Across diverse workloads, TriVS outperforms state-of-the-art systems in throughput and latency without increasing memory usage.
