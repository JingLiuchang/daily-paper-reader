# [Experiment, Analysis, and Benchmark] When the Bill Falls: Reusing the Old Index Defers, Never Avoids, Re-encoding in Complete Embedding-Model Migration 

Nannan Zhao, Jiameng Zhang, Taoyu Zhong, Hao Wang, and Bo Wang 

Northwestern Polytechnical University 

Xi’an, China nannanzhao@nwpu.edu.cn, {zhangjiameng, zhongtaoyu, kingshulk989, wb2022302586 }@mail.nwpu.edu.cn 

Abstract —Upgrading the embedding model underlying a vector index requires re-encoding the entire corpus. Recent literature proposes reusing the old index through several strategies: adapters translate new-model queries into the old embedding space; window re-ranking lets the old index nominate candidates for the new model to re-score while new vectors are encoded in the background. Testing them on sixteen model pairs over a passage and a code corpus, and eleven more on Natural Questions (NQ), we find three things. First, reuse never avoids re-encoding: once the migration must finish, every strategy encodes every document, and reuse changes only when that work is paid and how much quality is lost in the meantime. Second, adapters are evaluated against the wrong baseline: what matters is whether they beat the old model, and of five adapter families only bridges between two large language model (LLM) embedders of the same family do so, by a margin too small to count as an improvement. Third, the cost of window re-ranking at any depth can be computed from the old index alone, under a bound we prove for every workload; pre-registered replays on the full 8,841,823-passage collection confirmed this prediction on all three pairs tested. Workload skew can also change the best strategy: across 36 further pre-registered Zipf-skewed replays, the planned strategy held on 32. From these findings we derive three rules, all computed before migrating: whether reuse is worthwhile, which strategy to use, and how deep a window to take. The depth rule, committed before its replays, priced every window that reached target quality to within 4 parts in 10 5 of the corpus. On NQ, a plan committed before any replay chose the strategy on 10 of 11 pairs and priced every window exactly. The other two rules agree with the 13 core replays, the second among the strategies it considers. The quality inputs, the gap between the models and the window’s quality loss, transferred less completely: they must be measured on the index that will serve. 

Index Terms —Vector Search, Embedding models, Index Main-tenance, Model Migration, Cost-based Planning 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Dense retrieval ties an index to a single embedding model. The standard practice for an upgrade is a blue–green rebuild: re-encode the corpus with the new model, build a second index, validate it, switch traffic [1], and retire the old index. Until it is retired, the old index doubles storage and every write path, and retiring it requires the migration to finish; at hundreds of millions of documents, that takes days of accelerator time. Recent literature proposes three ways to reuse the old index instead. Adapters learn a map that translates new-model queries into the old embedding space, so that the old index can keep serving [2]; they aim to avoid re-encoding documents. Window re-ranking lets the old index nominate candidates, re-scores them with the new model, and encodes new vectors as queries arrive and in the background [3]; it defers encoding. Structure reuse exploits the old graph index to build the new one faster [4]; it neither avoids nor defers encoding. 

Observations. We tested these methods on sixteen model pairs over a 10 6-passage MS MARCO [5] corpus and a code corpus, and on eleven more pairs on NQ [6], asking what each method saves and how much quality it loses. Consider a typical pair (P2, Table IV): an index served by MiniLM [7] and upgraded to bge-base [8], which raises Recall@10 from 0.8335 to 0.8805. (i) Adapters are judged against the wrong baseline. The literature reports adapter recall relative to the new model, with Drift-Adapter reaching 95–99% [2]. The decision, however, asks whether an adapter beats the old model, since an adapter that serves below it is worse than not migrating. On this pair, that requires 94.7% of the new model’s recall, and the smaller the upgrade, the closer the bar is to 100%. In our replays (each a deterministic re-execution of a strategy over a fixed arrival stream), a Procrustes adapter [9], a learned rotation between the two spaces, reaches 46.8%, and the best of five adapter families 64%. Only on the P5r pair (Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B →8B [10]; Table V) does any family clear the old model, by a margin too small to count as an improvement; on every other pair every family serves below it, the replayed one by up to 0.7843 Recall@10. (ii) Reuse does not save encoding. If the old index is to be retired, every strategy eventually encodes every document; reuse changes only when the work is paid. On this pair, by the time the evaluated queries are served, window re-ranking has re-encoded 26.4% of the corpus and serves within 0.0023 Recall@10 of the new model, while the rebuild’s background has encoded only 3%. None of the cited arXiv:2611.20702v1 [cs.IR] 16 Nov 2026 work prices this cost in advance. 

Three decisions. Together with the fact that structure reuse neither avoids nor defers encoding, these observations split the migration decision into three questions, asked in order (Section IV). First, is the upgrade worth doing? While a rebuild runs, the old model keeps serving, so the quality lost is the gap between the two models; if that gap is within a tolerance declared in advance, the upgrade buys nothing distinguishable, and if it goes ahead a plain rebuild loses nothing either. Second, if it is worth doing, which strategy should be used? By (i), an adapter is considered only if it beats the old model; structure reuse is not an alternative; what remains is the window. Third, how deep should the window be? By (ii), a window defers encoding, so the deferral must be priced, and the old index alone suffices to price it: counting how many queries’ windows cover each document predicts how many documents will have been encoded after any number of queries, without encoding any of them (Section III), and an independent-window form bounds this cost for every workload. Like a column histogram in a database optimizer, it prices a plan without executing it. Before comparing strategies, we must fix three things: what we count, when we measure quality, and which strategies may win. Otherwise, each strategy could be charged in the way that flatters it: a window strategy could be scored on documents it has not yet encoded, and a rebuild could be measured only after its background has finished. We therefore count document encodings under the new model, measure recall when each query is first served, and let a strategy win only if it completes, reaches target quality, and serves no worse than the old model. Section II-D then defines the four quantities used throughout.

B. What we count: document encodings 

The dominant cost of migration is encoding documents with ft, so we count document encodings, and total encoding work means the number of documents encoded with ft. Query encoding, ANN search, BM25, adapter fitting and application, and index builds are not counted; they are orders of magnitude cheaper per call. Equal counts therefore do not mean equal CPU, GPU or wall-clock totals, since strategies differ in these uncounted steps. Inline encoding also adds query latency, which the count does not capture; Section VI-C measures it. 

Completion is a constraint. The old index can be retired only once all N documents are encoded under ft, so every admissible strategy must end there; one that does not get there on its own is paired with a background re-encoder that completes. Total encoding work is then N for every admissible strategy. This follows from the constraint, not from a measurement: any saving a reuse strategy appears to offer is a matter of when the work is paid, not whether. Section VI-A measures what does. 

C. When we measure quality, and what may win 

Quality is measured once per evaluation query, when that query is first served. A window strategy encodes its query’s missing window documents inline and is charged for them at that moment; a rebuild serves the old index and is charged for whatever its background has done. This rules out two accounting errors. Scoring window documents that are not yet encoded would credit a window strategy with quality it has not paid for. Re-measuring one query at successive checkpoints would reward a strategy for being measured late, after more background work. For strategy σ with mean served Recall@10 ¯Mσ over the evaluation queries, its debt debt( σ) = MT − ¯Mσ is the recall it forgoes while the migration runs; lower is better. A rebuild serves source quality until it switches, so its debt is the quality gap MT − MS whenever the queries are served before then. On P2, for example, the rebuild’s background has encoded only 0.03 of N when the evaluation queries are served, so its debt is the full gap, 0.0471; window re-ranking has paid 0.264 of N by then (Section III prices it in advance) and its debt is +0.0023. Section VI-A compares all six strategies.

Section III prices a window at any depth from the serving index. What a practitioner must still decide, since every strategy encodes N documents either way, is whether the upgrade is worth starting, what is served meanwhile, and when the work is paid. The planner estimates from statistics and never executes a candidate migration, as a cost-based optimizer does. Its decisions restate conditions of the frame with the constants declared in advance: D1 the band τ ; D2 the rule that a strategy serve no worse than the old model, ¯Mσ ≥ MS ; D3 the rule that it reach target quality, ¯Mσ ≥ MT −ε, and the band. We call the version fixed before the replays the committed planner ; “the planner” means it unless stated. It returns a family, a depth and a price. Coverage, which prices the migration, needs no target model at all. The quality inputs do (Table III): MT − MS on a judged sample of documents, and G(K′) on the documents in the judged queries’ source windows, at most qK ′ for q judged queries ( 2.5% of N =10 6 for 500 queries at K′=50 ). The planner cannot avoid running the new model on a sample, and that sample is not small when a decision is close: a corpus sample misstates the gap (Sections VI-E, VII), and D1 decides as on the full corpus for 10 of 12 pairs only at a 25% sample (25.3% of the target encoding). But under completion they are not overhead either. Every document vector the planner computes is one the migration needs, so when D1 says migrate it is the first instalment of the bill, paid early; it is wasted only when D1 says no. D1: worth more than a rebuild? The upgrade is worth disrupting only if the new model is better by more than the margin one has declared equivalent. Under first-arrival measurement this is not a proxy for anything. A rebuild serves source quality throughout, so its debt is the quality gap, and it equals MT − MS exactly on 11 of our 13 pairs. D1 therefore does not stop a complete migration: when g ≤ τ the rebuild is within the band, so no reuse strategy is worth its cost, and skipping the upgrade loses nothing distinguishable. 

D2: which family? The bridge clause follows from the frame: a bridge serves MA until the background finishes, so it beats not migrating only if MA > M S , and distinguishably so only by more than τ . In our data it never admits a bridge: the one pair whose bridges clear MS , P5r, clears it by less than 

τ (Section VI-B), so the released planner lists S1 and S3 as excluded; P5r’s replay shows that this can exclude a winning S3. The second clause is a scope condition, and we state it as one: A cost-based planner recommends only strategies whose cost it can bound from its inputs. 

The committed planner computed cov( K′) only over S2’s windows, which for S4 and S5 is a lower bound (Section III). We exclude them because the planner as committed cannot price them, not because they never win; Section VI-E lists every cell they win. 

D3: at what depth? The cost of the chosen depth is cov( K′)

once the workload is served, and Section III gives it at any point before that. Both conditions are needed. A window serves target quality minus its gap, so a shallower window is cheaper precisely because it is worse. Admissibility alone would accept a depth whose gap consumes the whole value of the upgrade; the second condition, g − G(K′) > τ , requires the window to keep more than τ of the upgrade’s gain g.V. E XPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Operating point, and why it can falsify the claims. At the primary traffic intensity the rebuild’s background has encoded only 0.03 of the corpus by the time the evaluated population is served, so a window strategy that looks cheap must be cheap in its own inline encoding, not because the background did the work. On text, windows at K′=50 touch only 0.2540–0.2653 of the corpus, so there is room both for a deferral to show and for the cost model to be wrong. The code corpus is the opposite case: its windows touch every function from K′=50 ,so window selection is vacuous by construction, and we use it as a boundary test (Section VI-G), not as evidence about selection.

Uniformity fixes the coverage numbers we report; it does not enter the identity between the on-demand strategy’s cost and the coverage of the served windows, nor Theorem 2, which holds for every workload. With background budget b encodings per round and λ queries per round, traffic intensity is tr = λK ′/b ∈ { 4, 16 , 64 , 256 }; tr=64 is pre-registered as primary. 

Skew, a sensitivity check. A skewed workload changes the window masses that Procedure 1 reads, not the procedure. Replayed under a pre-registered Zipf(0.9) workload, P2, P5, P11, P13 keep all 2,088 points below Theorem 2’s bound and S2’s cost within 0.00005 of N ; but quality is workload-weighted, so debts move by up to 0.0070 and the winner changes on P2 (to S4) and P5 (to S0); weighted by Zipf, P5’s gap is negative. We then replayed 36 more pre-registered Zipf workloads ( α = 0 .3, 0.6, 1.2 on the ten text pairs; 0.9 on the other six): the identity holds on all 36, and the committed family matches on 32, missing P5 at three skews and P14 at α = 1 .2. A static, post hoc scan of all 10 text pairs (five popularity orders per α) shows why: the weights shrink the 3,490 judged queries to an effective 2,896 at α=0 .3, 796 at 0.6, 93 at 0.9 and 18 at 1.2, and D1/D3 then decide otherwise than under uniform arrivals in 3, 8, 22 and 27 of 50 settings (P2: 13 of 20). It reproduces each replayed rebuild debt (difference at most 10 −15 ).

A. What separates strategies once total encoding work cannot 

A finished migration has encoded the whole corpus, so total encoding work is N for every admissible strategy by definition. The identity is trivial once stated; what the cited work omits is the price of the work left after a deferral. All 180 replayed strategy–order combinations that use the demand

The deferral is paid in latency. On one RTX 4090 D, that takes 31–187 ms for 50 documents and 16–93 ms for 25, plus at most 119–665 ms behind a background batch. A deployment that cannot afford that wait should rebuild or take the shallower window D3 allows.

G. The regime where reuse cannot help 

Windowed reuse defers 1 − cov( K′) of the work, and 

cov( K′) → 1 as |Q|K′ approaches N . Our code corpus is past that point: 43,730 servable queries over 45,773 functions. At K′=25 the windows already touch all but 12 functions (coverage 0.99974), and from K′=50 upward they touch every one. The ceiling on deferral is zero however compatible the models are, a property of corpus and workload that the planner reads from the same retrieval pass. A window then costs N

and a rebuild is as cheap, so selection is vacuous and the code pairs are no evidence. VII. S CALE

Embedding model upgrades. Work on upgrades falls into reuse mechanisms, compatibility measures and training-side methods. Drift-Adapter maps new queries into the legacy space and reports 95–99% of re-embedding Recall@10 at under 10 μsper query [2]; our S1/S3 are its family, and we add the bar a migration decision uses, MS . EmbedFlow serves a target model over source-index candidates while materializing target vectors [3]; S2 is its mechanism and G(K′) its diagnostic. We add the cost model, the decision whether to migrate at all, and the comparison across strategies. Residual reachability reuses old graph structure to build the new index [4]. Our compatibility axis follows Procrustes alignment [9], CKA [12] and relative representations [23]. Training-side methods make a new model backward-compatible with the old index [24], [25] or map an updated model’s queries into the old space [26], but they need control over training; we study upgrades between released models, whose relative quality benchmarks report [27]. 

Dynamic vector indexes and stale representations. The cost model reads only the serving index’s top-K′ windows, which graph, partition and quantization indexes [28], [29], [30], [31] all return. Updatable indexes [32], [33], [34] assume the new vector exists, which is the cost studied here. S4 and S5 combine dense and lexical candidates, as hybrid search does [35]. Query-time recomputation [36] and corrector networks [37] reduce re-encoding in other settings. CIPBench finds, under a fixed encoder, that an exposure signal adds nothing over change history for choosing what to re-embed [38], consistent with our ordering result. 

Systems. Vector databases [39], [40] treat the embedding model as fixed; the planner borrows System-R’s stance [41] of estimating from statistics, and learned indexes face a related question of when to replace a stale model [42]. IX. D ISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION 

Recommendations. Quality first: measure MT − MS on a judged sample of the index that will serve (a smaller corpus can misstate it either way); if it is within the band you declare in advance (the largest regression you would not notice), the upgrade buys nothing distinguishable: rebuild without reuse, or skip it. Then cost: measure cov( K′) on the serving index (one retrieval pass, no target encoding). If 1 − cov( K′) is small, rebuild. Otherwise use plain window re-ranking at the smallest depth whose G fits your quality band minus the margin by which served debt exceeds G (0.006 here; Section VI-E), with any completing order, if a cold query can wait one batched forward pass. Plain S2 is the cost-efficient default, unless the lexical union’s gain is worth paying 3.2–5.7 × as much up front (Section VI-E). 

Limitations. D3’s depths were scored at one traffic intensity, on MS MARCO and NQ, and its quality criterion is tight (P5 misses by 0.00006; debt exceeds G by up to 0.0058). The scale replays cover three pairs at one depth, K′=50 . At Zipf(1.2) the static scan leaves 18 effective judged queries and the replayed arrival order about 12, and D1 decides near its threshold unstably. D2 excludes S3 whenever the bridge falls short of τ , which can miss a winning S3, as on P5r. Latency is measured on an idle accelerator; a shared host multiplied it by up to 3. Table I lists what is not covered. 

Conclusion. Once a migration must finish, every strategy encodes the same N documents; strategies differ only in when that work is paid and in the quality served meanwhile. Window cost can be read from the serving index before migrating, and pre-registered replays at 8,841,823 passages matched it on all three pairs tested; a bridge must beat the old model; and quality inputs, skew included, must be measured on the serving index.
