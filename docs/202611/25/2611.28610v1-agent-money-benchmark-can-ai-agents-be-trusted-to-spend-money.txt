# Agent Money Benchmark: Can AI Agents Be Trusted to Spend Money? 

Zhe Chen, Emma Hsieh, Vicky Fu 

BlockRun Research Team andy@blockrun.ai emma@blockrun.ai vicky@blockrun.ai 

Abstract 

Giving an AI agent a wallet lets it buy data and services on a client’s behalf. Trust then depends on how it spends money and what that spending achieves. We introduce Agent Money Bench-mark (AMB) to test whether agents spend a client’s money well, starting with purchases of information for financial decisions. We link seller quotes and payments to final answers, evaluating 12 models on 80 payment tasks with simulated sellers and 76 research questions us-ing live data. Purchases settled in USDC on Solana mainnet via x402, a protocol for pay-ing per web request. Three of the 12 models passed 79 of 80 payment tasks without pay-ment or budget errors. Nine fell for a seller’s trap at least once; repeat charges and above-list quotes accounted for 79% of scam events. We estimate waste from data and inference costs, counting failed payment tasks in full and re-search answers in proportion to missed points. With spending split equally between tracks, es-timated waste ranged from $21.0 per $100 for Claude Fable 5.2 to $70.8 for GPT-6 Luna. 

1 Introduction 

Giving an AI agent a wallet lets it act on a client’s behalf, including buying information for financial decisions. The client must trust it to spend within budget, resist dishonest sellers and obtain useful ev-idence. With x402, a client can authorize an agent to pay for data one request at a time. Once a pay-ment settles on-chain, revising the answer leaves the transfer in place. Benchmarks such as Agent-Bench and WebArena evaluate whether agents ac-complish tasks in interactive environments (Liu et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2024). When the agent also controls a wallet, the client needs to know what it paid for along the way. Delegating a purchase gives an agent control over both the client’s money and the evidence the client will see. An agent can correctly value a company using market data after paying twice for the same filing. It can also pay the agreed price and use figures from the wrong market. Checking the purchase requires the seller’s terms and the payment record. Checking what the client got for the money requires the delivered data and the final answer. We make three contributions with Agent Money Benchmark (Figure 1). 1. Two complementary evaluation tracks. We test 12 model configurations on 80 payment tasks with simulated sellers and 76 financial research questions using live data, for 1,872 scored runs (BlockRunAI, 2026). 2. Grading against settled payments. On pay-ment tasks, deterministic checks match on-chain charges to seller quotes, deliveries and the agent’s expense report. On research tasks, we trace purchases to the answer and score it against a question-specific rubric. 3. The cost of unsuccessful work. We add data fees and inference costs. A failed payment task counts at full cost. For a research answer, we count the share of its cost corresponding to the share of available credit it missed. All 12 configurations passed a valuation task on which they made the same four purchases. When a seller requested a second payment for data already bought, agents paid again in 52 of 108 encounters. On a research question, GLM 5.3 and GPT-6 Luna each spent $0.0075 on the same hourly price data. GLM earned 87/100. Luna scored zero after its reported prices failed a required check.

3 Experimental Design 

3.1 Tasks and purchasing environment 

We evaluated all 12 configurations through the BlockRun gateway on the same 80 payment tasks and 76 research questions, yielding 960 payment and 912 research runs (BlockRunAI, 2026). Here, a configuration denotes a model together with its evaluation settings. Data purchases in both tracks settled in USDC on Solana mainnet via x402. In the payment track, simulated sellers share a receiv-ing wallet. Prices and deliveries vary by seller. The client specifies a budget and requires an expense report. Agents choose sellers and decide which checks to perform. 2We group payment tasks by their main chal-lenge. Our groups are Calculation & Accounting (27 tasks), Budget-Constrained Data Acquisition (27), and Adversarial Transactions (26). A task can draw on all three skills. Before paying, an agent can compare a quote with the listed price, check its expiry, and consult its own purchase his-tory. After paying, it can compare the returned item with the request and record the delivery. We grade payment tasks using deterministic checks on the answer, transactions and expense report. We asked the agents to calculate the combined market value of two companies from closing prices and shares outstanding. All 12 configurations bought the same two price quotes and two filing sheets, spent $0.030 each and passed (BlockRunAI, 2026). Research questions use live external sources and cover prediction markets, wallets, crypto prices, DeFi, stocks, social sentiment and multi-source judgment. We ask agents to identify position hold-ers, assess advertised yields and establish which market moved before explaining why. 

3.2 Grading research answers 

We used DeepSeek V4 Pro to score each research answer against the rubric for that question, item by item. We then manually reviewed every answer’s score (BlockRunAI, 2026).

5 Results 

5.1 Which payment failures recur? 

Of 960 payment runs, 175 failed. Of these, 119 (68.0%) contained a money error. Nine of 12 con-figurations recorded a scam event, and seven paid twice for previously purchased data. Above-list quotes produced the most scam events (60/343 en-counters, 17.5%). Repeat-charge requests had the highest rate once encountered (52/108, 48.1%). Together they accounted for 112 of 141 events (79.4%; Figure 2B). Gemini 4 Argon, GPT-6 Astra and Claude Fable 5.2 completed 79 of 80 tasks each (98.8%, Wilson interval 93–100%), with zero money errors. GPT-6 Astra recorded zero scams in 127 encounters. The other two each accepted one wrong delivery. For Beam 1.0, 16 scam events in 121 encounters (13.2%) affected 15 of 80 tasks (18.8%); ten tasks (12.5%) had a money error. Across configurations, 276/324 calculation runs (85.2%), 304/324 budget runs (93.8%) and 205/312 adversarial runs (65.7%) succeeded. The adversar-ial group accounts for 107/175 failures (61.1%). Claude Sonnet 5.5 passed every budget task and 26.9% of adversarial tasks; Beam 1.0 passed 85.2% and 61.5%, respectively. We group failed runs by their primary failure label (Table 1). 5.3 When does the spending mix change the ranking? 

Normalized waste ranged from $21.0 to $70.8 per $100 (Figure 4). Claude Fable 5.2 had the lowest point estimate, $21.0 (95% interval $12.5–29.4). Its wasted shares were 2.7% on payments and 39.2% on research, all classified as value loss. Of the $50 allocated to research, $19.60 counted to-ward waste ( 50 × 0.392 ), while all data purchases together cost about $0.15 ( 50 × 0.0068 /2.2096 ). Research inference therefore accounts for over $19 of the $21. Its interval overlaps those of GPT-6.1 Sol ($24.6, $9.4–35.9) and Claude Opus 5.5 ($26.2, $19.3–32.8). For Beam 1.0, money loss accounted for $12.2 of its $58.7 per normalized $100. Its 95% interval was $49.5–66.9. Pooling actual expenditure placed Claude Opus 5.5 first (20.2%) and Claude Fable 5.2 second (24.4%). Fable had lower waste than Opus on pay-ments (2.7% versus 3.6%) and research (39.2% ver-sus 48.8%). Fable therefore ranks ahead of Opus at every shared spending mix. 6 Conclusion 

Trust in an agent’s spending decisions depends on the model. On our payment tasks, some models handled purchases reliably, while others repeatedly accepted dishonest charges. We recommend grant-ing spending authority based on each model’s mea-sured performance, with receipt and price checks before payment. Gemini 4 Argon, GPT-6 Astra and Claude Fable 5.2 each passed 79 of 80 payment tasks with zero payment or budget errors. On the 626 adversarial tasks, GPT-6 Astra passed all 26 and GPT-6 Luna passed two. Even within the Claude family, Opus 5.5 declined all nine requests to pay again for data it had already bought, while Sonnet 5.5 paid again on all nine. Repeat charges and above-list prices accounted for 112 of 141 recorded scam events. A buyer can check its receipts for the same item and compare each quote with the listed price. For GPT-6 Astra, the 76 research answers missed 31.22% of the available credit on average. With questions weighted by their data and inference costs, the share of credit missed was 65.5%. Under our scoring rule this assigns $65.50 per $100 of research spending to missed credit. At equal track spending, Claude Fable 5.2 had the lowest estimated waste ($21.0 per $100). Of that amount, $19.60 came from research costs at-tributed to missed rubric points. As the payment share rose, GPT-6.1 Sol led from about 83% to 97% and GPT-6 Astra above 97%. 

Limitations 

We tested payment decisions with small budgets and simulated sellers sharing one wallet. The re-ported results include one scored run per configura-tion and task. Research questions used live market data (BlockRunAI, 2026). The task-bootstrap inter-vals describe variation across task samples.
