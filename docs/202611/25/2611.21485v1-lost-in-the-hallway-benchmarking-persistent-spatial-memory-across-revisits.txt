Memory Across Revisits

Huang2,†
1Tsinghua University, 2PICO, ByteDance

Abstract
Persistent spatial understanding is essential for embodied agents to operate reliably in environments
they repeatedly encounter. It requires recognizing revisited places despite viewpoint changes,
distinguishing them from similar-looking rooms, and updating memories as environments change.
Yet existing evaluations often leave physical place identity across separated visits implicit. We
introduce ReSpaceBench, a benchmark comprising 3,244 questions across 590 synthetic and
real-home scenes, organized into 9 tasks spanning room association, persistent state tracking,
and downstream reasoning. Controlled walkthroughs define visits by physical room entry and
deliberately include same-type room distractors and cross-session object changes. Our evaluation
of 32 models and 3 agentic video-understanding frameworks reveals substantial limitations: the
strongest evaluated zero-shot model achieves 50.5% overall accuracy and 52.2% on a 450-question
subset, compared with 90.1% for human annotators. The evaluated agentic frameworks offer no
overall improvement over direct inference with the same base model. Analysis of agent execution
traces reveals evidence omissions and incorrect cross-visit room associations as major failure modes,
suggesting that iterative evidence seeking alone does not ensure consistent spatial memory. Further
analyses expose a gap between recognizing familiar rooms and identifying the correct visited room,
while annotation-guided sampling highlights the importance of preserving intervening visits and
transitions. We also provide RS-30K, a separate training dataset from synthetic sources with no
template, scene, route, or video overlap with the benchmark. Fine-tuning Qwen3-VL-8B raises
overall accuracy from 35.5% to 60.4%, with the largest gains in association and improvements
extending to real-life scenes. ReSpaceBench and RS-30K provide resources for diagnosing and
improving persistent spatial memory across revisits.

or after its contents have changed. To build on past experience during these revisits, it must associate new
observations with the correct physical place and update its spatial memory accordingly [27, 30, 49]. Yet
viewpoint variation can make even a familiar room appear substantially different across visits, complicating

the correct identity of the room being revisited [37, 72]. Over time, the agent must also track state changes in
the room’s contents and update the corresponding memory [29, 40]. Revisits therefore test whether systems
can preserve place identity while tracking changes across temporal observations (Fig. 1).
Recent benchmarks have expanded the evaluation of multimodal large language models (MLLMs) from
single-image 3D relations to video-based spatial reasoning and long-horizon memory [31, 35, 39, 60]. Yet
models still struggle with global cognitive maps, cross-view correspondence, and coherent spatial memory
[34, 35, 60, 66]. Cross-visit place association, a task well studied in robotics through loop closure and visual
place recognition [8, 37], remains largely implicit in the spatial understanding task evaluations. Recent
benchmarks, UCS-Bench [53] and SpaMEM [34] evaluate persistent spatial reasoning and memory under
scene changes, while LongSpace-Bench [31] covers scene consistency and long-horizon recall; however, none
establishes a dedicated protocol for room re-identification across controlled revisits. OVO-S-Bench [33] includes
revisit detection and counting, but defines visits by whether a location enters the field of view, allowing a
room briefly glimpsed during a camera turn to count without physical entry. Its construction also does not
systematically control same-type rooms as competing identities. Thus, associating observations with specific
physical rooms across controlled revisits remains under-explored.
We present ReSpaceBench, a benchmark for persistent spatial understanding across revisits. Its 9 tasks
span three task families: association, recognizing previously visited rooms and matching them to earlier
observations; persistent state, maintaining and updating room and object memories; and downstream reasoning,
recovering temporal order and spatial topology. Walkthroughs follow controlled routes, with visits defined by
physical room entry rather than visual observation. We select homes with spaces of the same type to serve as
identity distractors, testing whether models recognize a specific place rather than merely remembering its
semantic category.
We construct ReSpaceBench from synthetic homes in 3D-FRONT [19] and ProcTHOR [13], real homes
scanned with FARO laser scanners [17], and egocentric videos recorded with a PICO headset [42]. Using
annotated scene metadata and constructed navigation meshes, we use our designed question templates to
generate task-conditioned routes: each template specifies both the question and the navigation requirements
needed to answer it. The resulting route trajectories include egocentric videos with repeated room visits. We
generate answers from metadata, then apply rule-based checks with human verification to remove question–
answer pairs that are inconsistent with the video.

Our evaluation of 32 models and three agentic video-understanding frameworks (Section 4) reveals substantial
limitations in persistent spatial memory. Proprietary models remains the strongest zeroshot models in our
evaluation, while specialized spatial and video models do not consistently outperform open-source general-
purpose MLLMs. The evaluated agentic frameworks offer no overall gains over direct inference. On a shared
450-question subset, humans achieve 90.1% accuracy compared with 52.2% for the strongest evaluated zero-shot
model GPT-5.6 Sol. Further analysis of the six proprietary models on query-to-memory association shows
that wrong-room selections account for 66.5–88.3% of their errors, exposing a gap between recognizing a room
as familiar and identifying the correct visited room. To support model development, we also provide 30k
training questions from ProcTHOR training scenes with no scene, route, or video overlap with the benchmark.
Fine-tuning Qwen3-VL-8B on this dataset raises overall accuracy from 35.5% to 60.4%, with the largest gains
in association and smaller, less consistent gains in state tracking and reasoning.
Our contributions are as follows.
• A benchmark for persistent spatial memory. We introduce ReSpaceBench, comprising 9 tasks in 3 task
families across synthetic and real homes. Controlled revisits, same-type room distractors, and cross-session
state changes support explicit evaluation of room association, persistent state tracking, and downstream
spatial reasoning.
• A systematic evaluation and error analysis. We evaluate 32 models and 3 agentic video-understanding
frameworks, with a human performance reference on a shared subset. Detailed analyses of room-association
errors, agentic failures, and evidence coverage identify recurring limitations in maintaining and using spatial
memory across revisits.
• A training dataset. We release 30k training samples generated from ProcTHOR scenes, with no template,
scene, route or video overlap with the benchmark. A targeted fine-tuning study demonstrates their utility
for developing models with stronger persistent spatial memory.

Related Work
2.1
Long-context visual retrieval.
Long-context evaluations examine whether models can retrieve and combine sparse visual evidence from
extended multimodal inputs. Visual Haystacks tests visual needle retrieval and cross-image reasoning, revealing
sensitivity to evidence position [57]. MMLongBench broadens evaluation to diverse interleaved vision–language
tasks at controlled context lengths [54], while Video-LevelGauge systematically probes positional bias in video
contexts [58]. Recently, agentic video-understanding frameworks such as AVP, DVD, and VideoAgent use
tools to retrieve and inspect video evidence [52, 56, 71]. Retrieving relevant observations, however, does not
establish whether they refer to the same physical room across separate visits. ReSpaceBench explicitly
evaluates this cross-visit room association over long visual contexts.
2.2
Spatial intelligence and long-horizon memory.
Recent benchmarks address complementary aspects of spatial intelligence and long-horizon memory. SpaMEM
studies spatial belief updates and object-identity continuity under action-conditioned scene changes in
ProcTHOR [34], while UCS-Bench evaluates continual spatial reasoning relative to users’ changing locations
in egocentric streams [53]. EgoMemReason probes entity, event, and behavioral memory over week-long videos
[55], and 3DMem-Bench evaluates memory-dependent question answering and embodied tasks in dynamic 3D
environments [25]. MMSI-Video-Bench includes cross-video spatial reasoning [35]. All-Angles Bench [66] and
SpaCE-10 [22] evaluate multi-view and compositional spatial understanding of a static scene from several
images, and SAW-Bench [32] evaluates situated awareness in egocentric videos, including changes between
two videos of the same scene. However, these benchmarks do not systematically evaluate whether models
can recognize a physical room when it is revisited. In generative world modeling, MemoBench evaluates
whether video generators recover objects in their updated states after disappearance and reappearance [10].
More closely related to our setting, LongSpace-Bench includes scene consistency, state change, and route
recall in long room-tour videos [31]. OVO-S-Bench evaluates scene revisit detection and counting under a
causal, prefix-only protocol, with visits in its counting task defined by a location entering the field of view [33].
ReSpaceBench defines visits by physical room entry and deliberately includes distinct same-type rooms as
competing identities along controlled revisit trajectories.
2.3
Persistent association and structured spatial memory.
Persistent association underlies place recognition and loop closure in SLAM [8]. REMIND uses appearance,
spatial context, and joint assignment for indoor object re-identification [15], while R4DSG indexes egocentric
memory by time, place, persistent objects, and anchor-relative changes [38]. PBD-AG separates a robot-verified
structural baseline from revisable dynamic events [3], and GESTO uses event context within a persistent
4D scene graph to refine object associations [4]. Complementing these methods, ReSpaceBench evaluates
whether MLLMs can associate observations with persistent room identities and use visit histories and state
information to answer questions across revisits.
3
ReSpaceBench
ReSpaceBench evaluates persistent spatial memory through egocentric walkthrough that return to previously
visited places. Its tasks span three task families (Fig. 2): association, recognizing a revisited room and
matching it to an earlier observation; persistent state, maintaining room inventories, object–place bindings, and
object states across visits; and reasoning, inferring temporal order and spatial connectivity from this history.
The benchmark evaluates these capabilities using rendered walkthroughs of synthetic and FARO-scanned
homes, together with PICO headset recordings of real homes.
Task overview.
For association, T1 (query-to-memory association) matches a query room to a visited room or
labels it as never entered; T2 (same-instance identification) identifies which previously visited same semantic
type room is being revisited; and T3 (revisit counting) counts prior entries into a specified room. For persistent
state, T4 (room inventory) counts distinct visited rooms, overall or by semantic type, or determines a room’s

3.1
Benchmark Construction Pipeline
Fig. 3(b) shows the 3 stages of benchmark construction. Scene metadata and camera trajectories connects
each question to the rooms and objects observed in its video, providing a shared and consistent basis for both
question generation and answer verification.
Scene collection, filtering, and metadata.
To ensure that the benchmark poses meaningful spatial-memory
challenges, we select large homes with diverse room layouts and multiple rooms of the same semantic type.
We source synthetic household environments from 3D-FRONT [19], which provides professionally designed
furnished apartments, and ProcTHOR [13], which contains procedurally generated homes. To complement
these synthetic scenes, we scan real-world residential homes using a FARO laser scanner and record egocentric
activities with a PICO headset. For synthetic scenes, we extract room geometry and semantic types, persistent
room and object identities, object categories and locations, and doorway connectivity from the source metadata.
For real homes, we manually annotate the corresponding information in the registered 3D scenes and align
the annotations with the recorded egocentric trajectories.
Route planning and egocentric video generation.
We plan walkthrough routes using room layout to balance
room coverage and controlled revisits. Task-specific configurations include visits to visually similar rooms of
the same semantic type, episodes spanning single and multiple homes, and repeated sessions in the same home

Habitat Simulator [48]. Then, we adapt the navmesh-based navigation setup of Pred-EQA [67] to generate
traversable paths that follow the planned room-entry sequences. We convert each path into a continuous
camera trajectory and render egocentric videos using BlenderProc [14], then concatenating videos across
multiple homes when needed. Rendered walkthroughs use nominal walking speeds of 0.6–0.8 m/s. At each

the room. Hallways and stairwells are treated as transit spaces and are not counted as room visits. Each video
is paired with camera poses, timestamps, room-entry events, and object-state records to support question
generation, answer verification.
Question generation from templates.
We instantiate task-specific question templates using scene metadata,
ordered room-entry sequences, and object-state records. The templates cover room association, room and
object inventories, object to place binding, state changes, temporal order, and room connectivity. Rooms are
referred to by semantic type when the type is unique within a scene. Otherwise, we disambiguate them by
order of first visit or unique visual cues (e.g., the second bedroom visited or the bedroom with wooden floor).
Ground-truth answers are deterministically derived from the corresponding scene annotations and realized
trajectory. For multiple-choice questions, distractors are included to reflect plausible spatial-memory errors,
such as confusing distinct rooms of the same type or inferring direct doorway connectivity from consecutive
visits.
Automatic and Human Quality Control.
We verify each walkthrough by comparing the realized room-entry
sequence with the planned route, rejecting trajectories with missing or unintended room entries. Ground-truth
answers are then recomputed from the verified entry sequence and object-state records, and questions involving
ambiguous visits or insufficient visual evidence are discarded. We further combine task-specific automated
checks with manual inspection to assess rendering quality, room and object visibility, and consistency between
the video and its metadata. During construction, shortcut diagnostics is implemented to examine answer-
frequency biases, question and option wording, and room-type priors. These checks guide revisions to question

templates and answer balancing, while blind and frequency baselines provide additional diagnostics on the
evaluation set.
Training data.
To support model development, we additionally provide RS-30K, a training dataset of 30k
questions generated from ProcTHOR training scenes. RS-30K uses a separate set of question templates
and shares no scenes, routes, or videos with ReSpaceBench. It provides targeted supervision without
reusing evaluation environments or walkthroughs. We assess its utility through supervised fine-tuning of
Qwen3-VL-8B, with results reported in Sec. 4.
3.2
Benchmark Statistics
ReSpaceBench contains 3,244 evaluation questions, including 3,026 questions derived from synthetic
environments and 218 questions from real homes. The real-home questions use 35 walkthrough videos
rendered from FARO-scanned homes, which imitate egocentric videos by following the camera height, field
of view, and motion of PICO recordings, and 13 videos from PICO headset recordings. Because a FARO
scan captures a static scene, the PICO recordings are used for T7 (cross-session state), where objects change
between sessions. Fig. 3(a) shows the distribution across 3 task families and 9 tasks. We additionally use
ReSpaceBench-mini, a 450-question subset containing 50 samples per task, for human evaluation, agentic
experiments, and frame-sampling analyses (Sec. 4). RS-30K is a separate training dataset and is therefore
excluded from these evaluation statistics.
3.3
Evaluation Protocol
Each model receives the walkthrough video and a textual question, together with textual answer options for
multiple-choice items. T1 additionally provides a short query clip showing a room from a new viewpoint. The
model must match this room to a previously entered room or select the “not entered” option. Scene metadata,
camera poses, and ground-truth event annotations are only for further analysis and not provided to models
under the default evaluation protocol.
Responses use one of two formats: an option letter for multiple-choice questions or a value for numeric
questions. Multiple-choice responses are scored by whether the selected option is correct, while numeric
responses are scored by exact match to the ground-truth value. Overall accuracy is computed as the unweighted
mean of the nine task accuracies. We report accuracy for each task and the average of each task family,
alongside the Blind, Chance, and Frequency baselines described in Section 4.
4
Evaluations on ReSpaceBench
We evaluate 32 models covering proprietary MLLMs, open-source general-purpose MLLMs, unified models,
and specialized spatial and video-understanding models, as well as 3 agentic video-understanding frameworks.
Except for targeted fine-tuning, models use their standard inference interfaces without benchmark-specific
training. For direct inference, we sample videos at 1 fps by default, retaining approximately 6–9 views per
room-entry look-around, and further downsample when required by model-specific context limits. Details are
provided in the appendix.
Blind, chance, and frequency baselines.
Blind evaluates Seed 2.0 Pro with task instructions, questions, and
answer options without any visual input. Chance averages 1/K over multiple-choice questions with K options.
Frequency predicts the most frequent ground-truth option letter or numeric value within each question subtype,
with scores weighted by subtype question counts. It is computed from the evaluation answer distribution to
quantify answer imbalance. All model responses follow the task-specific scoring rules (Section 3.3).

Further Analysis
Can models distinguish rooms across revisits?
We analyze T1 errors across the six proprietary models,
including the strongest zero-shot models. Errors comprise wrong room (choosing another visited room), false
merge (labeling an unentered room as visited), and false split (labeling an entered room as unentered). Wrong-
room selections dominate, accounting for 66.5–88.3% of errors (Figure 4(a)). False-split rates on visited-room
queries and false-merge rates on unvisited-room queries are below their respective uniform-random baselines
of 25.0% and 75.0% (Figure 4(b)). However, models still select an incorrect visited room in 44.8–58.0% of
visited-room queries. These results suggest that models can distinguish visited from unvisited rooms to some
extent, but often fail to identify which previously visited room the query depicts.
Why do agentic frameworks fail?
We manually inspect failed trajectories from AVP, DVD, and VideoAgent
on ReSpaceBench-mini, reviewing their reasoning traces, tool calls, observed frames, and final answers
(Fig. 4(c)). We identify four recurring major failure types: missing evidence, inaccurate observations, incorrect
room-identity associations, and reasoning or verification errors. Trajectories with multiple problems are
classified by the first observable error. AVP and DVD most often make errors in room identity association
across revisit, whereas VideoAgent more frequently misses relevant evidence from video sequence. These
findings suggest that evidence verification should check not only whether the necessary evidence has been
retrieved, but also whether it has been interpreted correctly and linked to the correct room and visit.
Does preserving event coverage improve performance?
We compare uniform, most relevant event weighted,
and event coverage aware sampling on ReSpaceBench-mini using GPT-5.6 Sol, with identical prompts and
a fixed budget of 128 frames. Both event-based strategies use walkthrough route annotations: event-weighted
sampling assigns 4× the sampling weight to relevant room-entry events and coverage-aware sampling retains
at least one frame from each room-entry, look-around, and hallway transitions interval. Coverage-aware
sampling achieves 55.3%, compared with 47.6% for uniform and 47.8% for event-weighted sampling; the
separate 1 fps baseline scores 52.2% (Table 4). Omitting an intervening bedroom visit, for example, can make
two bathroom visits appear continuous. These annotation-guided results suggest that preserving intervening
visits and transitions helps distinguish revisits from continuous stays, highlighting the importance of event
coverage alongside query-relevant observations.

5
Conclusion
We introduced ReSpaceBench for evaluating persistent spatial memory across controlled revisits, together
with RS-30K for targeted model training. Across synthetic and real homes, the benchmark evaluates room
association, persistent state tracking, and downstream reasoning under viewpoint variation, identity ambiguity,
and state changes. Our evaluations reveal a substantial human–model gap and no overall advantage from
the evaluated agentic frameworks. The analyses expose a gap between recognizing a room as familiar and
linking it to the correct spatial memory, while annotation-guided sampling demonstrates the value of retaining
intervening visits and transitions. Fine-tuning on RS-30K improves Qwen3-VL-8B from 35.5% to 60.4%, with
gains extending to scene sources absent from training, but state tracking and reasoning benefit less consistently
than association. These findings motivate spatial memory mechanisms that preserve room identity across
viewpoints, distinguish visually similar rooms, and update memories as room and object states change, while
retaining visit histories for downstream reasoning. We hope that ReSpaceBench, RS-30K together with
the analysis can provide a useful foundation for systematic evaluation, failure diagnosis, and targeted model
development, and ultimately support further research toward more robust and generalizable persistent spatial
memory and reasoning across revisits.

A.1
Model Evaluation Settings
We resize video frames to 640 × 480 pixels and initially sample at 1 fps for default setting for all evaluated
models. Due to model-specific context-length limits, we further uniformly downsample the sampled sequence
when necessary, retaining the maximum number of frames that each model can accommodate alongside the
question, answer options, reference inputs, and reserved response tokens. Sequences that fit are used in full.
All retained frames preserve their chronological order. For multi-home visit questions, the context budget
covers all sequences jointly.
For the models that can’t fit our 1 fps video input, our evaluation use 320 for VST-7B-RL, GLM-4.6V-Flash,
and GLM-4.6V (106B); 480 for Gemma-4 E2B, Gemma-4 E4B, Cambrian-S, and Cambrian-P; 150 for
InternVL3.5-8B, InternVL3.5-38B, and InternVL3.5-30B-A3B; and 120 for SenseNova-SI-1.5.

B
Data Construction and Question Definitions
Room entries and persistent identity.
Room entries are recovered from the rendered camera trajectory and
its room labels. A visit requires entering a room; viewing it through a doorway does not count. Re-entry into
the same physical room creates a new visit while preserving its identity. The initial countable room is included
as the first visit, and hallways, corridors, stairs, and other transit spaces are excluded. Room inventories and
first-encounter ordinals count each physical room once, whereas revisit counts retain separate entry events.
Ground-truth verification.
Answers are recomputed from the verified room-entry sequence and object-state
records. For entry-dependent questions, we check the annotated entry order against the recorded look-arounds.
Questions with inconsistent annotations or ambiguous room references are excluded. Object counts use
persistent instance identities to avoid counting the same object again on a revisit.
Task-specific evidence validation.
To ensure disambiguity in synthetic scene annotations, room labels
are checked against the furnishings and fixtures present after scene preparation. T1 (query-to-memory
association) verifies whether the query room was entered, and T9 (temporal successor) uses the realized order
of countable-room entries. Object related questions additionally applies geometric visibility and image-quality
checks to the queried objects over multiple frames, with further framing and occlusion checks for reference
images. Only visible objects in the video are counted towards the ground-truth.

B.3
Shortcut Controls During Construction
The construction pipeline includes several checks to reduce non-visual shortcuts [5]. At the question level, the
question text does not state information that must be recalled from the video. We balance the option letters
(A/B/C/D) as well as the answer content, such as the room type (bathroom/bedroom/living room) or the
ordinal option (first/second/not entered). Questions with the same wording and the same options are paired
with different visual inputs that lead to different answers, so that answering depends on the video. Distractors
are rooms of the same type that appear in the same video, so they cannot be excluded by common sense.
We further check for priors and textual shortcuts by testing strategies that guess a fixed answer from the
room type or object category, or that answer from option length, lexicographic order, or ordinal words. After
rendering, visibility, and quality filtering, we repeat these checks on the resulting answer distributions, so that
filtering does not reintroduce biases.
