# ABSTRACT 

Multi-step retrieval over long contexts is a key capability for retrieval-augmented generation, but strong existing approaches are costly and complex to train. Su-pervised retrievers are far simpler and cheaper, yet they fall well behind in this setting. We show that this gap stems not from supervised training itself, but from three misalignments between training and inference: easy training nega-tives, noisy retrieval prefixes, and position-agnostic scoring. Guided by this anal-ysis, we introduce S 2LMR, a simple yet effective supervised sequential retriever that models multi-step retrieval as the evolution of an explicit textual state en-coded by a single shared Transformer. On long-context reasoning, multi-hop QA, and needle-in-a-haystack benchmarks with context lengths ranging from 4K to 10M tokens, S 2LMR achieves performance comparable to state-of-the-art meth-ods, substantially outperforming prior supervised retrievers. Notably, it requires only ∼2 hours of training on a single A6000 for contexts of up to 10M tokens, while remaining robust across a wide range of hyperparameter settings. 

# 1 INTRODUCTION 

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) has become a standard paradigm for augmenting large language models (LLMs) with external knowledge, improving factuality without requiring large knowledge sources to be directly included in the model context (Lewis et al., 2020; Guu et al., 2020; Izacard & Grave, 2021; Gao et al., 2023). Most existing RAG studies focus on single-step retrieval, which is often sufficient for relatively simple tasks such as Needle-in-a-Haystack (Hsieh et al., 2024). However, single-step retrieval is insufficient for emerging scenarios that require multi-ple rounds of interaction with the context, such as multi-hop question answering (Yang et al., 2018; Trivedi et al., 2022) and reasoning over millions of tokens (Kuratov et al., 2024). These settings require the retriever to progressively gather relevant evidence across multiple steps. There are several types of approaches for multi-step retrieval. Graph-based approaches build struc-tured graphs over retrieved information to connect evidence across documents, but often incur addi-tional overhead from graph construction and traversal (Li et al., 2024; Mao et al., 2021). Some works use LLM agents to guide multi-step retrieval through generated reasoning or instructions (Anokhin et al., 2024; Singh et al., 2025). However, repeated LLM calls increase inference cost, while noisy evidence can misguide later retrieval steps. Another line of work fine-tunes LLMs to interact with re-trieval tools (Jin et al., 2025; Song et al., 2025), but fine-tuning LLMs can be expensive and difficult to scale. More recently, Sorokin et al. (2026) proposed a more resource-efficient approach, Q-RAG, that uses reinforcement learning (RL) to fine-tune the embedder model, achieving state-of-the-art results on multiple long-context multi-step benchmarks with contexts of up to 10 million tokens. However, compared with conventional supervised retrieval using bi-encoders or cross-encoders (Xiong et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2024), this approach introduces additional RL components, such as value and 1arXiv:2611.28995v1 [cs.LG] 21 Nov 2026 Preprint target networks, as well as temporal-difference updates, increasing implementation complexity and introducing additional hyperparameters. This raises an intriguing but non-trivial question: 

Can we achieve effective multi-step retrieval over million-token contexts using only a simple supervised learning framework? 

Through extensive analysis, we identify three misalignments that explain why supervised retrievers fall far behind recent methods such as Q-RAG: (i) training negatives are easy, unrelated passages, whereas test-time distractors come from the same context as the gold passage; (ii) later hops are trained on the model’s own, sometimes wrong, retrieval prefixes, which injects noise into their su-pervision; and (iii) the scorer ignores where a candidate lies relative to the passages already retrieved, even though this position strongly predicts the next relevant passage. Motivated by these observations, we propose the Supervised Sequential Long-Context Multi-Step 

Retriever (S 2LMR), a supervised bi-encoder designed to align training with long-context multi-step retrieval. S 2LMR addresses the identified mismatches through same-context negatives, gold-prefix next-evidence supervision, and history-adaptive relative positions, using a single shared Transformer encoder with an explicit textual state that evolves across retrieval hops. Across (ultra)-long context benchmarks and multi-hop QA, S 2LMR matches or outperforms the state-of-the-art RL-based re-triever Q-RAG (Sorokin et al., 2026). Our main contributions are as follows: • We propose S 2LMR, a supervised sequential retriever built on a single shared Transformer encoder and a simple contrastive supervision. • On long-context commonsense reasoning, multi-hop QA, and needle-in-a-haystack tasks with contexts from 4K to 10M tokens, S 2LMR matches or outperforms the state-of-the-art Q-RAG. It also substantially outperforms Beam Retriever, the strongest existing supervised retriever. On BabiLong QA3, S 2LMR keeps fact EM above 0.95 at up to 10M tokens, while Beam Retriever reaches only 0.2–0.3. • We further show that S 2LMR offers substantial advantages in training efficiency and sta-bility. For example, on BabiLong QA3, S 2LMR reaches its peak performance in 1.4 GPU hours and completes training in 2.25h on a single A6000, compared with 3h and 54.4h for Q-RAG, and more than 400 GPU hours for Beam Retriever. Moreover, compared with Q-RAG, S 2LMR exhibits more stable training dynamics and is substantially more robust to a wide range of hyperparameter choices, including learning rate and batch size. 

# 2 RELATED WORKS 

Multi-step retrieval. Multi-hop retrieval methods (Das et al., 2019; Qi et al., 2019) extend single-step dense retrieval (Karpukhin et al., 2020; Izacard et al., 2021) by conditioning each retrieval decision on previously retrieved evidence. MDR (Xiong et al., 2020) uses a bi-encoder and it-eratively augments the query with retrieved passages, while BeamRetriever (Zhang et al., 2024) combines a cross-encoder with beam search for stronger multi-hop retrieval at higher inference cost. Q-RAG (Sorokin et al., 2026) further studies embedder-based multi-step retrieval in long-context settings. Another line of work uses LLM agents to interleave retrieval with generated reasoning (Yao et al., 2022; Trivedi et al., 2023; Shao et al., 2023; Asai et al., 2024; Singh et al., 2025; Jin et al., 2025; Song et al., 2025). These methods provide flexible reasoning but require repeated LLM in-ference across retrieval steps. S 2LMR focuses instead on more efficient sequential retrieval with a lightweight shared encoder. 

Retrieval for long-context tasks. Long-context benchmarks such as RULER (Hsieh et al., 2024) and BabiLong (Kuratov et al., 2024) evaluate reasoning over contexts ranging from thousands to millions of tokens. Existing approaches improve long-context processing through architectural changes, in-cluding recurrent memory (Bulatov et al., 2022; Behrouz et al., 2026; 2025; Rodkin et al., 2024), state-space models (Gu & Dao, 2023), and positional encoding adaptation (Shang et al., 2025). An alternative is to first retrieve a small subset of relevant content from the long context (Jiang et al., 2024a;b). Q-RAG (Sorokin et al., 2026) follows this direction and shows that learned chunk embed-dings can support multi-step retrieval over very long contexts. S 2LMR builds on this retrieval-based paradigm while introducing history-adaptive retrieval states and candidate positions for simple su-pervised multi-step retrieval. 2Preprint 

Figure 1: Overview of S 2LMR, which performs sequential multi-hop retrieval with a shared bi-encoder. The initial state s0 contains the query q, and all chunks of the long context are embedded and cached at the start. At each hop t, S 2LMR embeds the current state st, re-rotates cached chunk embeddings according to the retrieval history, and scores candidate chunks by their inner products with the state embedding. During training (blue), gold chunks provide per-hop supervision; during inference (orange), S 2LMR greedily selects and appends the highest-scoring chunk. 

Supervised training for dense retrieval. Dense retrievers are commonly trained with contrastive objectives (Oord et al., 2018; Karpukhin et al., 2020). DPR (Karpukhin et al., 2020) trains a bi-encoder using in-batch negatives together with hard negatives from term-based retrieval, and multi-hop methods such as MDR (Xiong et al., 2020) largely follow the same training strategy. Such objectives were originally developed for open-domain retrieval, where negatives often come from other queries or documents. In long-context multi-step retrieval, however, the main competitors at inference are chunks from the same context, and the correct target can also depend on previously retrieved evidence. Q-RAG (Sorokin et al., 2026) instead formulates retrieval as a Markov decision process and trains with temporal-difference learning (Mnih et al., 2015). In contrast, S 2LMR retains simple supervised contrastive training while aligning the candidate set, retrieval state, and relative positions with those encountered during inference. 

# 3 METHODS 

3.1 REVISITING SUPERVISED RAG FOR LONG -C ONTEXT MULTI -S TEP RETRIEVAL 

Supervised retrievers remain attractive for multi-step retrieval because they are efficient and can be trained directly from retrieval labels. Two representative approaches are MDR (Xiong et al., 2020) and Beam Retriever (BR) (Zhang et al., 2024). MDR performs sequential dense retrieval with a bi-encoder, while BR scores each new candidate together with the question and previously retrieved passages. These methods were mainly developed for open-domain or relatively small candidate sets. Long-context retrieval is different: the model must search over many passages from the same context, where distractors can be highly similar to the correct evidence. To test how well existing designs transfer to this setting, we first train a bi-encoder with the standard DPR/MDR recipe on the challenging long-context BabiLong QA3 benchmark (Kuratov et al., 2024). It achieves only 1.6% fact exact match (fact-EM), substantially below recent multi-step retrievers. We identify three issues that become especially important as the context and candidate pool grow. 

Issue 1: Training negatives do not match hard same-context distractors. MDR mainly learns from negatives associated with other queries, which are often easier than distractors from the same context. BR uses candidates from the same example, but its original training setting contains only 10–20 candidates, far fewer than the thousands of chunks that may appear in long contexts. Existing training therefore provides limited supervision for distin-guishing the gold passage from many highly similar distractors. Figure 2a illustrates this problem: on our 128 evaluation examples, an MDR-style model ranks at least one same-context distractor above the correct first-hop passage in every case. 

Issue 2: Predicted prefixes make multi-hop supervision noisy. BR trains later hops using pas-sages selected by earlier hops. If an earlier prediction is wrong, later hops are trained on an incorrect retrieval history. This makes the supervision noisier and can reduce the score gap between the gold passage and strong distractors. On 100 held-out QA3 examples with 400k candidates each ( ∼10M tokens), BR-style training gives a negative gold-vs-distractor margin on 12% of examples, with a worst margin of −0.247 , while a matched model trained with ground-truth prefixes flips on only 

4% of examples, with a worst value of −0.034 (Fig. 2b). The problem becomes more serious as the candidate pool grows: from 100 to 400k passages ( ∼10M tokens), the hop-1 failure rate rises from 

0.4% to 10% and end-to-end EM drops from 0.994 to 0.880 , while the matched control remains below 3% (Fig. 2c). 

Issue 3: Retrieval scores ignore the chunk position. Neither MDR nor BR explicitly uses where a candidate appears in the document. This loses useful information in long-context tasks, where rel-evant facts are often positionally related. On BabiLong QA3, the next relevant fact lies within 25% of the previous pick’s position in 90% of examples, with a median offset of only 5%. For example, after retrieving a fact at chunk 48 for the question “Where was the apple before the bathroom?”, the word “before” suggests that the next relevant fact should appear earlier in the document. Aposition-agnostic retriever cannot directly use this signal. It aligns training with inference along three complementary dimensions: the candidate set used for contrastive supervision, the sequential retrieval state conditioned on previous evidence, and the history-adaptive relative positions used to score candidates. # 5 CONCLUSION 

We introduced S 2LMR, a simple supervised bi-encoder for long-context multi-step retrieval. We showed that supervised retrievers fall behind in this setting not because supervision is too weak, but because their training is misaligned with how they are used. By correcting these misalignments, S2LMR matches or outperforms the strongest RL-based retriever at up to 10M tokens, while train-ing substantially faster and remaining robust to hyperparameters. Future work includes extending S2LMR to heterogeneous retrieval corpora with evidence distributed across different sources.

C.1 EMPIRICAL EVIDENCE FOR ISSUE 1 ( DISTRACTOR -DISTRIBUTION MISMATCH )To isolate what MDR-style in-batch training actually optimizes, we compare the encoder’s hop-1 discrimination on the two negative distributions that matter—the training pool (other in-batch queries’ gold chunks) and the inference pool (same-document distractors)—across three check-points: (i) a zero-shot Contriever initialization, (ii) an MDR-style reproduction trained on in-batch negatives, and (iii) the S 2LMR checkpoint trained on same-context negatives. All three share the same encoder, tokenizer, and RoPE positional encoding. Query states are teacher-forced with the true hop-0 answer so measurements isolate per-hop discrimination. The pattern is symmetric at initialization (margin sc =margin ib =0 .06 ) and diverges opposite to in-ference needs under MDR training (margin ib >margin sc ). MDR’s gradient signal itself is strong (median p(gold ) on its in-batch pool is 0.04, implying gradient-magnitude proxy ≈ 0.96 ), so the failure is not gradient collapse but distributional misalignment—the encoder converges to a bound-ary that separates the wrong distribution. Consequently the MDR checkpoint ranks the hop-1 gold above the 99 same-document distractors on 0 of 128 samples, whereas S 2LMR does so on all 128 (Figure 7). C.2 TEACHER FORCING OR NOT : SUPERVISION NOISE VS . EXPOSURE BIAS 

BR-style beam-expanded supervision was originally motivated by an exposure-bias concern: train-ing only on gold prefixes leaves the model unfamiliar with the noisier trajectories it will produce at inference. S2LMR accepts this trade-off because the alternative—training on noisy predicted prefixes—is empirically more harmful in the long-context regime. Fig. 2c and Fig. 3b show that as context length grows, the beam-expanded performance degrades noticeably faster than the teacher-forced curve, while at the training length the two are within 0.04 Fact EM (Figure 2c, Issue 2). The exposure gap that teacher forcing incurs is small enough to be dominated by per-hop InfoNCE against the full same-context pool, which keeps the gold-vs-distractor margin large enough that small prefix perturbations at inference rarely flip the argmax. 18 Preprint

C.3 POSITIONAL BOTTLENECK IN CHUNK -POSITION -AGNOSTIC RETRIEVERS 

This appendix expands the analysis in Sec. 3.1 of the positional-encoding bottleneck in existing supervised multi-hop retrievers. We (i) enumerate the architectural facts that make MDR and BR chunk-position-agnostic, (ii) quantify the positional structure of BabiLong QA3 that these architec-tures cannot exploit, (iii) measure the resulting per-hop retrieval margin, and (iv) show the failure mode is stable across all three hops. C.3.1 ARCHITECTURE : TOKEN -AXIS ONLY POSITIONAL ENCODING 

MDR (Xiong et al., 2020). MDR uses a shared RoBERTa-base bi-encoder to embed candidates and queries independently. Each candidate chunk cj is embedded as Ea(cj ) = RoBERTa( cj ).Only tokens within cj receive positional information via RoBERTa’s absolute learned positional embeddings (maximum position 514). The resulting embedding depends solely on the text of cj and is invariant to cj ’s document-level position. At the query side, MDR concatenates the question with previously retrieved passages and re-encodes. The maximum sequence length is again 514 tokens, and no chunk-level position is exposed. 

BR (Zhang et al., 2024). BR uses a DeBERTa cross-encoder that scores candidates in flat con-catenation [CLS ; Q; ˆ p1; . . . ; ˆ pt−1; cj ; SEP ] (maximum 512 tokens). DeBERTa’s disentangled rela-tive positional encoding operates on token-token offsets within this flat sequence. BR’s Appendix E 

explicitly shuffles the order of concatenated passages during training to enforce robustness to per-mutations. This is architecturally equivalent to training the encoder to be order-invariant across chunks: any positional distinction between the concatenated passages that could have arisen from token positions in the flat sequence is deliberately regularized away. 

Common consequence. In both architectures, the retrieval decision at hop t depends only on the 

textual content of cj and the (question, previous picks) tuple, never on where cj sits in the source document. We call this property chunk-position-agnostic .C.3.2 TASK -SIDE POSITIONAL STRUCTURE IN BABI LONG QA3 BabiLong (Kuratov et al., 2024) embeds bAbI supporting facts as a temporally-ordered subsequence within a long stream of PG-19 noise sentences. Why fine-tuning cannot separate gold from distractors. BabiLong noise chunks are sampled from PG-19 book text but interleaved with bAbI-style distractor sentences that share the same syn-tactic template as the gold facts (subject-verb-location: “ Mary moved to the office. ”). Under In-foNCE fine-tuning, the encoder learns to give high cosine to any (question, bAbI-template sentence about the queried entity) pair. Because the distractors are indistinguishable from golds under a purely semantic similarity criterion — both mention the queried entity, both involve a location and an ac-tion — the fine-tune procedure pushes them to the same region of the embedding space.
