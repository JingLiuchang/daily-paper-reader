Abstract 

Agentic AI platforms-systems in which LLM agents plan, invoke tools, retain memory and coordinate to change the state of enterprise systems-are being procured faster than they can be measured. Public agent benchmarks score models in fixed harnesses; enterprises buy integrated platforms whose orchestration, memory, governance and tooling layers dominate outcomes, and they buy them largely on the strength of vendor demonstrations. We report an industrial evaluation programme (“bakeoff”) run by the innovation laboratory of a global bank in which ten platforms-spanning open-source frameworks, proprietary products and hyperscaler ecosystems-were assessed by a cross-functional panel on nine capability dimensions across three financial-services workloads: counterparty credit risk, retrieval-augmented generation and process automation. Scores separate the field into three tiers that are stable under alternative weightings, and surface workload-specific strengths that aggregates hide. A secondary analysis, however, finds Cronbach’s α = 0 .98 across nominally distinct dimensions and rank correlations ≥ 0.93 

with the total for eight of nine: a halo effect indicating that demonstration-based scoring measures presentation as much as capability. From these lessons we propose a gate-then-score evaluation protocol with four layers (capability, execution reliability, governance gates, business value), trace-level reliability metrics estimated over repeated runs, an autonomy-readiness scale and a reproducibility protocol. We state precisely what the present evidence supports, and what a controlled harness must add to turn enterprise platform evaluation from structured judgement into measurement. 

1. Introduction 

Within three years, LLM agents have progressed from prompting patterns that interleave reasoning and action (Yao et al., 2023b; Wei et al., 2022) to systems that resolve real software issues (Jimenez et al., 2024), operate desktop en-vironments (Xie et al., 2024) and coordinate as teams of specialised roles (Wu et al., 2023; Hong et al., 2024; Four-ney et al., 2024). Vendors have responded with a rapidly expanding catalogue of “agent platforms”, and interoperabil-ity protocols-the Model Context Protocol (MCP) (Anthropic, 2024b) and Agent2Agent (A2A) (Google, 2025)-promise a common substrate for connecting agents to tools and to each other. For a global bank the question is no longer whether such systems are interesting but which of them can be trusted to formulate and execute sequences of state-changing actions inside a regulated operating environment. That question is hard for three reasons. First, the benchmark

2. Background and Formalisation 

2.1. The automation continuum 

RPA , AI agent and agentic AI are routinely conflated. They occupy distinct positions on a continuum defined by how much of the “what” and the “how” of a task is decided at run time rather than fixed at design time (Fig. 1). RPA bots mimic human interaction with application UIs to execute rule-based work (van der Aalst et al., 2018); they are de-terministic and non-invasive but brittle to UI change. A classical agent perceives its environment and acts toward goals (Wooldridge & Jennings, 1995; Russell & Norvig, 2020) within one bounded task. We use agentic AI for systems-often multi-agent-that dynamically plan, adapt and collaborate toward open-ended goals, discovering and in-voking tools and maintaining memory across interactions (Wang et al., 2024b; Xi et al., 2023). A further practical distinction: an agentic workflow fixes control flow at de-sign time and lets the model fill adaptive steps, whereas an agentic system lets the model direct control flow (An-thropic, 2024a). Most enterprise deployments we observed were workflows with agentic steps; the evaluation problem is partly a design-selection problem in which the target is the minimum autonomy that reliably achieves the outcome. 2.2. Working definition 

The Lab’s parent institution defines agentic AI as systems that achieve goals on behalf of a user or another system by autonomously formulating and executing a sequence of actions that invoke other systems and change their state .Two clauses carry the weight: (a) autonomous formulation and execution of an action sequence, and (b) state-changing invocation of other systems. Clause (b) separates agentic AI from conversational assistants and raises its risk profile: errors propagate into records, workflows and downstream decisions (Chan et al., 2023; Shavit et al., 2023). 

2.3. A formal characterisation 

In the vocabulary of sequential decision-making (Kaelbling et al., 1998; Russell & Norvig, 2020), an agentic system operates in an environment with latent state s ∈ S , re-ceives observations o ∈ O and selects actions a ∈ A =

Atool ∪ A msg ∪ A text (tool invocations, messages to agents or humans, text outputs). A single agent is a policy with explicit memory state mt:

at = π(ot, m t; θ), mt+1 = μ(mt, o t, a t), (1) where μ is a memory-management function (in LLM agents, context management plus external stores (Packer et al., 2023; Sumers et al., 2024)) and θ the underlying model. Tool ac-tions induce transitions st+1 ∼ T (· | st, a t), formalising clause (b). Planning selects a1 . . . a n to maximise expected goal attainment; reflection is an inner loop in which the agent evaluates its trajectory and revises behaviour through verbal feedback rather than gradient updates (Shinn et al., 2023). A multi-agent system is a set {πi} with commu-nication topology Γ and an orchestration function routing sub-goals; most multi-agent failures arise in specification and coordination rather than in individual reasoning (Cemri et al., 2025). The formalism is standard; its role here is to state which component each evaluation dimension probes.

3. Related Work 

Agent evaluation has developed along two tracks. 

Model-level benchmarks-AgentBench, GAIA, WebArena, SWE-bench, OSWorld-fix a harness and vary the model. Enterprise-workload benchmarks are more recent: WorkArena evaluates knowledge-work tasks on a live Servi-ceNow instance (Drouin et al., 2024); τ -bench adds multi-turn user interaction under policy constraints and the pass k

reliability metric (Yao et al., 2024); AgentArch compares agent architectures on enterprise tasks and reports at most 

≈35% success on its harder task (Bogavelli et al., 2025); EnterpriseOps-Gym tests multi-step operations with irre-versible actions (ServiceNow & Artificial Analysis, 2026); DRBench targets enterprise deep research (Abaskohi et al., 2026); and a multi-dimensional evaluation framework for enterprise agents has been proposed (Mehta, 2025). Agent-Dojo adds adversarial robustness, including banking tasks (Debenedetti et al., 2024). Measurement-quality critiques (Kapoor et al., 2024; Stroebl et al., 2024) and the long-horizon analysis of Kwa et al. (2025) motivate our reliabil-ity layer. This paper is complementary: it is a buyer-side, platform-level evaluation of commercial products as deliv-ered, inside a regulated institution, with the organisational and governance attributes that public benchmarks abstract away. Its contribution is the protocol and its diagnostics rather than a new task suite.

6.3. Internal consistency and the halo hypothesis 

Treating the nine dimensions as items and ten platforms as cases, Cronbach’s α = 0 .98 and eight of nine dimensions correlate with the total at ρ ≥ 0.93 . Genuinely distinct capabilities-memory management and protocol support, say-should not co-vary this tightly across products of such dif-ferent heritage. The parsimonious explanation is a strong common factor driven by overall demonstration impression: a halo effect (Thorndike, 1920). This does not invalidate the tiering, which deliberation corroborated, but it implies the rubric as administered measured roughly one-and-a-half constructs rather than nine. Two protocol changes follow: require dimension-specific evidence artefacts, and score di-mensions in separate sessions or by specialist sub-panels to break the shared impression.

6.5. Scenario-level disaggregation 

For the top three platforms (supplementary Appendix B, Table A1), H2O.ai’s profile is flat across scenarios with a peak on D9 in CCR (8.75); Google’s D5 is uniformly high (8.67–9.00) while its D6 is uniformly capped at 6.00-a sys-tematic rather than scenario-specific concern; Microsoft’s scores are almost invariant across scenarios, which is itself informative: raters saw little scenario-specific adaptation. 

7. Threats to Validity 

Table 4 states the threats precisely rather than claiming more than the evidence supports. Two deserve emphasis. Assisted conditions : the Lab helped vendors prepare, so the object measured is demonstrated capability under assisted con-ditions, not out-of-the-box performance. Unequal panels :score granularity implies panels of roughly 3–10 raters per vendor (e.g. 7.44 = 67 /9, 4.33 = 13 /3; an inference from decimal patterns), inflating variance for small panels.

In financial services the last two terms often dominate: an automation that saves analyst time but raises the probability of a material misstatement has negative net value. 9.2. Lessons for practitioners 

Three changes raise the evidential standard of enterprise bakeoffs without prohibitive cost: (1) move from vendor-run to buyer-run demonstrations on controlled infrastructure with pinned task definitions; (2) require dimension-specific artefacts-trace logs, evaluation dashboards, memory dumps-so raters score evidence, not narration; (3) retain per-rater data and report reliability statistics alongside means. With these, the taxonomy becomes a repeatable instrument rather than a structured opinion survey. 

10. Conclusion 

Enterprises are adopting a class of technology whose defin-ing property-autonomous, state-changing action-is what reg-ulated institutions must control most tightly. We formalised a bakeoff into a reusable protocol: an organisational screen, a nine-dimension taxonomy grounded in the literature, a scenario portfolio from bespoke analysis to autonomous execution, and a scoring model with reliability diagnostics. Applied to ten platforms it produced a robust three-tier struc-ture in which hyperscaler ecosystems and research-derived platforms led, while exposing-through α = 0 .98 -the halo risk inherent in demonstration-based scoring. The specialist strengths it surfaced (memory at Letta, protocol depth at Microsoft, evaluation tooling at H2O.ai, action grounding at UiPath) are a reminder that “best platform” is workload-relative. We then extended the rubric into a gate-then-score layered model with trace-level metrics and a reproducibility protocol so the next iteration can measure rather than rate. The most consequential finding is procedural: current enter-prise evaluation practice measures presentation more than capability, and the remedy-buyer-controlled tasks, evidence artefacts and measured reliability-is within reach.
