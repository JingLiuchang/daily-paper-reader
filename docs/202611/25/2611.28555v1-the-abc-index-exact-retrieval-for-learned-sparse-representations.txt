# The abc index: Exact Retrieval for Learned Sparse Representations 

# Rossano Venturini 

University of Pisa Pisa, Italy rossano.venturini@unipi.it 

## Abstract 

In learned sparse retrieval (LSR), queries have tens of terms with learned weights, on which classic dynamic pruning is believed to fail, and approximate indexes such as Seismic and HNSW are considered faster than exact ones. We challenge this view with 

abc , an exact index built only from standard IR techniques: term-at-a-time (TAAT) evaluation, Block-Max MaxScore, binary packing and doc-id reordering. They are combined in a new design and engineered for modern CPUs. On MS MARCO encoded with four LSR models, the abc index is the fastest exact index at every 𝑘 :6.5–84 × faster than the exact search of Qdrant , 13–55 × faster than that of Lucene , and 2.9–18 × faster than BMP with an index 5–7 ×

smaller. Against approximate indexes, it takes 1.5–3.6 × less space, and its latency grows much more slowly with 𝑘 : they are up to 4.3 ×

faster at 𝑘 = 10, but at 𝑘 = 1000 it overtakes them, by up to 7.4 ×.Exactness comes with an extra benefit: there are no parameters to tune.

## 1 Introduction 

Learned sparse retrieval (LSR) models such as SPLADE [19, 21] en-code queries and documents as sparse vectors over the vocabulary, and retrieve the 𝑘 documents with the largest inner product with the query. They keep the interpretability and the infrastructure of the inverted index, but they break the assumptions on which decades of query processing were built: queries have tens of terms instead of a handful, and their weights are learned rather than de-rived from term statistics, the “wacky weights” of Mackenzie et al. [29]. On these queries the classic dynamic pruning algorithms, such as WAND and Block-Max WAND, can be slower than scoring every posting [29, 33]. Two families of indexes have answered this challenge. Exact indexes, such as BMP [33], SP [7] and LSP [8], reorganize the col-lection into blocks of documents with precomputed score bounds, at the price of indexes several times larger than the data. Ap-proximate indexes, such as Seismic [5, 34] and graph indexes like HNSW [11, 31], give up the guarantee of exact results, and trade re-call for speed through parameters tuned for every collection, model and depth 𝑘 . The two families have been compared at length, and the received view is that approximate indexes are faster than exact ones [5, 11, 34, 41]. This paper challenges that view with classical IR alone. We present abc , an exact index for LSR with no parameters, built only from standard techniques: term-at-a-time (TAAT) evaluation [43], Block-Max MaxScore [9, 14], binary packing [24] and doc-id re-ordering by graph bisection [12]. What makes the difference is how they are combined and engineered for modern CPUs. Take TAAT, considered in the research literature inferior to document-at-a-time (DAAT) evaluation because its accumulators do not fit in cache [18]. Cut the doc-id space into aligned blocks whose accumulators stay in the L1 cache, and store each posting as a 16-bit offset that is directly its accumulator slot: scoring a posting then needs no decoding and no comparison, only a multiply-add into a slot that is already in cache. On top of this loop, dynamic pruning over posting lists pays off again, wacky weights notwithstanding. Our abc-bmms runs Block-Max MaxScore inside every block, with maxima aligned across terms, so that the bounds of the query terms add up, and with a cost model that decides, block by block, which lists to read in full and how to complete the few candidates that may still enter the top 

𝑘 . Pruning makes it always faster than the exhaustive abc , by up to 4.1 ×, whereas Lucene ’s Block-Max MaxScore scorer, which also prunes window by window, is almost always slower than Lucene ’s own exhaustive evaluation in our experiments. Our indexes compute every score from the stored weights: with 8-bit weights, as in the other block-based indexes, they lose less than 1% of recall, and with the original 32-bit weights they return the exact top-𝑘 , for at most 40% more time than with 8-bit weights. An f32 abc index, built on the fly, thus also computes the exact ground truth of a query set, the reference against which LSR models and approximate indexes are measured, 27–52 × faster than a brute-force scan. There is nothing to tune: every model is indexed the same way, in seconds, and one index serves every depth 𝑘 , whereas 

Seismic needs a grid search and a different index for every 𝑘 , and HNSW takes hours to build. We evaluate our indexes on MS MARCO encoded with four LSR models, from the historical CoCondenser to the recent OS-gte, SparseUp and SPLARE, against the best exact and approximate indexes. Our contributions are the following. 

• Exhaustive TAAT over cache-resident blocks, built in seconds (abc ). Our index turns TAAT, believed to lose to DAAT on cache misses, into a loop that stays in the L1 cache, with a compressed index smaller than those of Qdrant 

and Lucene . The doc-id reordering, which makes the index smaller, no longer dominates the build: our graph bisection arXiv:2611.28555v1 [cs.IR] 21 Nov 2026 Rossano Venturini 

reorders the collection 19.5 × faster than rgbisect [27], with compressed doc-ids only 1.2% larger. 

• Block-Max MaxScore inside the TAAT loop: the fastest exact index ( abc-bmms ). Lucene also prunes posting lists window by window with Block-Max MaxScore, and there pruning slows it down. Inside our blocks it pays off: abc-bmms is 13– 55 × faster than Lucene and 6.5–84 × faster than the sparse index of Qdrant [38], and the fastest exact index on all four datasets and at every 𝑘 ; against BMP it is 2.9–18 × faster with an index 5–7 × smaller. 

• Exact, yet competitive with approximate indexes. At 𝑘 =

10, Seismic and HNSW at 99% recall are up to 4.3 × faster than abc-bmms , with indexes 1.5–3.6 × larger, but their cost grows faster with 𝑘 . At 𝑘 = 100 they are at most 2.4 × faster, and abc-bmms is the fastest on OS-gte, where HNSW does not reach 99% recall, and faster than Seismic 

on SparseUp. At 𝑘 = 1000 abc-bmms is the fastest index on all four datasets, 1.7–7.4 × faster than Seismic , and it is built in 13–32 s instead of the 9–27 hours of HNSW. Exactness and space succinctness come with the extra benefit that no parameter tuning is needed. 

## 2 Background and Related Work 

Learned sparse retrieval. LSR models encode queries and docu-ments as sparse vectors over the vocabulary of a language model [19, 21]. Relevance is the inner product, so retrieval is top-𝑘 maximum inner-product search over sparse vectors, and the natural data struc-ture is the inverted index, whose posting lists can be compressed with the techniques surveyed by Pibiri and Venturini [37]. Learned weights break the assumptions of classic query processing: queries have tens of terms, weights are large on frequent terms, and doc-uments are expanded with terms they do not contain. Mackenzie et al. call them “wacky weights”, on which “few documents can actually be skipped if exact query evaluation is desired” [29]. 

DAAT and dynamic pruning. Document-at-a-time (DAAT) pro-cessing advances one cursor per query term in doc-id order and scores one document at a time. Dynamic pruning skips the docu-ments that cannot enter the top 𝑘 , using per-term upper bounds. MaxScore [43] splits the terms into essential ones, read in full, and 

non-essential ones, whose total contribution is too small to bring a document into the top 𝑘 on its own. The non-essential terms are read only for the few documents that may still enter it. WAND [4] pivots on the prefix sums of the bounds, and Block-Max WAND [15] adds per-block maxima. Block-Max MaxScore [9, 14] runs MaxS-core inside doc-id intervals, with tighter bounds for each interval. Dimopoulos et al. [14] already compute these bounds over fixed ranges of doc-ids whose size is a power of two, as abc-bmms does (Section 4). Anytime ranking visits ranges of doc-ids in decreasing order of their bound [28]. 

Block-based pruning for LSR. BMP [33] partitions the reordered doc-id space into blocks of a few documents, stores 8-bit block maxima per term and, per block, inverted lists of the block’s post-ings, and scores the blocks by decreasing upper bound until the threshold exceeds the next bound. Each block is scored term at a time, into one accumulator per document of the block. SP [7] groups blocks into superblocks pruned before their blocks, and 

LSP [8] visits the most promising superblocks with 4-bit maxima and a forward index. In their rank-safe settings they are an order of magnitude faster than the MaxScore of PISA [32] on SPLADE, but their indexes are several times larger than the data. RISE [39], a Rust re-implementation of DS2I [36] about as fast as PISA , offers the same document-at-a-time algorithms, MaxScore and Block-Max MaxScore among them. 

Approximate retrieval. Seismic [5, 34] keeps the top entries of each posting list, clusters them into blocks summarized by quan-tized max-vectors, skips the blocks whose summary score is below a fraction of the threshold, and scores the others with a forward index. Graph indexes such as HNSW [31], implemented for sparse vectors in kANNolo [11] and in the winners of the BigANN’23 sparse track [41], are the other family. Both trade recall for speed through parameters chosen by a grid search on each collection [6]. 

Term-at-a-time. Term-at-a-time (TAAT) processing adds one posting list at a time into an accumulator per document. DAAT came to dominate research [42]. On large memory-resident indexes, the cache misses of TAAT increase drastically, which Fontoura et al. attribute to “random access over the large array of accumula-tors” [18]. The research literature studies TAAT with accumulators over the whole collection or over a pruned subset of it [18]. Cache-sized windows of doc-ids appear in DAAT, but only to hold a bitmap of live blocks [13]. Search engines took another road: Lucene scores disjunctions term at a time in small windows of doc-ids, whose accumulators stay in cache [1], and so does Qdrant [38]. The com-pressed sparse index of Qdrant scores every posting, in windows that start at the smallest current doc-id of the query lists, so ev-ery posting must be rebased to its window. In abc , instead, the blocks are fixed and aligned, and the 16-bit offset of a posting is directly its accumulator slot. Lucene ’s Block-Max MaxScore scorer prunes the windows, splitting the clauses anew in every window and completing the candidates term at a time [2]. Our abc-bmms 

also prunes window by window, but differs in three choices. The offset of a posting is its accumulator slot. The 8-bit maxima, one for every 1024 doc-ids, are aligned across terms, so that the bounds of the query terms add up ( Lucene ’s cover chunks of postings, so each list’s bound must be recomputed for every window). A cost model decides which lists to skip, and whether to probe the few candidates in a list or to add the list whole (Section 4). To our knowledge, no published study measures TAAT over cache-sized windows of global posting lists, with or without pruning, against DAAT, block-based or approximate LSR indexes. 

## 3 The Exhaustive Index abc 

Classic TAAT keeps one accumulator per document: each posting list is read sequentially, but its updates land at random positions of an array as large as the collection, and on large indexes the cache misses grow sharply [18]. Lucene and Qdrant avoid these misses by evaluating the query on one small window of doc-ids at a time, whose accumulators stay in cache. Both use what we call 

soft windowing : the window exists only at query time, and for each term the search reads postings until the next one falls outside the window. The search must thus find where the window ends in the The abc index: Exact Retrieval for Learned Sparse Representations 

list of each term and bring every doc-id to its position within it. Soft windowing is usually paired with a partitioning of the posting lists by cardinality, for example into chunks of a fixed number of postings, none of which corresponds to a window. Our index abc uses instead what we call hard windowing : the windows are fixed, aligned intervals of the doc-id space, the same for every term, and they are also the blocks in which every posting list is stored. The postings that a term has in a window are thus known in advance, and the search does not have to find where the window ends. A posting does not store its block either, only its position within it, which is already its slot in the accumulator. In exhaustive search, this keeps the work per posting simple for the CPU: no comparison with the window and no shift, only its contribution added to its slot. This already speeds up exhaustive search, and hard windows will be crucial for dynamic pruning (Section 4). Hard windows cost negligible space: stored with known tech-niques, the compressed index of abc is smaller than that of Qdrant 

(Section 3.2), and its search is several times faster than that of 

Qdrant with the same weights and an order of magnitude faster than that of Lucene (Section 3.3). All the measurements of this sec-tion are on MS MARCO [35] encoded with the CoCondenser-based SPLADE model [19, 22] (Section 6.1). 

## 3.1 Term-at-a-time over hard windows 

In TAAT over windows, the window is the range of doc-ids covered by the array of accumulators, and it is convenient to make it coincide with the blocks into which the doc-id universe is partitioned. The universe is cut into blocks of 𝐵 consecutive doc-ids, the same for all the terms, and the array has one slot per doc-id of a block. In this way, a posting stores only its offset within its block, since the list is stored block by block; its doc-id is block · 𝐵 + offset , and its offset is directly the index of its slot. The width 𝐵 thus fixes the size of the accumulator, and we choose it for the accumulator to stay in the L1 cache. We fix 𝐵 = 213 , the fastest value in our experiments: an accumulator of 32-bit floats takes 32 KiB and fits in the 48 KiB L1 data cache of our machine, and an offset fits in 16 bits. A query is evaluated one block at a time, in ascending order, visiting only the blocks in which its terms have postings. Within a block, the query terms are taken one after the other, and the contri-bution 𝑞 𝑡 𝑑 𝑡 of each of their postings is added to its slot, where 𝑞 𝑡 

and 𝑑 𝑡 are the weights of term 𝑡 in the query and in the document. Once all the terms are done, the accumulator holds the exact score of every document of the block. A last pass over the array of accu-mulators then moves these scores to a heap that keeps the best 𝑘 

documents seen so far, and prepares the accumulator for the next block. The threshold of the heap is −∞ until the heap is full, and then the lowest score it holds. This last pass reads every slot of every visited block, so it must cost little. In its simplest form, it offers each slot to the heap, which accepts it only if it exceeds the threshold, and it resets the slot to zero as it reads it, so the accumulator needs no separate clearing. No slot is tested against zero: documents with a zero score are discarded when the results are collected. Every slot still costs a comparison with the threshold, and SIMD instructions make it cheaper. An AVX2 register holds 8 scores of 32 bits, so four SIMD comparisons check 32 slots at once, with a single branch. Only when this branch is taken does the scan look for the groups of 8 that hold a score above the threshold, and hand their slots to the heap. Once the heap is full the branch is almost never taken, so it is well predicted. Each slot is still reset in the same pass. 

## 3.2 The abc memory layout 

The representation of a posting list consists of three parts: the blocks in which the term has postings, the offsets of its postings within each of these blocks, and their weights. It is inspired by the elegant representation of Elias and Fano [16, 17], from which quasi-succinct indices [45], partitioned Elias-Fano [36] and Roaring bitmaps [10] also drew inspiration: every value is divided into a high part, which selects a bucket of the universe, and a low part, its position within the bucket. In abc the high part is the block, implicit in the structure of the list, and the low part is the offset. Figure 1 shows the layout on one term. We first describe the layout that stores the offsets uncompressed, which is the fastest, and then its compressed variant. 

Used blocks. To know which blocks to visit, each term has a bitvector with one bit per block, set when the term has postings in the block, and the bitwise OR of the bitvectors of the query terms gives the blocks to visit. The bitvectors take negligible space: with CoCondenser, about 4 MiB in all, 0.1% of the index of abc .

The postings of a block. Each block of a posting list is stored as the offsets of its postings, sorted, in 16 bits each, and their weights in the same order. During a query, the blocks are visited in ascending order and each posting list is read from left to right, so every offset that is read belongs to the block being visited. The bitvector of the term tells whether its list has postings in that block, and a small array of block ranges stores where every block of a list begins, so its length is the distance between its start and the next one. Each term thus reads its list with a cursor that only moves forward, and reaching the next block requires no search. Adding a block of a list to the accumulator is a tight loop over its offsets and weights, with no branches on the data. In Figure 1, the cursor of 𝑡 reads the four postings of block 0, adding the contribution of weight 83 to slot 147, skips block 1, and reads the two postings of block 2 and the three of block 5; block 3 is a different case, which we discuss below. 

Weights. The contribution of a posting comes from its weight, stored as an 8-bit integer ( u8 ), a 16-bit float ( f16 ) or a 32-bit float (f32 ). With 8 bits, each posting list has its own affine quantization 

𝑑 𝑡 ≈ 𝑚 𝑡 + 𝑟 𝑠 𝑡 , where 𝑟 is the stored integer. The query weight is folded into the two parameters of the list once per query, 𝑞 𝑡 𝑑 𝑡 ≈(𝑞 𝑡 𝑚 𝑡 ) + 𝑟 (𝑞 𝑡 𝑠 𝑡 ), so each posting costs a single multiply-add. With 32-bit weights the stored weights are the original ones, so the search returns the true top-𝑘 , and with 8-bit weights it loses less than 1% of recall on all our models. On CoCondenser, 16-bit weights lose about 0.1%, and against 8-bit weights abc takes 2–5% more time with 16-bit weights and 36–40% more with 32-bit ones (Table 1).

when the block is dense. The density of a block of a list is the fraction of the doc-ids of the block in which the term occurs. A dense block is therefore stored in dense format : one weight for every doc-id of the block, 0 for the documents without the term, and no offsets. The weight zero is free to mark the absent documents, because 8-bit weights are quantized to the levels 1 , . . . , 255 and a 16-or 32-bit weight equal to zero contributes nothing; a dense block is thus added to the accumulator with one sequential, vectorized pass. In Figure 1, block 3 is stored in dense format. A block is stored in dense format when its density makes this format no larger than offsets and weights: 1/2 with 16-bit weights and 2/3 with 32-bit ones. With 8-bit weights the threshold is 1/4 instead of 1/3; between the two, time and space change by less than 1%. Dense blocks become more frequent when the doc-ids are reordered so that documents that often occur in the same posting lists are close. We use the recursive graph bisection of Dhulipala et al. [12], in the faster version of Mackenzie et al. [27], which we improve in Section 5. The postings of a term then gather in fewer, denser blocks, and this is where the dense format pays off. On CoCondenser with 8-bit weights, 0.4% of the blocks of lists are dense, and they hold 23% of the postings, against 0.02% without reordering. The dense format saves 4.8% of the space of abc and 10–11% of its query time, while without reordering it changes neither. Quasi-succinct indices, partitioned Elias-Fano and Roaring bitmaps also switch to a dense representation where the values are dense; our dense format, in addition, carries the weights. 

Compressed layout. In the sparse format, every offset still takes 16 bits. Binary packing [24] compresses a sorted sequence by cutting the gaps between consecutive values into groups, usually of 128, and storing each group with the number of bits of its largest gap. In the compressed variant of the index, abc-packed , each block of a list is stored as a header with its first offset and its length, 16 bits each, followed by its gaps, bit-packed in groups of 8 instead of 128, because within a block a term has few postings. A group is decoded with a handful of SIMD instructions and no branches, and the search reads the blocks of a list in the same order, with the same forward-only cursor. In abc-packed , dense blocks are stored in sparse format too, because their gaps are small and take a few bits. With the threshold of abc , the dense format would make the compressed index 13% larger. 

The cost of hard windows. Besides the bitvectors, hard windows add to the index only where each block of a list ends. In abc this is a 4-byte entry in the block ranges. In abc-packed it is the 16-bit length in the header of the block; the other half of the header, the first offset, belongs to a posting. On CoCondenser, a block of a list holds 44 postings on average, and 65 with reordered doc-ids, so this information takes less than 0.1 bytes per posting and 2–3% of the index, depending on the layout and on reordering: negligible space. Even with it, the index of abc-packed is smaller than that of Qdrant and, with reordered doc-ids, than that of Lucene , whose index is reordered too.

Space. Qdrant and Lucene divide each posting list into chunks of a fixed number of postings, 128 in Qdrant and 256 in Lucene , and bit-pack all the gaps of a full chunk with a single width, whereas 

abc-packed uses one width for every 8 gaps. Against Qdrant , whose 8-bit weights also take one byte per posting, the difference lies in the doc-ids, which abc-packed packs with finer widths. Everything but the weights takes 10.1 bits per posting in Qdrant , 8.9 in abc-packed and 6.3 with reordered doc-ids, while abc stores each offset in 16 bits. Against Lucene , which also compresses its weights, only the total sizes can be compared. With 8-bit weights, reordering makes the index of abc-packed 15% smaller. It helps Qdrant too: on the reordered collection its index is 10% smaller and its search 15% faster. With both reordered, abc-packed is about 4 × faster than

The work of soft windows. At query time, soft windows cost 

Qdrant and Lucene work that hard windows remove. For every window and every term, they must find where the window ends in a chunk and bring every doc-id to its slot. The soft windows of Qdrant are also floating : a window starts at the smallest doc-id not yet read among the lists of the query. Qdrant finds where the window ends in a chunk with a binary search, and subtracts the start of the window from every doc-id. Lucene finds the end of the window by searching the chunk for the first doc-id past it, and brings every doc-id to its position within the window. Neither is needed in abc , where a window is a block of the index. On the uncompressed abc without the dense format, replacing the hard windows with floating windows like those of Qdrant , with the same sum of the contributions, the same scan and the same heap, makes the search about 30% slower. 

The inner loop. By inner loop we mean the work that every window repeats: adding the contribution of each posting to its slot, and scanning the accumulator at the end. Two choices in this loop also count, and they do not depend on the windows: how the contribution of a posting is computed, and how the accumulator is scanned. In abc each posting costs one multiply-add straight into its slot. Qdrant and Lucene instead go through the postings of a chunk twice, with a buffer in between. Qdrant first dequantizes the weights of the chunk into the buffer, and then multiplies each of them by the query weight and adds it to its slot. Lucene first computes the score of every posting of the chunk into the buffer, applying its similarity to the stored weights, and then adds each score to its slot. As for the scan, abc in its simple form offers each slot to the heap and resets it in the same pass (Section 3.1). Qdrant 

instead zeroes its array of accumulators before scoring a window, and then tests every slot with a branch that depends on the data, keeping the score if it is non-zero and above the threshold. We ported our simple scan into Qdrant , and it made Qdrant about 2.5 times faster. Folding the query weight into the quantization of 

Qdrant , instead, did not make it faster. However, our version of 

Qdrant is still 1.4–1.5 × slower than abc-packed with the same scan. This is probably because of soft windowing, which alone makes 

abc about 30% slower. 

## 4 Dynamic Pruning with Block-Max MaxScore 

Exhaustive abc scores every document of every block that contains a query term, although only the best 𝑘 matter. Dynamic pruning would like to skip this work, using upper bounds on the scores, but on learned sparse representations it is known to pay off little, or even to hurt. On SPLADE, WAND and Block-Max WAND are slower than scoring every posting [29, 30], and MaxScore is slower than an exhaustive score-at-a-time evaluation [33]. The two production-ready engines of Section 3.3 confirm it. Lucene prunes with Block-Max MaxScore, window by window [2], and in our experiments it is almost always slower than its own exhaustive evaluation, by up to 2.7 × (Section 6.3). ## 4.1 One byte per sub-block 

To bound the scores, each block of 8192 doc-ids is divided into 8 sub-blocks of 1024 doc-ids, fixed and aligned like the blocks. For every block of a list, the index stores the largest weight of the term in each of its 8 sub-blocks, quantized to one byte and rounded up, and 0 for a sub-block without postings of the term. The 8 maxima are stored next to the block ranges, so the cursor that reaches the postings of a block of a list also reaches its maxima (Figure 2). Nothing else changes: postings, used-block bitvectors and dense blocks are those of abc .The maxima are stored only for the blocks of a list that its bitvec-tor marks, 8 bytes each, whatever the number of their postings. On CoCondenser they take 124 MiB, 4% of the index. Doc-id reordering keeps this cost low, because it gathers the postings of a term in 32% fewer blocks; without it, the maxima would take about 184 MiB. 

BMP [33] keeps one 8-bit maximum for every term and every block of 𝑏 documents, zero meaning absent. Blocks of 8 or 16 docu-ments give tight bounds, and on CoCondenser their maxima take 30.0 and 15.0 GB at 𝑏 = 8 and 16, and 5.5 and 4.1 GB compressed [33]; 

LSP reports 7.6 GB for a sparse layout at 𝑏 = 8 [8]. Our maxima cover 1024 documents instead of 8 or 16, and the finer pruning happens inside the blocks (Section 4.3). 

## 4.2 Visit the promising blocks first 

Before reading any posting, the search sums the maxima of the query terms. One ascending pass over the blocks of the query lists, the same pass that exhaustive abc makes, adds to each sub-block the query weight of every term times its maximum there. The result is the bound of the sub-block, and the bound of a block is that of its best sub-block. The same pass records where the postings of every list begin in every block, so the blocks can be visited in any order. The blocks are then visited in decreasing order of their bound, as in BMP [33]; with 1,080 blocks on MS MARCO, sorting them takes negligible time. The first blocks fill the heap with documents of high score, so the threshold 𝜃 , the 𝑘 -th best score found so far, rises quickly. As soon as the bound of the next block falls below 𝜃 , no document in it or in any later block can enter the top 𝑘 , and the search stops. Inside a visited block, the sub-blocks whose bound is below 𝜃 are skipped. The main effect of this order is a high 𝜃 from the first blocks on. Most blocks are still visited, 626 of the 1,080 on CoCondenser at 

𝑘 = 10, because queries have 44 terms on average and most blocks hold a sub-block whose bound exceeds 𝜃 . Most of the work must therefore be saved inside the blocks. 

## 4.3 MaxScore, one block at a time 

Inside a visited block, the search applies MaxScore to the lists of the query. The bound of a list in the block is the query weight of its term times its largest maximum over the sub-blocks not skipped. The lists whose bounds sum to less than 𝜃 can be declared non-essential in the block, because a document that occurs only in them cannot enter the top 𝑘 . The essential lists are added whole to the accumulator, as in exhaustive abc . A single vectorized pass over the accumulator then keeps as candidates only the documents whose partial score, plus what the non-essential lists can still add in their sub-block, can reach 𝜃 . The test uses the maxima of the lists in the sub-block of the document, which are tighter than the bounds of the lists. All the other documents are dropped without reading a single non-essential posting. Finally, as in the term-at-a-time MaxScore of Turtle and Flood [18, 43] and in Lucene , the non-essential lists are added to the candidates one at a time, and after each list a candidate is dropped when the lists still missing can no longer bring it to 𝜃 .The candidates that remain after the last list enter the heap with their exact scores, and when none remains earlier, the other lists are not read at all. The split is redone in every block, with the current 𝜃 and the bounds of the lists in that block, so a term with large weights in a few blocks is essential there and non-essential everywhere else. Term-at-a-time processing makes this cheap, because the split is decided once per block of 8192 documents and the candidates are slots of an accumulator of 32 KiB that stays in cache. Lucene also redoes the split in every window [2], but it first derives the bound of every list from the maxima of the chunks that overlap the window, while in abc-bmms the bound of a list is the largest of its maxima in the sub-blocks not skipped. On CoCondenser at 𝑘 = 10, the split inside the blocks makes the search 3.0 × faster than block and sub-block pruning alone. 

Exactness. A block, a sub-block or a document is dropped only when an upper bound of its score is below 𝜃 , and 𝜃 never decreases, so the result is the exact top-𝑘 of exhaustive abc , up to ties. The maxima are rounded up, so a bound is never below the weights it covers, and 𝜃 is lowered by a relative slack of 10 −4 that absorbs the different summation orders of bounds and scores. Query weights are assumed non-negative, as in SPLADE; with a negative weight the bounds would not hold. 

## 4.4 Choosing what not to read 

Any set of lists whose bounds sum below 𝜃 can be non-essential, so the split is a choice, and it decides how much a block costs. Two things can be chosen: the threshold below which the bounds of the non-essential lists must sum, and the lists themselves. Any threshold 𝜏 ≤ 𝜃 keeps the search exact, and all the imple-mentations of MaxScore we know of use 𝜏 = 𝜃 . A lower 𝜏 seems wasteful, because fewer lists can be non-essential, but it can pay off. Take a block with 𝜃 = 10 and three lists: A, essential, with bound 6, and two long lists B and C, with bounds 5 and 4.9. With 𝜏 = 𝜃 , B and C are both non-essential, and a document needs a partial score of only 0.1 from A to be a candidate. Textbook MaxScore, RISE and PISA take as non-essential the terms with the smallest bounds. Lucene sorts its clauses by a ratio of bound to cost, the bound of a clause in the window over the length of its whole list [2], and takes them greedily. We solve the knapsack with the same kind of greedy choice, but our ratio counts only the postings of the list in the block: the lists are sorted by their bound per post-ing in the block, and the non-essential ones are chosen greedily in this order, keeping the sum of their bounds below 𝜏 . With our 𝜏 ,this choice makes the search 6–8% faster than taking the smallest bounds, while with 𝜏 = 𝜃 it gains at most 4%, because the smaller threshold keeps the candidates few. Choosing must also cost little. Bounding and sorting every list of a visited block costs about as much as reading a few postings per list, and many of these lists are never read. Once per query, the lists are therefore sorted by their bound per posting over the whole list, and the pass of Section 4.2 that computes the bounds of the sub-blocks also sums, for each sub-block, the bounds of three groups: the 30%, 50% and 70% of the lists with the smallest bound per posting. In a block, the largest of these groups whose bound stays below half of 𝜏 is non-essential as a whole, and its lists are bounded and sorted only if candidates are still left after the other non-essential lists. On CoCondenser this makes the search 16% faster at 𝑘 = 10, and 2% at 𝑘 = 1000. 

## 4.5 Completing the few candidates 

After the essential lists, very few documents of a block are still candidates: on CoCondenser, on average 14.4, 21.4 and 42.5 out of 8192 at 𝑘 = 10, 100 and 1000. Adding a non-essential list whole to the accumulator would read thousands of postings to update a handful of documents. Since the offsets of a list in a block are sorted, the search can instead probe the list, looking up each candidate with a binary search, or with a single load in a dense block. For each list, it chooses the cheaper way. It probes when the number of candidates times the number of steps of a binary search is well below the number of postings of the list, |cand | · (⌊ log 2 len ⌋ + 1) · 8 < len , and adds the list whole otherwise. On CoCondenser, probing makes the search 14–15% faster. To run out of candidates early, the non-essential lists are taken in decreasing order of their bound per posting. Candidates run out quickly, and the lists that come after are never read: on CoCon-denser, 90%, 84% and 70% of the non-essential lists at 𝑘 = 10, 100 and 1000. ## 4.6 A compressed abc-bmms 

A compressed abc-bmms needs a format that keeps random access, so that a probe can jump to the right place in a block of a list. In 

abc-packed (Section 3.2) the gaps can only be decoded in order from the start of the block, so our compressed format arranges them differently. It cuts the postings of a block of a list into groups of 9: the first offset of each group is stored in 16 bits, followed by the 8 gaps to the next offsets, bit-packed with a width chosen for the group. A probe finds the group of a candidate by a binary search on the first offsets, and decodes only that group, with the SIMD decoder of abc-packed . Storing a width per group comes almost for free. The 8 gaps of a group cannot add up to more than the distance Rossano Venturini 

between its first offset and the next one, which bounds the width; and since an offset takes only 13 of its 16 bits, the 3 spare bits of each first offset tell how much narrower than this bound the group actually is. Every 8 groups, the start of the group is also stored, so a probe reaches any group by adding at most 7 widths. Dense blocks stay in dense format, unlike in abc-packed , because a probe reads them with a single load. The compressed variant, abc-bmms-packed , returns the same results. On CoCondenser it takes 24% less space than abc-bmms ,2,349 against 3,109 MiB, and it is at most 11% slower: 1,199, 2,120 and 3,776 μs at 𝑘 = 10, 100 and 1000, against 1,078, 1,906 and 3,459. Against abc-packed (1,814 MiB, reordered), the bounds and the random access cost 29% more space and make the search 4.9, 2.9 and 1.7 times faster at 𝑘 = 10, 100 and 1000. 

## 5 Faster Doc-id Reordering 

Reordering the doc-ids so that documents that often occur in the same posting lists are close pays off in every part of abc . On CoCon-denser it makes the doc-ids of abc-packed about a third smaller, it is what makes dense blocks appear (Section 3.2), and it gathers the postings of a term in 32% fewer blocks, which in abc-bmms means fewer maxima and tighter bounds (Section 4). The standard method is recursive graph bisection (BP) [12]. BP splits the documents into two halves and refines the split with swap rounds . In each round it estimates, for every document, how much the log-gap cost Í𝑡 

Í𝑖 log 2 (𝑑 𝑡 𝑖 +1 − 𝑑 𝑡 𝑖 ) would decrease if the docu-ment moved to the other half, and swaps the pairs of documents with the largest gains. After up to 20 rounds it recurses on both halves, down to nodes of 16 documents. It takes into account only the lists of at least 4096 documents, and every round reads all their postings. Its fastest public implementation, the rgbisect crate [27], takes 1.7 minutes on CoCondenser on 20 threads, plus half a minute to prepare its input, while the rest of the abc build takes seconds. We rewrote BP around three ideas. 

The real cost of an order. Log-gap is the cost of an ideal gap coder, while abc-packed pays, for each group of 8 gaps, the width of the largest one, and a header for each block of a list. Moving documents inside a window of a few hundred doc-ids changes the gaps but rarely the widths, so the index barely notices, while log-gap keeps rewarding such moves. Refining the order inside small windows thus costs full passes over the data and gains almost nothing. We therefore choose every parameter of the bisection by the exact size of the index it produces, computed from the posting lists in a few seconds. With this measure, the recursion stops at nodes of 256 documents instead of 16, which saves four levels without enlarging the index. The blocks of abc suggest a second choice: a node larger than a block of 𝐵 = 213 documents is split at the multiple of 𝐵 nearest to its middle. Every block is then one subtree of the recursion, and its documents come from one group of similar documents. 

Sample, then rank. Swap rounds converge slowly, and the split itself matters less than it seems. In our experiments, splits that differ in a large share of their documents give indexes of similar size, as long as each half gathers documents that share terms. Cheap passes. The pass that scores the documents is bound not by memory but by the chain of floating-point additions that sums the gains of the terms of a document. We compute the gain of each term once per round, as a 16-bit integer, so the gains of a document are summed in four independent integer accumulators, and the pass becomes about twice as fast. Integer sums do not depend on their order either, so the order produced is the same for any number of threads, and so is the index. The documents are laid out contiguously, and the layout is rebuilt at every level, so that every pass reads memory sequentially.

## 6 Experiments 

We first compare our four indexes with each other, and then abc-bmms and abc-bmms-packed with the exact and the approximate indexes for learned sparse retrieval. We use four datasets, encoded by four learned sparse models that we chose for their different characteristics. Among the exact indexes, abc-bmms is the fastest on every dataset and at every 𝑘 , and abc-bmms-packed takes about as much space as Qdrant , Lucene and the other indexes over posting lists. Compression. The compressed abc-packed is 24–71% slower than 

abc , the cost of decoding the doc-id gaps, and takes 37–40% less space. With pruning, compression costs much less: abc-bmms-packed is at most 19% slower than abc-bmms , because Block-Max MaxScore decodes far fewer postings, and takes about a quarter less space. 

## 6.3 Comparison with the exact indexes 

Since compression costs little with pruning, Table 5 reports both 

abc-bmms and abc-bmms-packed , with the other indexes, on the four datasets at 𝑘 = 10, 100 and 1000. We start from the exact indexes, after checking which of them are exact. 

Some exact indexes are not so exact. BMP , SP , LSP and IOQP are designed to be exact, but they lose recall for three reasons, all in the implementation rather than in the algorithm. Query weights are quantized to few levels (the largest to 32 in BMP , SP and IOQP , to 128 in LSP ): on CoCondenser and OS-gte, where nothing overflows, this alone limits their recall, at any 𝑘 , to 0.966–0.978 for BMP and 

SP and to 0.985–0.989 for LSP . Block maxima on 4 bits, as in the paper of LSP , are not upper bounds of the scores: at 𝑘 = 10, the results of between 670 (CoCondenser) and 3,005 (OS-gte) of the 6,980 queries differ from those of the same engine without pruning. Scores, bounds or thresholds on 16 bits overflow on the long queries of recent models: on SparseUp the original BMP reaches a recall@10 of 0.38, SP 0.22 and LSP 0.75; on SPLARE, whose 123,627 dimensions do not fit in 16-bit term ids, LSP stays below 0.75. We removed these causes in BMP and IOQP , and the first two in LSP , and we report these versions. We left the overflow in LSP and every cause in SP :removing them would only widen scores and bounds, which would not make either faster, and both are slower than abc-bmms at every 

𝑘 . We report them with their own recall, which for SP stays below that of exact search on every dataset, and for LSP on SparseUp and SPLARE, where it overflows; SP also misses documents in 10 queries at 𝑘 = 1000. With our fixes, BMP and IOQP , like RISE , Qdrant and 

Lucene , are exact: their recall differs from that of abc-bmms only because each quantizes the weights in its own way. 

Time. Our abc-bmms is the fastest exact index on every dataset and at every 𝑘 . With the original 32-bit weights, which give the true top-𝑘 , abc-bmms-packed is also faster than every other exact index with 8-bit weights. Against the indexes over blocks of documents, 

BMP , SP and LSP , the margin widens at large 𝑘 , because the number of blocks they score grows with 𝑘 : against the fastest of them, where all three can be built, it is 1.9–4.1 × at 𝑘 = 10 and 100, and 3.1– 7.1 × at 𝑘 = 1000. Against the indexes over posting lists, Qdrant ,

Lucene , RISE and IOQP , the margin is 6.5–84 ×, and it shrinks with 

𝑘 , because most of them score every posting, at a cost that does not depend on 𝑘 . On SPLARE, where BMP and SP cannot be built, the fastest of the other exact indexes is Qdrant , 6.5–20 × slower than 

abc-bmms .

Pruning in the other indexes. In the other indexes, pruning does not always pay off. Lucene ’s Block-Max MaxScore is slower than its own exhaustive evaluation, by up to 2.7 ×, on all datasets and at every 𝑘 , except on OS-gte at 𝑘 ≤ 100. ## 6.4 Comparison with the approximate indexes 

We now compare abc-bmms with the approximate indexes of Ta-ble 5, Seismic and kANNolo .

Time. We run Seismic and kANNolo at the fastest setting whose recall@ 𝑘 reaches 0.99, just below that of exact search with 8-bit weights (Table 3). On OS-gte, kANNolo does not reach 0.99 at any 𝑘 

(0.981–0.985), nor does Seismic with a budget of 1 .5× on SparseUp at 𝑘 = 10 and 1000. At 𝑘 = 10, Seismic with a budget of 2 × is 1.1– 4.2 × faster than abc-bmms , and kANNolo 1.5–4.3 × faster, except on OS-gte, where it is 19 × slower. Their cost, however, grows faster with 𝑘 , because to keep the recall at 0.99 they must examine more documents: kANNolo with a larger ef 𝑠 , Seismic with more query terms or less pruning of its blocks. At 𝑘 = 100 they are at most 2.4 × faster, and abc-bmms is the fastest on OS-gte and faster than 

Seismic on SparseUp. At 𝑘 = 1000 abc-bmms is the fastest index everywhere, 1.7–7.4 × faster than Seismic with a budget of 2 × and 1.6–2.2 × faster than kANNolo on the other three datasets. 

Space and build. Our abc-bmms takes 1.5–3.6 × less space than 

Seismic and kANNolo . Our index is built in seconds, against minutes for Seismic and 9–27 hours for kANNolo . It has no parameters to tune, and one index serves every 𝑘 , whereas Seismic needs a grid search and a different index for every 𝑘 , and kANNolo a different value of ef 𝑠 for every 𝑘 .

## 6.5 Exact ground truth computation 

The recall of an approximate index is measured against the exact top-

𝑘 of a set of queries, the ground truth, which also serves to measure the effectiveness of a model. The ground truth is usually computed by exhaustive search, with a multithreaded brute-force scan such as that of vectorium [44], the library under Seismic and kANNolo .The tool abc-groundtruth computes it on all cores with an f32 

abc index, which gives exact scores. The index is not reordered, because at this number of queries reordering costs more than the search time it saves. On the four datasets (6,980 queries, 20 threads), the tool takes 15–30 s at 𝑘 = 1000, reading the input and building the index included, and 11–25 s at 𝑘 = 10 with abc-bmms . The brute force of vectorium takes 9–25 minutes, so our tool is 27–52 × faster at 𝑘 = 1000. 

## 7 Conclusions 

Standard IR techniques, combined and engineered for modern CPUs, give a fast exact index with no parameters for learned sparse re-trieval. In abc , term-at-a-time evaluation runs over blocks of doc-ids whose accumulators fit in the L1 cache, so that scoring a posting is a multiply-add into a slot already in cache. Block-Max MaxScore inside these blocks makes abc-bmms faster than abc at every 𝑘 , and the fastest exact index: an order of magnitude faster than Qdrant 

and Lucene , and several times smaller than the other block-based indexes. Approximate indexes win at small 𝑘 and abc-bmms at large 𝑘 , with a smaller index built in seconds. Our blocks and pruning do not depend on learned weights, so they also apply to classic term weights, such as BM25, which are outside the scope of this paper. Future work includes reordering other indexes with the graph bisection that reorders abc , evaluating 

abc-bmms on larger collections such as MS MARCO v2, and making the index updatable, which is simpler because a full rebuild takes seconds and no parameter needs to be tuned.
