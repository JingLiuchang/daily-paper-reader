ABSTRACT 

Building autonomous agents that continually explore open worlds and acquire new capabilities through experience is a long-standing aspiration in artificial in-telligence. Existing research often pursues lifelong learning within predefined tasks, curricula, or objectives, with self-improvement largely confined to indi-vidual agents. We introduce OpenKAGE, an agent-group evolution framework for open-ended lifelong learning. Within this framework, agents autonomously explore open worlds and continually acquire new capabilities by accumulating and reusing knowledge from their own interactions and the experience of oth-ers. OpenKAGE builds on two key mechanisms: (1) a two-layer knowledge representation, with a memory layer for situated memories and an experience layer for transferable experience and skills that retains the applicability condi-tions needed for reuse; and (2) a knowledge-sharing mechanism that semantically consolidates textual experience into a continuously evolving shared knowledge graph, while executable skills are shared verbatim. In Minecraft, OpenKAGE supports open-ended lifelong learning, enabling agent groups to sustain explo-ration and reach advanced technological milestones. Furthermore, the accumu-lated knowledge can be transferred to a newly initialized agent group on a different backbone model, improving its survival and exploration progress. 1 INTRODUCTION 

We study open-ended lifelong learning as a continuing process in which agents autonomously gener-ate goals, accumulate knowledge from interaction, and reuse that knowledge to acquire new capabil-ities. Unlike learning toward a fixed terminal task, this process is not organized around a predefined endpoint. A suggestive image comes from The Wild Robot : cast ashore on an unfamiliar island with only her built-in directives, Roz learns to coexist with the wildlife and, by raising an orphaned gosling, comes to belong to the island. Future autonomous agents may likewise enter physical or digital worlds carrying only system-level directives, and must confront unfamiliar phenomena, dis-cover new things to do, and devise new strategies without human intervention. Toward this vision, substantial progress has been made in enabling agents to learn and act in open worlds. Reinforcement and imitation learning support long-horizon control (Baker et al., 2022; Hafner et al., 2023), while LLM-based agents combine planning, memory, and reflection to tackle increasingly complex tasks (Zhu et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2025; Shinn et al., 2023). Systems such as Voyager, Optimus-1, and AriGraph further demonstrate how reusable skills, structured experi-ence, and knowledge graphs can support knowledge accumulation and reuse (Wang et al., 2024; Li et al., 2024; Anokhin et al., 2025). Despite these advances, much of the research remains organized around predefined tasks, curricula, or objectives, with self-improvement largely confined to individ-ual agents. Although systems such as Voyager support autonomous exploration, an individual agent 1arXiv:2611.24302v1 [cs.AI] 18 Nov 2026 still learns primarily from its own interactions, limiting the range of experience available to guide its development. Open-endedness research does generate new challenges rather than fixing them, by co-evolving environments with their solvers or by asking a foundation model which tasks are inter-esting (Wang et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2023), but this concerns what a single learner pursues next. Knowledge acquired by one agent does not automatically become available to others. This moti-vates moving beyond individual learning toward agent-group evolution, where experience acquired through diverse interactions can contribute to a shared, growing body of knowledge. The bottom strip illustrates milestones, not a prescribed curriculum. Learning from others offers a natural route from individual learning to collective improvement. CAMEL and MetaGPT enable agents to collaborate through role-based communication and struc-tured workflows (Li et al., 2023; Hong et al., 2024), while Generative Agents combines memory, re-flection, and social interaction to support information diffusion and coordination (Park et al., 2023). More directly, Group-Evolving Agents (GEA) shares evolutionary traces across agents and turns them into framework-level improvements through reflection (Weng et al., 2026). Memory systems such as G-Memory and LLMA-Mem keep shared memory across LLM agents (Zhang et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2026), and selective-sharing methods pass only chosen experiences or policy modules between agents (Gerstgrasser et al., 2023; Nath et al., 2026). These approaches demonstrate differ-ent ways for agents to benefit from one another, from coordinating actions to improving their own implementations or sharing memory and experience, though most are evaluated with tasks given in advance. For open-ended lifelong learning, we focus on a complementary challenge: how to turn knowledge acquired through individual interactions into a shared resource that supports continued exploration and capability acquisition across the group. This calls for distinguishing memories tied to an agent’s particular circumstances from experience that can be reused by others, and continually consolidating the latter as agents explore and learn. To this end, we introduce OpenKAGE, an agent-group evolution framework for open-ended lifelong learning (Figure 1). OpenKAGE enables agents to autonomously explore open worlds and contin-ually acquire capabilities by accumulating and reusing knowledge from their own interactions and the experience of others. Two mechanisms underpin this process. First, a two-layer knowledge representation separates private situated memories from transferable experience and skills, retaining the applicability conditions needed for reuse across agents. Second, a knowledge-sharing mecha-nism semantically consolidates textual experience into a continuously evolving shared knowledge graph, while executable skills are shared verbatim and remain maintained by their authors. Situ-ated memories remain private, while transferable knowledge becomes a cumulative resource for the 2group rather than remaining isolated within individual agents. To bring this accumulated knowledge into subsequent decisions, intent-aware subgraph retrieval draws relevant knowledge from both the agent’s local graph and the shared graph to support its ongoing exploration. We evaluate OpenKAGE in Minecraft, where agents must survive, explore, and develop increas-ingly advanced capabilities through sustained interaction with an open world. Starting with empty knowledge stores, agents accumulate and share knowledge acquired through their own exploration. Across four backbone models, OpenKAGE supports sustained exploration and progression to ad-vanced technological milestones, obtaining obsidian on all four and entering the Nether on three, while reducing deaths relative to the planner-only control; on the default backbone it also out-performs Voyager and an Echo baseline, neither of which reaches obsidian or the Nether within the evaluated horizon. Furthermore, knowledge accumulated by one group can be transferred to a newly initialized group using a different backbone model, substantially improving its survival and exploration progress. Together, these results show that knowledge accumulated through individual interactions supports continued learning within a group and capability acquisition by new agents.

OpenKAGE enables a group of agents to accumulate and reuse knowledge from their individual interactions with an open world (Figure 2). Each agent interacts with a separate Minecraft world in-stance, and agents exchange knowledge rather than in-world messages or resources. Each agent runs a ReAct loop organized into game-day cycles (Yao et al., 2023), using a planner to act and a reflector 

to separate situated memories from transferable experience; an aggregator merges experience-layer knowledge into the shared graph, semantically consolidating textual experience records and adding executable skills verbatim as isolated nodes; an intent-aware retriever supplies relevant subgraphs to the planner. This subsection describes how these pieces operate over time. 

Planner. During each game-day cycle, the agent alternates between thinking, acting, and observ-ing. Each morning it sets a plan for the day (a goal and ordered steps); planning with a goal triggers retrieval from the agent’s own graph and the shared graph, so the day’s work is informed by previ-ously accumulated group knowledge. The agent then acts by writing short asynchronous JavaScript programs, one program per step, over the environment’s low-level native Mineflayer API (pathfind-and-move, dig, place, craft, equip, . . . ). The API provides no task-level routines for gathering, 3smelting, or shelter construction; packaging them into skills is precisely the agents’ job. Executed programs return structured observations (position, health, inventory, nearby entities and blocks, time, advancements). Following Wang et al. Reflector. At the end of each game-day cycle (at dawn), the world pauses and the agent reflects on the elapsed day in two phases. The first re-reads the day’s trajectory and writes both layers of its local graph. It extends the timeline, updates locations and possessions, and proposes decontextualized experience-layer concepts supported by the day’s events. The second updates its skills based on per-skill success and failure counts. The reflector also emits a one-line factual summary of the day’s progress. Apart from the knowledge stores, this summary and the previous day’s goal are the only free-form text carried from one day to the next; everything else lives in the graphs. 

Aggregator. Skills written or revised in a dawn reflection enter the shared graph immediately; every K days, after all reflectors finish, the textual experience records are consolidated as well, so the agents enter the next K-day aggregation window with the shared graph already updated. The loop of act, reflect, aggregate, and retrieve continues for the whole run: competence that any single agent acquires is consolidated where every agent can find it, and the group’s knowledge compounds. No step of the loop involves human feedback, a human-authored curriculum, or parameter updates. 2.2 TWO -LAYER KNOWLEDGE GRAPHS

Sharing operates on both forms of knowledge in the experience layer, and the two call for different treatment. Textual experience records reach an agent only through its context, so the group is free to rewrite them into whatever consolidated wording serves best: they are semantically consolidated into the shared graph every K days. Executable skills are executed rather than read, so their code must not be rewritten: each skill enters the shared graph verbatim as an isolated node, without semantic consolidation, in the same dawn reflection in which it is written or revised. 

Textual-experience consolidation. Every K days, the aggregator reads the agents’ experience-layer text and updates the shared graph (Appendix A.6). It consolidates textual experience records rather than merely appending them: merging synonyms , where several agents’ wordings of one insight become a single concept: a vector-similarity pre-screen nominates candidate duplicates, and names already in use are reused, so a concept named once keeps its name; recording cross-agent corroboration , where a facet states explicitly when experience is confirmed by multiple agents, an unconfirmed claim still being admitted to await corroboration; reconciling contradictions , where conflicts are first tested for differing applicability conditions (one observation holds by day, the other fails at night) and corrected in place when one side is simply wrong; and aligning with prior knowledge , where new experience is folded into existing concepts wherever they match. Skill sharing. Skills bypass semantic consolidation. A skill node in the shared graph carries its author’s code unchanged, together with its author and its cumulative success and failure record; it has no edges, so retrieval reaches it by matching the goal against the skill’s stated intent (a one-line description of when to use it), as in vector anchoring, rather than by relational expansion. A skill is propagated verbatim and selected by its record; the author alone revises or retires it, so what circulates in the group is exactly the set of skills their authors still stand behind. The skill nodes are implemented as a read-only registry over the agents’ own skill libraries, so code is neither copied nor rewritten in transit (Appendix A.9). For skills, code delivery and context presentation use different channels: the code is injected wholesale into the execution environment, while only the signatures of the top-nskill =20 skills with the strongest execution records occupy the context, supplemented by signatures retrieved for the current goal. 2.4 INTENT -AWARE SUBGRAPH RETRIEVAL

3.1 SETUP 

Experimental setup. Runs last 30 Minecraft game days on one fixed world seed (seed 7) at hard difficulty with inventory retained after death; game time advances only during action execution. Each group has N = 3 agents in separate worlds with distinct personas, aggregating every K = 2 

days. DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash (DS-V4.1F) is the default backbone, GLM-5.3, hy4-preview and Kimi-K3 are additional backbones, and GLM-5.3-Flash is the target of the transfer experiment (§3.4); one backbone serves all roles of a run. Every run starts from empty knowledge stores, each method– backbone cell has 10 independent group runs, and reported values are averages over them. Full configuration and sources of randomness are in Appendix B.1. 

Baselines. The planner-only control has the same backbone, personas, action API, world seed and horizon but no persistent memory, reflection or skill library (Appendix B.3), so it measures the combined effect of the knowledge components, not sharing alone. On the default backbone we also re-implement two external methods under the same world, interface and horizon: Voyager (Wang et al., 2024) (automatic curriculum, iterative code generation, self-verification, skill library) and Echo (Li et al., 2026) (successful tasks stored as structured experience, new tasks induced by analogy). Differences from both originals are in Appendix B.3. 3.2 MAIN RESULTS

Better survival: fewer repeated mistakes. Mean deaths per agent fall on every backbone (Ta-ble 2). Two readouts locate the reduction. Deaths that repeat an earlier one (same cause, within 16 blocks of the agent’s previous death) fall from 15 .1 to 7.4 per agent on the default backbone, and also on hy4-preview and Kimi-K3, and make up a smaller share of all deaths ( 45% under OpenKAGE against 52% for the planner-only control, 70% for Echo and 88% for Voyager, whose isolated agents often fall into the same lethal situation dozens of times; mean of per-run shares). Combat deaths from hostile mobs, arrows and explosions fall on four backbones (Figure 5; 22 .2 to 10 .5 per agent on the default backbone), while drowning falls clearly only on hy4-preview and rises on GLM-5.3, the backbone where the most agents obtain obsidian: obtaining obsidian requires working beside water and lava, which is very dangerous. Agents learn to handle threats they meet repeatedly, and the re-maining deaths shift towards the hazards of deeper exploration. This is not bought by exploring less: mean XP per life is higher on three of the four backbones (markedly on GLM-5.3 and hy4-preview). 

Shared knowledge: growing and reused across agents. On the default backbone the skill library available to each agent grows almost linearly to 132 .5 skills by day 30, of which an agent writes about 44 itself; on every backbone 62 to 65% of skill calls invoke a skill written by another agent, and 94% of those succeed on the default backbone (success means the call returned without error; growth curves, failure rates and skill revisions are in Appendix B.4). The knowledge components cost more: per game night OpenKAGE makes 21% more LLM calls than the planner-only control, processes about 4.2 times as much text, and takes about 1.6 times as much wall-clock time per complete run, the extra time cost of reflection and aggregation (Appendix B.7). 3.3 ABLATIONS

Open-world learning and agent memory. Voyager combines an automatic curriculum with a growing executable skill library for Minecraft exploration (Wang et al., 2024); OpenKAGE instead places goal generation inside the acting agent’s own loop (§3.2); this concerns how goals are pro-duced, not whether exploration is autonomous. Open-endedness research likewise generates the next challenge outside the learner, by co-evolving environments with their solvers (Wang et al., 2019) or by asking a foundation model which learnable tasks are interesting (Zhang et al., 2023); both concern what a single learner pursues next, whereas OpenKAGE concerns what a group keeps and shares. We use “open-ended” to mean no predefined endpoint, with progress measured by milestones and no novelty criterion. Other agents organize long-horizon behavior around injected task knowledge (Zhu et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2025), a hybrid graph-and-pool memory (Li et al., 2024), an episodic What-Where-When memory (Park et al., 2025), a knowledge-graph world model (Anokhin et al., 2025) or a causal graph (Yu & Lu, 2025; Tang et al., 2024). In OpenKAGE the stores start empty, and the distinction is the unit of accumulation: the memory–experience split becomes a sharing boundary, so situated records stay local while transferable knowledge is consolidated. 

Multi-agent experience sharing. Multi-agent frameworks coordinate through communication and role division (Li et al., 2023; Hong et al., 2024); GEA treats the group as an evolutionary unit and pools members’ trajectories (Weng et al., 2026). Parameter-level sharing includes feder-ated continual learning, which aggregates knowledge through parameters and leaves no inspectable record (Yang et al., 2024), and selective decentralized reuse: agents relay a few high-priority tran-sitions (Gerstgrasser et al., 2023) or retrieve and compose policy modules by task similarity (Nath et al., 2026), both with tasks given in advance. Memory systems for multi-agent LLMs are closer in form. G-Memory retrieves related past task queries with their insights and trajectories, filtered by role (Zhang et al., 2025); its hierarchy is keyed by task queries, which an open-ended setting does not provide. LLMA-Mem keeps episodic, procedural and transactive memory under local, shared or hybrid topologies on assigned tasks (Wu et al., 2026); its hybrid topology likewise keeps episodes local and shares consolidated procedures, so that design choice is not new to us. DAMCS gives each LLM agent a hierarchical knowledge graph and exchanges structured messages with chosen partners toward a fixed goal (Yang et al., 2025), without consolidating them into a group store. OpenKAGE differs in drawing the boundary by kind of knowledge rather than priority or partner, in sharing a structured graph whose applicability conditions stay with executable skills, in returning subgraphs rather than a ranked list, and in running without assigned tasks. A controlled comparison with verbatim sharing (Appendix B.5) isolates consolidation inside an active sharing channel, and GEA, G-Memory and LLMA-Mem are compared with OpenKAGE at the level of design (Appendix A.10). 

Retrieval-based memory. Retrieval-augmented generation retrieves external knowledge (Lewis et al., 2020), and GraphRAG and AriGraph use graph representations (Edge et al., 2024; Anokhin et al., 2025). In agent memory the same pattern usually yields a flat ranked list (Wang et al., 2024): nearest neighbors of the query enter the context ranked by similarity, without the dependencies, preconditions and failure modes that bind them to the situation. OpenKAGE instead returns a sub-graph, via vector anchoring and query-conditioned relational expansion, over knowledge consoli-dated across agents. Appendix A.10 compares systems in detail. 

5 CONCLUSION 

We presented OpenKAGE, a framework that drives lifelong learning and collective evolution of agents: it separates private situated memories from shareable experience, consolidates textual expe-rience and shares executable skills across the group, and returns this knowledge to decisions through intent-aware subgraph retrieval. Across four backbones and 30-day Minecraft runs, it reached mile-stones that a planner-only control, Voyager and Echo did not, and reduced deaths, mostly repeated ones; its shared skills were reused across agents, and transferred knowledge helped a new group on another backbone. 

Limitations and outlook. Our evidence is limited to Minecraft, one world seed and a 30-day horizon (60 days only in the scaling runs), so it speaks to open-ended lifelong learning only to a 10 limited extent, although to our knowledge it is the longest-running study in an open-ended world to date. At 10 runs per condition the ablations do not resolve which component they depend on. AI USE STATEMENT

Two consequences of this schedule are worth stating. First, because consolidation is synchronous rather than running in the background, every agent entering day K + 1 already sees the conclusions of window K in the shared graph, so the knowledge feedback loop has no lag; skills reach the other agents sooner still, on the morning after they are written. Only the retrieval path of the acting agent writes it, by the two gains of Table 10, and no operation in the reflector’s or the aggregator’s tool sets writes it. Strength records use, not authorship. 

What the model deliberately does not carry. Embeddings are not fields of the graph, so retrieval can be retuned without touching the knowledge. Facets and edges carry no strength and no decay or archival flag. Usefulness is a property of what the agent reaches for, and everything the agent reaches for is a concept. And no node points back into the trajectory it came from: the day’s raw events are compressed into the graph at dawn, and the only text that survives into the next day unchanged is the one-line summary of progress. 

Enforced and requested properties. The sections below mix two kinds of property: those the implementation guarantees, which the tool layer checks and on violation fails loudly, and those the prompts ask a model to produce, which are standing instructions whose observance nothing checks at run time. The first kind is stated where it applies; the second lives entirely in the prompts released with the code. A.3 AGENT –WORLD INTERFACE 

The agent’s only way to change the world is to write a short asynchronous JavaScript program, one per step, over an atomic action API exposed through the Mineflayer bot library; it never emits low-level controls. Programs are temporally extended, compositional, directly executable and, once verified, can be saved as skills (§2.1). This appendix lists the API in scope, the execution semantics of a step, and the observation returned after one. 

The atomic API. Everything below is already in the scope of the generated program; nothing is imported. One feature of this list is deliberate: there are no capability primitives such as Voyager’s 

mineBlock , craftItem , smeltItem or killMob . Voyager’s mineBlock , for example, scans every matching block within 32 blocks—including blocks hidden behind solid rock—and then delegates pathfinding, digging and item pickup to a single collectBlock.collect call. No composite capability is handed to our agents; they compose one in their own programs, and the skill channel is what lets that composition persist and travel to the other agents (Appendix A.10 gives the argument in full). The single exception is waitUntil , because the client’s own tick-wait is decoupled from the server clock, so a hand-written wait loop cannot move the world forward at all. 

Execution semantics. One write code call is one step. The program is evaluated as the body of an asynchronous function; world-changing calls must be awaited, because an unawaited one keeps acting on the world while the agent has already moved on to the next step. The agent declares its own budget for the step in game ticks , with a default of 500 and bounds of 100 and 3,000; a full day–night cycle is 24,000 ticks, and the budget is spent in world time, not wall-clock time. A step ends when its budget is spent, when the agent dies, or at midnight, whichever comes first. Observation. Every step returns the execution result together with a complete fresh observation, a text rendering of world state rather than pixels. A.5 THE REFLECTOR 

At dawn the world is frozen and the day is closed out. The reflector runs in two phases, each a separate session with its own tool set, and the group is held at a barrier between them so that no agent starts the next phase before every agent has finished the previous one. What follows is given in English translation; the originals that drive the runs are released with the code. 

Phase 1: writing the graph. The reflector is told that tomorrow its context will be empty and that the graph is the only thing it carries across, so this is a compression, not a transcript, and choosing what to keep is its main judgement. It is given: • its persona, the day number, and the identifier to use for the day’s own concept; • the day’s server-authoritative facts: what new items it obtained, where each advancement tree stands, how many steps it took, its deaths grouped by cause, and the milestones it reached; • the full trajectory of the day, that is, each step’s program, the observation it acted on, and the feedback it got back; • an index of its own graph: every concept with its layer tag, the kinds of facet it carries and the relations it has, but not the content, which is read on demand; It is then asked to write both layers: for the memory layer, the day’s concept chained to the previous day and a concept with numeric coordinates for every place reached or built; for the experience layer, whatever generalises into a sentence that holds for someone else, under a common name. Tools. Phase 1 has one narrow tool per operation over concepts, facets, edges and skills: create, update, merge and delete a concept; search and fetch one; add, rewrite and delete a facet; and add, change or remove a relation. Phase 2: skill maintenance. The second session maintains the agent’s own skill library, with only the five skill tools, against what the library actually did during the day. The access restriction by itself does not guarantee that such details are absent from what the reflectors wrote, and we do not check that they are, so we make no claim about leakage beyond the access bound-ary. The one thing that does appear from the memory side is the name of a memory concept an experience is linked to, shown as a private anchor: readable, not retrievable, never writable into the shared graph. The local textual-experience buffers are cleared once the window’s contribution has been merged, so a private experience layer spans one window of reflection, while private memory and skill libraries are kept and the shared graph keeps growing. 

What the aggregator is given. One block per agent: its experience concepts, each with its sum-mary, its facets under their labels, and its relations, with the direction of every relation spelled out. Then a pre-screen: for each candidate, up to three concepts already in the shared graph whose simi-larity exceeds a threshold, offered explicitly as a hint and not a verdict. Then the labels and relation names the shared graph already uses, with counts, but not a list of what the graph contains: each window is an independent session, and whether something is already there is something the aggre-gator has to look up; the label list answers a different question, what this graph calls this kind of thing. The aggregator is told to work in order: read all N submissions and group them first, then search the shared graph and read what is already there before writing, then write. 

The aggregator’s system prompt (English translation). The prompt is short by design: it defines the role and the four duties of §2.3, states what the input looks like, and gives the representation, that is, what a concept, a facet and an edge are and what each field may hold (section 3). Everything that is a property of an operation, what a call does and what it refuses, is left to the tool descriptions, which are in the context as well and are not repeated here. One sentence of section 2 describes each exploration as running in a freshly regenerated world; in the reported runs all worlds are generated from the same seed, so natural terrain coordinates coincide while built structures and dates do not. The instruction serves to keep agent-specific coordinates and dates out of the shared graph, and is a requested property, not a checked one.

A.8 RETRIEVAL : IMPLEMENTATION AND HYPERPARAMETERS 

This appendix completes §2.4 with the implementation and the hyperparameter values. All embed-ding goes through one asymmetric instruction-tuned encoder (KaLM, 896-d) (Zhao et al., 2025), over two indices keyed by slug, one for concepts and one for skills. A concept is indexed as several shards—one identity shard over its name and summary and one shard per facet—and a query scores a concept by fusing its shards back into one cosine-scale score, so that a facet can bring back the concept it belongs to and a short name cannot outvote a well-supported one. Anchoring admits a concept by the relative gate of §2.4 alone, with no absolute floor: cosine values are not compara-ble across encoders or corpora, whereas the relative gate is portable. On expansion, query–relation similarities are z-standardized within each entry’s candidates before entering Eq. equation 1, which restores their spread without changing the ranking: every relation text shares the entry name as a prefix, so the raw similarities sit in a band in which softmax would be nearly uniform. The candidate set is the relation types actually present on the current frontier. Recall returns every facet of every node reached, which is bounded by the (concept, kind) uniqueness of Appendix A.2, and every in-jected block is deduplicated against the conversation so far, so that a repeated injection returns only 27 the increment. Retrieval defaults to the experience layer, since the memory layer is already injected each morning. Table 10 lists the values used in every run; they are identical across conditions. A.9 SKILL LIFECYCLE AND THE VERBATIM CHANNEL 

This appendix completes §2.3 on the skill side: the record a skill carries, how it is created and maintained, and how it crosses agent boundaries without ever being rewritten. The design question is the mirror image of the textual channel’s: there, knowledge is prose and needs an LLM to merge it; here, knowledge is executable code, and what the world reports back is unambiguous, namely whether a call resolved or threw, so the machinery is measurement and bookkeeping, and the only LLM judgement needed is “which skill is failing and how do I fix mine”. What that measurement records is execution completion , not semantic correctness: a call that resolves has run, which is a weaker claim than having achieved what the caller intended, and no stage of the pipeline checks the latter. 

The record. A skill is one row in its author’s library: an intent (the natural-language condition under which it should be used, which is the text the skill index embeds, so retrieval matches on 

when to use it , not on the code), a signature (what the context shows: the call form, which must begin with the function name defined in the code, since the acting agent calls by signature and a mismatch would make the skill unreachable, which the creation tool rejects), the js code 

itself, optional preconditions , and a verified flag. Beside these, the two count pairs and the revision count of Appendix A.2: the current-version pair is the evaluation window, the all-time pair the skill’s lifetime reliability. One number answers “did my fix work”, the other “do I trust this slot at all”. 

Creation. Skills are written from programs that executed successfully that day and whose outcome the reflector reads as having achieved its intent; the creation prompt states this threshold and the reflector applies it, and no independent test re-checks it. This includes skills composed from several successful fragments of a day, in which case the composed function is not separately executed before it is registered. Skills are written by the phase-1 reflector in the first place, and by the phase-2 maintenance session when it adds one. A name collision is rejected outright: the JS function name and the slug are unique across the whole group. A near-duplicate intent , by contrast, is only reported back to the author as an advisory list, since whether two intents name the same job is a judgement the author makes and not a fact the tool can settle. 

Measurement: the verbatim channel. The code of every verified skill is injected wholesale into the execution environment, in the same scope as the atomic API and available to every write code 

step at zero context cost, while what enters the context is only signatures: a base block of the top-20 by cumulative success, supplemented by signatures retrieved for the current goal. Listing all signatures instead fills the context with skills unrelated to the current goal. Use is measured by the world, not inferred from text: each injected skill is wrapped so that a call either resolves (recorded as an execution completion) or throws (recorded as a failure, with the error message kept). The counts are routed back each night, deterministically, to the author’s library, so the record a maintainer sees is the skill’s true usage across the whole group, and a user’s failing calls adjust the author’s record, not the user’s. 28 Maintenance and retirement. The phase-2 session (Appendix A.5) reads, for each of its own skills: both count pairs, the revision count, and up to ten deduplicated error messages from the current window. Its brief is to act only on what is failing, with the fix driven by the error pattern; the prompt below states it in full. Revising the code resets the evaluation window while the lifetime totals keep accumulating. This is what makes maintenance a feedback loop rather than guesswork; editing only the intent or preconditions does not reset the window, as that is documentation, not code. A skill that cannot be fixed is retired , that is, demoted out of the verified set so that it is no longer injected or retrieved. Retirement is reversible by rewriting. A skill nobody ever calls is read as a retrieval failure of its intent, and is fixed by making the intent more specific (“when smelting iron ore” matches; “mining” does not). The author may revise or retire only its own skills, and the barrier structure of Appendix A.1 ensures that no agent starts a day calling a skill that was retired overnight. 

Propagation. In the shared graph every verified skill is an isolated node that carries its author’s code verbatim. We implement these nodes as a read-only registry over the per-agent libraries: re-trieval fans out to every library, merges by similarity with each hit tagged by author and counts, and the code is neither duplicated nor rewritten in transit. The code that runs is the author’s code, unmodified, and its revision history lives in exactly one place. The contrast with the textual channel (§2.3) is what the pair is for: prose has to be interpreted to be merged, so it goes through semantic consolidation, whereas code can be reused as it stands, so what the group accumulates about it is measurement rather than an opinion. 

The skill-maintenance prompt (English translation). The phase-2 prompt, abridged to its oper-ative rules.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                      

> You are the maintainer of your own skill library. Each dawn, the skills you wrote were called by many users during the past day; now you look at how they performed, and whether to improve or retire them. You will see all of your own skills, one line each: cumulative successes/failures of the current code version since its last revision; lifetime successes/failures over all versions, never reset; how many times the code has been revised; and real error samples from this window, deduplicated, at most ten per skill. Judge each skill; act on the ones with problems. Askill with only failures, or ahigh failure rate, has abug, not bad luck: rewrite it with update_skill, reading the errors---timeouts: split the work or request alonger budget; missing inputs: check the inventory first; pathfinding failures: aim at alooser target; wrong blocks: re-read the world. One you cannot fix, demote for now: aretired skill stops being injected, and stopping the loss beats letting it burn your step budget again tomorrow. Skills that work are left alone. Askill nobody calls means its intent is too broad or too narrow to be retrieved---that too is fixed by rewriting. Touch only your own skills. Revising code resets the counters: that is your clean evaluation window, and tomorrow’s counts tell you whether the fix worked. Do not let abad skill linger---it is injected every day and wastes your budget until you fix it or retire it. Three rows deserve a sentence beyond the table, because the resemblance is closest and the differ-ence easiest to miss. 

AriGraph. The nearest single-agent ancestor of our graph, evaluated on text games (Anokhin et al., 2025): an agent grows a knowledge-graph world model with episodic and semantic layers, explores it locally when deciding, and revises it from interaction. OpenKAGE adopts the graph memory and the two-way split, and differs in what the split is for . In AriGraph both layers serve one agent’s understanding; in OpenKAGE the split is the sharing boundary. The semantic layer is what may leave the agent, and the episodic layer never does. A second mechanism (aggregation, Appendix A.7) exists precisely to reconcile N agents’ semantic layers into one graph. Retrieval also differs in kind: AriGraph’s agent walks its own graph interactively, whereas our retrieval is intent-keyed, scored, and sampled, and runs over a graph the acting agent did not write. 

Voyager. The source of our action space and of the skill artifact, and the row our skill channel most resembles. The difference that matters most is where goals come from. Voyager organizes exploration around tasks proposed by a dedicated automatic curriculum module; OpenKAGE inte-grates goal generation into the acting agent’s ongoing interaction loop, in which the agent selects and revises its own goals using its current state and its accumulated knowledge, with no separate curricu-lum module. The distinction concerns how goals are generated and how they govern interaction, not whether human intervention is required: Voyager’s curriculum is automatic, and the system is eval-uated on open-ended exploration, tech-tree mastery and map coverage. Taking the group as given, three further differences follow. Skills are shared through a registry rather than living in one library, so a skill’s record accumulates every agent’s calls, not just its author’s; maintenance is driven by that cross-agent record (Appendix A.9); and skills are the second channel, alongside the consolidated textual graph, which has no analogue in Voyager. The ablations of Appendix B.5 do not separate the two channels, since each removal that affects sharing ( −S, −E) takes both away. The evaluated action interfaces also differ from Voyager’s original design (Wang et al., 2024). Its appendix lists expert-written control primitives under “Control primitive APIs implemented by us” separately from “Control primitive APIs provided by Mineflayer”; the list includes exploreUntil , mineBlock ,

craftItem , smeltItem , killMob and others. mineBlock , for example, mines and collects a specified number of blocks within a 32-block distance. These routines enter the prompt as expert-written programs. In Voyager’s own baseline comparisons (Table A.3), the listed differences are the skill library, automatic curriculum and self-verification. We take the essence of learning to be the induction of generalizable skills from many trajectories of interaction; the upper bound of learning is such a set of generalizable skills. A primitive packages resource search, navigation, mining and collection into one long-horizon routine written in advance. Adding it to the agent’s initial capabil-ities raises its lower bound of competence and shortens the interval between that lower bound and what remains acquirable; starting from atomic actions preserves this interval, with the upper bound unchanged. Our protocol therefore removes these primitives to measure what can be acquired from atomic actions. Our adaptation keeps the automatic curriculum, iterative code generation with exe-cution feedback, self-verification and per-agent skill library, and starts every method from the same atomic API. Under this bare API, Voyager’s hard-coded first task can fail repeatedly; Appendix B.3 reports this as a concrete limitation of our adaptation. These results characterize the adapted imple-mentations under our interface. The original end-to-end system lies outside this comparison. The comparison remains a comparison of mechanisms: Voyager and Echo keep their respective mecha-nisms while the supplied action capabilities are held fixed.

OpenK-AGE also lets the group read what every member produced, but the shared artifact is a graph whose records are consolidated rather than a set of parallel traces, and skills travel on a separate channel. B.2 METRIC DEFINITIONS 

All metrics come from the structured snapshots recorded at the end of each day. Deaths is cumulative per agent, averaged over the agents of a cell. XP/life is final-day cumulative XP divided by deaths 

+1 , computed per agent and then averaged; since XP is not lost on death, it measures what an agent gains per life. Tool era is the highest technology tier reached in the agent’s equipment, iron or diamond in our runs, and read from the tool tier itself rather than from obtaining obsidian, which is a milestone with its own column. Each Tool era entry carries that tier, the mean day on which the agents that reached it first did so, and their count out of all agents of the cell. Obsidian is the day of first obtainment and Nether the day of first entry, each averaged over the agents that reached it and reported with that count in parentheses. Counts are out of all agents of a cell (three per run, 30 per cell). Repeat counts an agent’s deaths that repeat one of its own earlier deaths: same recorded cause, within 16 blocks of the earlier death’s position; it is a per-agent mean like Deaths. Weakest XP/life takes, in each run, the lowest XP/life among its three agents and averages that over the runs of a condition. Others’ skills is the share of all skill calls in a condition that invoke a skill whose author is another agent of the same group, as recorded in the skill registry; it is zero by construction wherever sharing is removed. Lower is better for Deaths, repeated deaths and attainment days; higher is better for XP/life, the tool tier and the counts in parentheses. All values are averages over the ten sampled runs of a cell; because the agents of a run share one graph, the run is the sampling unit. B.3 BASELINES 

The same-backbone baseline. The baseline is OpenKAGE with the knowledge machinery re-moved and nothing else changed: same backbone, prompts, tool set, night count, group size, world seed, and day–night protocol. The baseline agent keeps the planner of §2.1. It still sets the day’s goal and ordered steps itself, still acts by writing one short program per step over the same atomic API, and still reasons over the observations and feedback that come back. What it loses is persis-tence. No local graph is written, no shared graph is consolidated, no skill is registered or retrieved, and no memory slice is injected in the morning; because in OpenKAGE the reflector is the process that maintains an agent’s graph and emits the one line that crosses the day boundary, removing the graphs also removes the nightly reflection, so no text at all is carried from one day to the next. Re-trieval has nothing to retrieve, and the planner meets each day with the model, the observations, and nothing else. The residue is exactly the standard single-agent LLM-agent stack (Yao et al., 2023; Shinn et al., 2023; Significant Gravitas, 2023), in the same family as the planner-only control of Wang et al. (2024); we do not claim the two match item by item in configuration. Relative to it, OpenKAGE adds persistent memory, nightly reflection, the skill channel, intent-aware retrieval and group-level consolidation. The gap between the two rows of a backbone is therefore the combined effect of that knowledge stack under a matched agent–world interface: it is not an estimate of what sharing alone contributes, and it does not establish superiority over other memory-equipped agents. 

External baselines. Voyager (Wang et al., 2024) and Echo (Li et al., 2026) are re-implemented on the same server, world seed, spawn point, difficulty, day–night protocol and 30-day horizon, with three isolated agents per group that share nothing, and with DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash as the single backbone of every module. Both act through the same bare Mineflayer API as OpenKAGE instead of their original action layers, and both receive the long-term objective of our protocol (survive, explore, build) as their overall goal, so that what differs is the mechanism that turns that goal into tasks and experience. Tasks continue across day boundaries, since neither system has a day–night notion. • Voyager. Automatic curriculum, iterative code generation with execution feedback (up to four rounds per task), self-verification, and a per-agent skill library, with the original tem-peratures and warm-up schedule. The optional wiki question-answering context is omitted. Because the hard-coded first task can fail repeatedly under the bare API, the curriculum takes over after five consecutive failures of it. 33 • Echo. The released code does not include the runtime it depends on, so we port its mech-anisms rather than run the original system. Successful tasks are stored as five-dimensional structured experience (attribute, structure, function, procedure, interaction); the most recent success serves as the anchor, the top-3 other experiences are retrieved by the official equal-weight five-dimension similarity, and in-context analogical learning induces the next task. Code generation and self-verification use the same executor as Voyager. The visual dimen-sions are written from text observations, since the agents receive no images. Two guards are ours: a seed task is skipped after three failures, and an induced task must advance beyond its anchor, because without this rule agents fell into loops of same-tier material substitutions whose value never rose. 

Scope of the comparison. The planner-only control measures the combined effect of the knowl-edge stack under our protocol. Voyager and Echo are adapted to the same low-level action interface, which fixes the action capabilities supplied in advance. Voyager’s expert-written control primitives are listed separately from Mineflayer’s API in its own appendix; our protocol studies the acquisi-tion of such composite capabilities. This design addresses acquisition from a common interface and keeps every method on the same atomic API. Under this bare API, Voyager’s hard-coded first task can fail repeatedly; Appendix B.3 reports this as a concrete limitation of our adaptation. The ex-ternal comparisons characterize the adapted implementations under our interface. Voyager keeps its automatic curriculum, iterative code generation with execution feedback, self-verification and per-agent skill library; Echo keeps its structured experience and analogy-induced tasks. B.5 ABLATION CONDITIONS 

What each condition of §3.3 leaves intact matters for reading the gaps, so it is stated per condition. 37 • Raw. Direct sharing (no consolidation). No component is removed: private graphs, subgraph retrieval, the memory layer, the shared graph and cross-agent skill reuse all stay as in Full. Only the aggregation step changes: at each window the three agents’ experience-layer concepts, facets and edges are appended to the shared graph verbatim, with window– arm prefixes on slugs, no merging or selection, and no edges crossing into the memory layer; the aggregation agent is not called. Retrieval budgets, skill sharing and layer clearing are unchanged; its comparison with Full is reported below. • −S. No sharing (individual memory). Private graphs and subgraph retrieval stay; both sharing channels go: no aggregation (the shared graph stays empty, retrieval sees only the agent’s own graph) and no cross-agent skill reuse (skills are read from the author’s library only). • −R. Flat retrieval (RAG-style). The full knowledge pipeline stays, including writing, aggregation, sharing, skill retrieval and the vector anchoring step with the same relative gate. Retrieval then stops at the anchors: it returns their facets as a flat list, with no relational expansion, no edge structure in the result, and the agent’s expand tool is removed. The condition applies to both the private and the shared graph. The three removals are bundled, so the comparison with Full does not separate multi-hop expansion from seeing the edges, and −R also reads less retrieved text when the plan is set (Appendix B.7). On the default backbone the agents of Full rarely call retrieve themselves (67 calls in 10 runs, 57 of them at one hop) and never call expand, so the difference arises mostly in the retrieval that setting a plan triggers. Only retrieval varies, though the knowledge agents subsequently act on may diverge as they take different actions. • −M. No memory layer. The experience layer, aggregation, the shared graph, and skills all keep operating; reflection no longer writes memory concepts and the morning injection disappears. Unlike the others, this one varies which layer stores knowledge, not what is stored. • −E. No experience layer. Reflection writes only to the memory layer: no textual experi-ence record is written and the skill channel is off, so no skill is created, maintained, injected or reused across agents. The aggregator receives nothing, the shared graph stays empty, and experience retrieval returns nothing. • B. No graph (bare model). Removes every graph, private and shared: no memory, no aggregation, and no skills. This is the no-graph control of the main experiment, here also the floor of the suite. Two caveats follow from the design itself. First, −S and −E both leave the shared graph empty; they differ in whether the agent’s private experience is still written. It is under −S but not under −E,so their gaps overlap by construction and must not be read as additive independent contributions. Second, the run-to-run spread within a condition is wide (Table 15), so small differences in Deaths and XP/life between conditions are read as lying within that spread. GLM-5.3-Flash is a weak backbone: deep milestones stay out of reach. The target backbone of the transfer experiment (§3.4) is a small model, and its own ceiling bounds what that experiment can show. The scaling runs above, all on GLM-5.3-Flash and starting from empty stores, give that ceiling. Enlarging the group to N =5 or N =8 agents does not carry any of them to obsidian or the Nether within 30 game days (Figure 12(a)); even doubling the horizon to 60 game days yields obsidian for only 1 of 3 agents, on day 54, and no agent enters the Nether (Figure 12(b)). In the transfer experiment, by contrast, one of nine agents obtains obsidian within 30 days. Thus on this weak backbone the accumulated knowledge reaches, within 30 days, a milestone that groups starting from empty stores do not reach with more agents or with the same 30-day horizon. B.7 INFERENCE COST 

Game time is paused during deliberation, so a fixed number of game days does not fix the inference budget. Table 16 counts what each condition spends per game night, and Table 17 splits OpenK-AGE’s share by component and reports the wall-clock hours of a complete run for each condition. On the default backbone OpenKAGE makes 21% more LLM calls than the planner-only control (164 vs. 136 per night), while executing slightly fewer environment steps per agent ( 32 vs. 39 ). The two external baselines make more calls than OpenKAGE ( 336 and 439 ) and take more steps per agent ( 57 and 67 ). Reflection and aggregation account for 53 of OpenKAGE’s 164 calls per night (32% ); on the other backbones the extra calls over the planner-only control range from 12 to 59 per night. All cells are counted in the same way. By this measure OpenKAGE processes about 4.2 times as much text per night as the planner-only control (per-run range 389 –449 k vs. 91 –107 k): 62% of it is reflection and aggregation, which read each agent’s day, and planning itself carries 1.6 times the text because retrieved knowledge and the morning injection enter the prompts, even though it makes fewer calls. The extra cost of the knowl-edge components is therefore larger in text than in calls, and we make no claim about computational efficiency; the results establish progress and survival within a fixed game horizon. Full and −R are not at equal retrieval budget (ten runs and all three agents per condition, mean over the 30 days; ranges are across runs).
