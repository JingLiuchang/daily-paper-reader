## ABSTRACT 

Dense retrievers are a foundational component underlying modern RAG and search systems. Training these models typically requires a curated collection of annotated query-document pairs, which are prohibitively expensive to obtain out-side a handful of well-resourced domains. This has motivated a line of work that leverages large-scale unsupervised retrieval pretraining from unlabelled text alone. Most such methods learn by optimizing a language modelling or contrastive learn-ing objective over short spans within a document . This raises the question: how can we extract richer learning signals from a single document for retrieval pre-training? To address it, we introduce xPRT (Cross-Passage Retrieval Pretraining), an unsupervised framework that learns from the key information distributed across an entire document and the connections between its parts. We evaluate xPRT 

against five state-of-the-art objectives on MTEB English v2, continuing pretrain-ing from a public unsupervised checkpoint while holding the corpus, schedule, and training procedure fixed. Across diverse tasks spanning all task types, we observe that xPRT consistently outperforms the strongest baseline by 3.2% on an average. Notably, in this continual pretraining setting, many baseline meth-ods incur substantial performance regressions, whereas our method delivers the largest complementary gains over the initial pretrained checkpoint. Controlled ablations confirm that xPRT ’s performance gains stem from its proposed cross-passage signals. xPRT demonstrates that a single unlabelled document is a much richer source of retrieval signal than prior work assumed, and offers complemen-tary gains to what current retrievers already capture. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Dense retrievers are a core component of modern search, open-domain question answering and retrieval-augmented generation (RAG). A dual encoder maps queries and passages to vectors in a shared space and retrieves candidates from a prebuilt index by approximate nearest-neighbour search (Karpukhin et al., 2020; Reimers & Gurevych, 2019; Zhao et al., 2024). In RAG, a language model answers from the passages it is given (Lewis et al., 2020), so a passage the retriever misses cannot be recovered later in the pipeline. Since the encoder is the whole retriever, retrieval quality rests on how well a single pooled vector stands in for a passage. Learning that vector has traditionally relied on costly supervision: large collections of annotated query-passage pairs, mined hard negatives and cross-encoder filtering (Nguyen et al., 2016; Xiong et al., 2021; Qu et al., 2021). Such data exist for a handful of well-resourced domains and are prohibitively expensive to curate elsewhere. This cost motivates learning from unlabelled text. Pre-trained language models such as BERT (De-vlin et al., 2019), RoBERTa (Liu et al., 2019) and T5 (Raffel et al., 2020) are the standard initializa-tion for dense retrievers (Karpukhin et al., 2020; Xiong et al., 2021). However, they are pre-trained with token-level supervision, and the pooled vector never learns to represent a passage as a whole, in a manner that is suitable for retrieval (Chang et al., 2020; Reimers & Gurevych, 2019), and thus labelled data must close the gap. 

Retrieval-oriented pre-training aims to close part of this gap without labels, by training the pooled vector directly on unlabelled text. Contrastive methods build pseudo-relevant pairs from the corpus,

Bottleneck auto-encoding methods instead train the pooled vector to summarize its passage (Gao & Callan, 2021; Xiao et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2023; Ma et al., 2024; Li et al., 2023b; Shen et al., 2023). The encoder compresses a passage into the vector, and a decoder must reconstruct the passage from it, so the vector is pushed to retain the passage’s content. Both families, however, make some strong assumptions for sourcing the learning signal that might contradict with what a good retriever needs. Auto-encoders reconstruct a passage from itself, so the pooled vector is never asked to anticipate content it has not read, which is exactly what a query asks of the passage that answers it. Contrastive methods look beyond the span, but pair it with a second span chosen by position or at random. They reduce the relation between the two to a single label, same document or not, applied with equal weight whether the spans share a subject or only a URL. On web text, a navigation block is as likely to be drawn as the paragraph that states the topic (Penedo et al., 2024), and nothing else in the document answers a query drawn from boilerplate. CoT-MAE (Wu et al., 2023) does look outside the passage, but supervises only a single adjacent context span. Two document-level signals thus go unused: which passages carry a document’s subject, and how 

those passages relate to one another. This raises our central question: how much more retrieval signal can a single unlabelled document supply? 

We propose xPRT (Cross-Passage Retrieval Pre-training), a self-supervised objective that exploits both signals using the document alone. Content, not position, decides what a training example contains. A passage becomes an anchor only if its embedding under the frozen initial encoder lies close to the centroid of its document, which keeps boilerplate out of the query side. The anchor’s 

siblings are the passages of the same document most related to it under fused dense and lexical similarity (§2.1). Supervision then runs across passages rather than within one. A contrastive loss makes the anchor single out its most related sibling among passages of other documents (§2.2). A lightweight decoder that sees the anchor only through its embedding predicts the masked tokens of sibling passages the encoder never read (§2.3). This mirrors the relation retrieval needs between a query and the passage that answers it. We continue pre-training the public e5-base-unsupervised checkpoint (Wang et al., 2022) on FineWeb (Penedo et al., 2024) under each objective. We hold the backbone, corpus, schedule, batch size, passage length, retention regularizer and seeds fixed, and evaluate every checkpoint under 2Preprint one protocol without fine-tuning, so that the objective is the only variable (§4). On MTEB(eng, v2) (Muennighoff et al., 2023; Enevoldsen et al., 2025), xPRT outperforms the strongest of five baselines across three data scales experimented with, by 3.2% on average over 41 tasks and by 

6.1% nDCG@10 on the ten retrieval tasks. Our contributions are: • We identify two document-level signals that unsupervised retrieval objectives leave unused: which passages represent a document, and how its passages relate. We propose xPRT ,which learns from both by making the embedding of a representative passage predict the related passages it never read (§2). • In a controlled continual pre-training comparison against five established objectives, xPRT 

is the only one that improves its starting checkpoint substantially. It gains 1.8 to 1.9 points on MTEB(eng, v2), on every task type, at every corpus scale from 100K to 1M documents. No baseline gains more than 0.4 points at any scale, and four of the five end below the checkpoint at 100K (§4.1 and §4.2). • Controlled ablations trace the gain to the cross-passage signal from curated related pas-sages. Removing cross-passage prediction and pairing each anchor with a random passage of its document costs 9.3 nDCG@10. Within-passage even given as much text, or up to five times the steps, stays between 18 .8 - 19 .2 nDCG@10, below the strongest baseline (§4.3). 

## 2 CROSS -P ASSAGE RETRIEVAL PRE -T RAINING 

Established retrieval pre-training objectives learn a passage’s embedding from the passage itself or from heuristic pairs, such as random crops or adjacent spans. xPRT instead learns from cross-passage signals : the embedding of a passage that represents its document must single out, and predict the content of, the passages of the same document that are most related to it or complements it, e.g. answer a question posed by the first passage. The encoder fθ , a 12-layer Transformer, is later deployed as the retriever. Documents are split into passages of 128 tokens, and each training example is a group g = ( a, s 1, . . . , s n): an anchor passage a and n = 4 sibling passages of the same document, ranked by their relatedness to the anchor (§2.1). For a passage x, ex is the mean of the encoder’s final hidden states, the embedding used for retrieval. During training, the encoder reads a light view mL(x) of every passage, with 15% of the tokens replaced by [MASK], and a one-layer decoder hϕ, discarded after pre-training, reads a heavy view mH (x), with 60% masked at the positions MH (x), a superset of the light mask (Appendix B). The objective combines two cross-passage terms, which carry this signal, with a self-prediction term and two standard regularizers:

## 3 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Pre-training corpora. All models are pre-trained on English web documents from FineWeb (Penedo et al., 2024). We take the first one million non-empty documents in the corpus’s released order, tokenize them with the BERT uncased vocabulary shared by all our models, and split them into 128-token passages, keeping a trailing passage only if it has at least 64 tokens; documents with fewer than five passages cannot form a group and are discarded. From this pool we build three cor-pora. 100K is a length-stratified sample of 100,000 documents, of which 40%, 30%, 21% and 9% have 5–8, 9–16, 17–32 and 33–64 passages. 1M contains every document of the million with 5–64 passages, and 500K is the subset of 1M that falls in a fixed random half of the million. 

Continual pre-training from a strong retriever (main setting). We initialize every model from E5-base-unsupervised (Wang et al., 2022), a BERT-base encoder contrastively pre-trained on large-scale weakly supervised text pairs. E5 ships without an MLM head, so we initialize the head from BERT-base-uncased, which shares its vocabulary. Pre-training continues for one pass over the groups of each corpus with batch size 512 (1 epoch over all the documents) and a peak learning rate of 10 −6 with 5% linear warm-up and linear decay; the encoder is frozen for the first 100 steps while the newly initialized components warm up (Table 4). Continual pre-training must not erase what the initial model already knows, so we apply a retention regularizer of the same form, weight and schedule to every method, baselines included (Appendix E lists the passages each method uses): a symmetric KL divergence between the in-batch similarity distributions of the current model and of the frozen initial model (Tung & Mori, 2019; Park et al., 2019). Its weight decays from 40 to 0 (Appendix E) as λret (t) = 40  1 − p(t − 1) /(T − 1)  over the T training steps, which protects the initial model early and lets the pre-training objective dominate late. 

Pre-training from BERT. To test whether the signal depends on a contrastive starting point, we also pre-train from BERT-base-uncased (Devlin et al., 2019). xPRT uses the same configuration as 5Preprint in the continual setting – four siblings per anchor and every term of Eq. 1 – but without the retention regularizer, since there is no pre-trained retriever to preserve. Training uses the 100K groups for six epochs (8,172 steps, batch 512) at learning rate 10 −4, without a backbone freeze (Table 4). The groups are the same as in the continual setting and were selected with E5, so this regime tests the objective, not a selection-free pipeline. Because each run is cheap, we also use this regime for the ablations of §4.3. 

Baselines. We compare with five retrieval-oriented pre-training objectives, using their official im-plementations adapted to our data pipeline. Four of them learn within a passage: RetroMAE (Xiao et al., 2022), masked auto-encoding with an enhanced one-layer decoder; DupMAE (Liu et al., 2023), which adds a bag-of-words objective to RetroMAE; SimLM (Wang et al., 2023), replaced-language modeling through a representation bottleneck; and BoW-DPR (Ma et al., 2024), bag-of-words prediction without a decoder. Each of them trains on the anchor passage of every group – the same passages that xPRT uses as anchors, including the multiple anchors per document – with its native masking rates and loss weights (Table 6). The fifth, Revela (Cai et al., 2026), learns from other passages: a language model (Qwen2.5-0.5B with LoRA) predicts each passage while attending to the other passages of its group, weighted by the retriever’s similarities. Each Revela group holds an anchor, its four siblings and eleven anchors of other documents. All baselines use the initialization, number of steps, batch size, learning-rate schedule and, in the continual setting, retention regularizer of xPRT . We also report the initial model, E5, without further training. 

Evaluation. We evaluate the final checkpoint of every run, without fine-tuning, on the 41 tasks of MTEB(eng, v2) (Muennighoff et al., 2023; Enevoldsen et al., 2025). The tasks span classification (8), clustering (8), pair classification (3), reranking (2), retrieval (10), semantic textual similarity (9) and summarization (1). Embeddings are mean-pooled and L2-normalized, inputs carry E5’s 

query: and passage: prefixes, the maximum length is 512 tokens, and inference runs in bf16. We report the average main score over the 41 tasks ( Avg. ) (Table 8), the number of tasks on which a model is better or worse than the initial E5 (W/L), and nDCG@10, MRR@10, Recall@10 and Recall@100 averaged over the ten retrieval tasks. We select hyperparameters for the final config through validation loss that is disjoint from the evaluation benchmark which is contrastive perplexity on the MS MARCO passage-ranking dev set (Nguyen et al., 2016). 

## 4 RESULTS 

We ask whether the relations between the passages of a document add to what a strong retriever and established objectives already capture (§4.1), where the gains appear (§4.2), and which ingredients produce them (§4.3 and §4.4). 4.1 MAIN RESULTS 

We conduct our experiments on E5, which was contrastively pre-trained on hundreds of millions of text pairs. We observe that well-established pre-training objectives could not improve it mean-ingfully (Table 1) in a continual pre-training setting. Across three corpora we observe that, the four within-passage objectives exhibit an average performance difference of about −2.05 to +0 .38 

points. Even the SimLM objective is unstable, losing 2.05 and 1.70 points at 100K and 1M data scales. Revela, which learns from other passages through a language model, ends 0.05 to 0.26 points below the E5 starting point. xPRT , with identical data and budget, improves E5 by 1.80, 1.87 and 1.83 points at 100K, 500K and 1M, on 34-36 of the 41 tasks (Table 8) and on every task type at every scale. Furthermore, its nDCG@10 at 100K, 45.78, is 6.2% above that of the strongest baseline, demonstrating the effectiveness of the proposed method. One intuition that may help explain the result is that E5 as well as most of the pre-training objec-tives in literature lack direct supervision from the relation between different passages in a single document. E5’s pairs are linked by web structure, such as a title and its body, and reconstructing a passage from its own embedding, in each of the four forms we tried, provides minimal comple-mentary value. xPRT supervises the embedding with this intra-document relations directly. It trains on the same groups as xPRT – an anchor, its four siblings and eleven anchors of other documents – but the retriever’s similarities only weight the attention of a language model that predicts each passage, so the retriever learns only indirectly, from how much each passage helps the language model. In xPRT , the relation acts on the embedding itself. Crucially, the signal does not depend on the model used as the starting point. In a controlled ablation, we observe that pre-training BERT for six epochs on the 100K groups (Table 2), xPRT scores 4.4 points above the strongest baseline, SimLM, with a 41% higher retrieval nDCG@10 (29.21 against 20.68), and it is the best of the six models on 28 of the 41 tasks (Table 11). Revela, the other objective that learns from other passages, ends 0.5 points below SimLM. The gains lie mostly in retrieval, STS, pair classification and clustering; classification and summarization are the only task types on which baselines remain ahead. 4.2 GAINS ACROSS TASK TYPES AND CORPUS SCALES 

The gains generalize across various types and corpora that xPRT is evaluated on. At 100K, gains are largest on STS (+3.5) and retrieval (+2.5 nDCG@10), which score whether two different texts mean the exact thing, and smallest on the reranking task (under 0.1), pair classification (+0.6) and clustering (+0.7). Within retrieval, the largest gains appear on tasks whose query and target are linked by an argument or a claim: Touché-2020 (+6.6), ArguAna (+5.8), FEVER (+4.1), Climate-FEVER (+3.6) and HotpotQA (+3.0) (Table 10). 4.3 CURATION OF RELATED PASSAGES IS CRITICAL 

Table 3 ablates xPRT in the BERT regime, isolating the contributions of each term in the objective and of sibling selection by removing ingredients one at a time. The decisive ingredient is the cross-passage signal from curated related passages. Removing cross-passage prediction alone lowers the average by 0.45 points and nDCG@10 by 0.75, on every task type (Table 2). Removing the cross-passage signal altogether – dropping Lcross and pairing each anchor with a random passage of its document instead of its selected sibling – costs 4.9 points on average and 9.3 of nDCG@10, 32% of the retrieval score, and falls below SimLM; a sibling from another document is worse still. More text cannot substitute for this signal: three controls that drop both cross-passage terms and give self-prediction or RetroMAE as much text as xPRT or more, with up to five times the training steps, still plateau at 18.8–19.2 nDCG@10. Random pairs fail because web pages mix genuine content with widgets, link lists, and contact details (Figure 1; Table 5) – noise inherited by random same-document pairing, as used in contrastive pre-training (Izacard et al., 2022; Gao & Callan, 2022). Just as filtering noisy pairs benefits weakly supervised pre-training (Wang et al., 2022), curating them is essential in the unsupervised setting. Once the pairs are curated, the remaining design choices matter far less. Sibling ranking method changes the average by at most 2.3 points (TF-IDF alone even gives a slightly higher nDCG@10 than the fused ranking, +0 .67 ), whereas a random passage costs 4.9. Among the loss terms, Lxp 

carries most of the gain ( −9.7 nDCG@10 when removed), LMLM is also necessary ( −3.9 points), 8Preprint since it grounds the prediction targets in the actual tokens, and likewise Lself (−2.7 points) matters, removing Lunif , by contrast, changes the average by only 0.4. 4.4 WHAT THE CROSS -PASSAGE SIGNAL CHANGES 

The cross-passage signal teaches the embedding which passages address one another, beyond which ones share words. ArguAna shows this most clearly: its target is the counter-argument of the query, it has one of the largest gains (+5.8 nDCG@10), and its debate pages, which collect points and coun-terpoints on one motion, resemble xPRT ’s groups. Re-encoding its 1,406 queries in full precision (Appendix H), xPRT ranks a passage from the query’s own debate first for 79.2% of the queries, against 73.9% for E5. The gain does not come from lexical matching: after splitting the queries into thirds by the IDF-weighted overlap between query and counter-argument, xPRT improves over E5 in every third (+3.7 to +8.8 nDCG@10), whereas BoW-DPR, whose objective is lexical, gains only in the highest-overlap third and loses 10.3 points in the lowest; CQADupstack-Gaming shows the same pattern. For the argument that legalizing drugs would reduce crime, E5 ranks first an-other argument of the same debate, which does not address crime, whereas xPRT ranks first the counter-argument that most drug crimes are committed by people on drugs (Table 7 gives six such queries). 

## 5 RELATED WORK 

Unsupervised pair construction: Lacking labelled pairs (Nguyen et al., 2016), one family builds pseudo-relevant pairs from an unlabelled corpus and trains the pooled vector contrastively (van den Oord et al., 2018): inverse cloze pairs a sentence with its context (Lee et al., 2019; Chang et al., 2020), Contriever and SPIDER pair two crops of one document (Izacard et al., 2022; Ram et al., 2022), coCondenser adds a corpus-aware term over a bottleneck (Gao & Callan, 2022), and weakly supervised mining filters noisy pairs at scale (Wang et al., 2022); supervised pipelines then add hard negatives and cross-encoder filtering (Xiong et al., 2021; Qu et al., 2021). In all of them the positive is fixed by position or by chance, so a pair counts as equally relevant whether its two passages share a subject or only a URL. 

Bottleneck auto-encoding: A second family reconstructs a passage through the pooled vector, weakening the decoder so that the vector is the only route to the target (Lu et al., 2021; Gao & Callan, 2021; Xiao et al., 2022; Li et al., 2023b). Later variants change what is reconstructed rather than where it comes from: a bag-of-words term (Liu et al., 2023), replaced-language modeling (Wang et al., 2023), a lexicon bottleneck (Shen et al., 2023), or the passage’s own words with no decoder at all (Ma et al., 2024). Every target still comes from the passage just read. 

Supervision that leaves the passage: CoT-MAE reconstructs one neighbouring span alongside the input (Wu et al., 2023), and Revela reaches the retriever through a language model that predicts each passage while attending to the others in its batch (Cai et al., 2026). Our machinery is standard: soft targets from a better-informed view (Hinton et al., 2015), distillation that transfers geometry rather than logits (Park et al., 2019; Tung & Mori, 2019), and InfoNCE read through alignment and uniformity (Wang & Isola, 2020). What xPRT adds is which passages to supervise (§2.1), which separates it from contrastive objectives whose positive is a random crop, an adjacent span or a structural counterpart, from auto-encoders whose targets never leave the encoded passage, and from Revela, where the relation between passages reaches the retriever through a language model’s attention rather than through the embedding. 

## 6 CONCLUSION 

Established retrieval pre-training objectives leave unused how the passages of a document relate to one another. xPRT selects representative anchors and their most related passages, aligns their embeddings and predicts the related passages through the anchor’s embedding. Under a controlled protocol, it is the only objective that improves E5 substantially, by 1.8–1.9 points on MTEB(eng, v2) and on every task type at every corpus scale, and from BERT it outperforms every baseline by 4.4 points or more; ablations trace the gain to the cross-passage signal from curated related passages. 9Preprint AI USE STATEMENT

## A THEORETICAL ANALYSIS 

Our experiments establish two facts. Cross-passage prediction outperforms within-passage recon-struction (Table 3), and reconstruction-based pretrainers such as RetroMAE and DupMAE end be-low the retriever they are initialized from (Table 1). The first fact is expected. The second is not: a weaker training signal should yield a smaller gain, not a loss. The analysis explains both through a single mechanism. 1. The text pairs dictate what any of the objectives learns. Under squared error or KL, the optimal predictor is the average target given the input. When the target is another passage of the same document, this average removes everything except what the two passages share: their subject, which is exactly what relevance depends on (Theorem A.1). 2. Reconstruction makes the subject compete with passage-specific content. When the decoder also reads a masked copy of the target and the conditioning vector has limited capacity, reconstruction pays for each kind of information in proportion to how often the copy hides it. Topical words recur within a passage, so the copy reveals the subject more often than passage-specific content, and the subject loses the competition for capacity. In the limit of an unrestricted encoder, the optimum carries no information about the subject at all (Theorem A.4). This is consistent with the degradation. 3. The conditions are made explicit. The results assume that sibling passages share nothing beyond their subject and that queries are encoded like passages. They therefore also indi-cate where cross-passage training should help less: siblings that share non-topical content, and queries unlike passages. This result highlights promising directions for future work by improving how cross-passage training pairs are constructed for self-supervised retrieval model training. The analysis does not imply that within-passage signals are useless: removing Lself from the full ob-jective lowers performance (Table 3). Cross-passage prediction supplies the subject; within-passage prediction can complement it, but on its own it puts the subject at a disadvantage. The results con-cern the optima of the objectives, not training dynamics, and Theorems A.3 and A.4 hold in a model of passages rather than on web data. Section I states the setting and the results. The proofs in Section A.2 can be skipped without losing the argument. A.1 SETTING AND RESULTS

Reading Theorem A.4. Part (ii) is the general picture. Reconstruction pays for each kind of information in proportion to how often the decoder’s copy hides it. Topical words recur within a passage, so the copy reveals the subject more often than a specific fact ( βY > β ν ), and the subject loses the competition for capacity. Part (iii) is the limit: an encoder that may also combine the two components drops the subject entirely, using codes such as (Y + νx) mod m that are informative only together with the copy. Cross-passage prediction involves no such competition. The sibling’s specific content cannot be predicted from the anchor, so the subject is the only information worth encoding. The comparison with Theorem A.3 isolates the cause: without the copy, the within-passage optimum contains the subject term M p (v); with it, the subject is at a disadvantage.

## G EXTENDED RELATED WORK 

Contrastive pre-training for retrieval. Without labels, positive pairs have been built with the inverse cloze task (Lee et al., 2019), body–first-section and wiki-link pairs (Chang et al., 2020), recurring spans (Ram et al., 2022) and random cropping (Izacard et al., 2022), and combined with corpus-aware pre-training on spans of the same document (Gao & Callan, 2022); large-scale weakly supervised pairs, filtered with a consistency check, underlie today’s general-purpose embedders (Wang et al., 2022; Li et al., 2023a). xPRT shares this contrastive form and changes the pair. Spider pairs passages of a document that share a recurring span, a lexical notion of relatedness; xPRT pairs an anchor that must represent its document with the passage most related to it by fused dense and lexical similarity, and every document contributes several such pairs. Our ablations show that this selection matters (§4.3), and xPRT adds a signal on top of a model pre-trained contrastively on weakly supervised pairs rather than replacing such pre-training (§4.1). 

Bottleneck auto-encoding. Weak decoders force information into a single vector in SEED (Lu et al., 2021), Condenser (Gao & Callan, 2021), RetroMAE (Xiao et al., 2022) and its duplex ex-tension DupMAE (Liu et al., 2023), SimLM with replaced-token detection (Wang et al., 2023), and LexMAE with a lexicon bottleneck (Shen et al., 2023); BoW-DPR drops the decoder in favor of bag-of-words prediction (Ma et al., 2024). The closest work is CoT-MAE (Wu et al., 2023), which reconstructs a span with the help of the embedding of its adjacent span. xPRT uses prediction dif-ferently: as content supervision alongside a contrastive objective, with targets drawn from related passages anywhere in the document. 

Learning from related passages. Revela (Cai et al., 2026) trains a retriever through language modeling: a language model predicts each passage of a batch while attending to the other passages, weighted by the retriever’s similarities, so the retriever learns which passages help to predict a given one. xPRT shares the premise that related passages carry a training signal, but it selects the related passages within a document, makes the retriever’s own embedding align with them and predict them, and needs no language model. Under our protocol, Revela does not improve E5 (§4.1) and, from BERT, stays below SimLM (§4). 27 Preprint 

Predictive self-distillation. Predicting a model’s own targets from a privileged view underlies self-supervised learning in vision and speech (Grill et al., 2020; Caron et al., 2021; Baevski et al., 2022; Assran et al., 2023). xPRT uses such targets in its within-passage term Lself , where the prediction must pass through the passage’s own embedding; its cross-passage term Lcross instead predicts the actual masked tokens of a related passage. 

Conditioning on continuous vectors. Prefix and prompt tuning condition a frozen or shared model on a few continuous vectors (Li & Liang, 2021; Lester et al., 2021). Gist tokens, Auto-Compressor and ICAE compress contexts into such vectors for later generation (Mu et al., 2023; Chevalier et al., 2023; Ge et al., 2024). xPRT uses the same interface as a training-time bottleneck and asks the vectors to support prediction of other text rather than reconstruction of the compressed one. 

Continual pre-training and forgetting. Continued pre-training adapts models to new data (Guru-rangan et al., 2020) but can erase prior capabilities (Kirkpatrick et al., 2016; Li & Hoiem, 2018). Our retention regularizer follows similarity-preserving and relational distillation (Tung & Mori, 2019; Park et al., 2019): it preserves the initial model’s in-batch similarity structure and is annealed away during training.
