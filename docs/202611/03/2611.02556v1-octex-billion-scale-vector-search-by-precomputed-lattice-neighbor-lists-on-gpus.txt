# OctEx: Billion-Scale Vector Search by Precomputed Lattice Neighbor Lists on GPUs 

## Dongfang Zhao (dzhao@uw.edu) 

## https://github.com/hpdic/octex 

## Abstract 

Approximate nearest-neighbor search on GPUs serves retrieval-augmented generation, recommendation, and dedu-plication over datasets of up to billions of vectors. The fastest GPU indexes at high recall, such as CAGRA, walk proximity graphs in chained rounds, each of which waits for the dis-tances of the previous one. High recall therefore costs many dependent rounds per query. Our insight is to spend the HBM of the GPUs on precomputed neighbor lists, so that a query reads its candidates in one parallel step instead of a chain of rounds. This paper turns the above insight into a GPU vec-tor search system, namely OctEx, which holds hundreds of randomly projected copies of the dataset in GPU memory. Every copy rounds its vectors to the nearest points of a reg-ular grid whose cells are nearly round and stores for every populated cell the list of vectors nearest to its center. A query reads the lists of its cells in all copies at once and then, in a fixed number of waves, the precomputed neighbor lists of its best hits. OctEx ranks the union of all hits by an int8 sketch before one exact rerank. OctEx runs the whole search of a batch as CUDA graphs over all GPUs of a server and places every part of its index in HBM or host memory by its access frequency. On four H100 GPUs at recall 0.95 to 0.995, OctEx outperforms CAGRA by 2.0 × to 46.4 × on seven datasets of up to one hundred million vectors, and by 1.46 × to 2.05 ×

on the one billion vectors of Deep1B and SIFT1B, whose indexes it builds in 44 to 47 minutes. OctEx also outperforms GGNN, PathWeaver, FlowANN, and the cuVS inverted file indexes IVF-PQ and IVF-RaBitQ by 5.4 × to 343 ×.

## 1 Introduction 

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) [44], dense passage retrieval such as DPR [40] and ColBERT [41], and the dedupli-cation of training data such as SemDeDup [1] require approx-imate nearest-neighbor (ANN) search at high recall, because every missed neighbor removes a relevant passage or keeps a duplicate. These applications search datasets of hundreds of millions to billions of embeddings, for instance the Deep1B benchmark [9] and the billion-scale challenge [61]. A modern GPU server holds several hundred gigabytes of HBM and tens of thousands of cores. The natural target for such a server is a search that turns this memory and this parallelism into throughput at high recall. Existing GPU indexes follow two designs, of which prox-imity graphs pay for recall with a chain of dependent rounds and inverted file (IVF) indexes pay with scans of whole in-verted lists. Proximity-graph indexes such as CAGRA [53], GGNN [25], SONG [72], and BANG [64] adapt CPU graphs such as HNSW [47], NSG [19], and DiskANN [34] to the GPU. Each of their iterations expands the best unvisited ver-tices, which depend on the distances of the previous iteration. A query thus advances through a chain of rounds whose length grows with the recall target. IVF indexes such as IVF-PQ in Faiss [37] and cuVS [50] and the recent IVF-RaBitQ [60] avoid the chain, as a query probes a fixed number of inverted lists in one pass. However, IVF indexes scan every com-pressed code of every probed list, which keeps their through-put on four H100 GPUs one to two orders of magnitude below CAGRA in our measurements. Figure 1(a) quantifies the re-sulting cost on Deep1B, where CAGRA on four H100 GPUs falls from 156k queries per second (QPS) at recall 0.992 to 87k at recall 0.995 as each further 0.003 of recall needs a longer chain of rounds. On the same GPUs, IVF-RaBitQ re-mains below 28k QPS. Breaking the chain requires candidates that do not depend on an earlier round. Precomputed lists of nearest vectors pro-vide such candidates: a query that finds the list stored for its own position reads its neighbors in one step. Lists keyed by positions in the original space are, however, infeasible in hundreds of dimensions, because a ball around the query in-tersects almost every partition of the space before it reaches the tenth neighbor [11]. The lists must therefore be keyed by cells of low-dimensional projections and computed at build time, since an expansion at query time would reintroduce the chain. This paper turns the above insight into a GPU vector search system, namely OctEx (Octa Explosion), which spends the HBM of the GPUs on these lists. OctEx projects the dataset into hundreds of random copies and rounds every block of eight projected coordinates to the nearest point of the E8 lattice [14], a regular grid whose cells are the roundest known in eight dimensions. For every populated cell, OctEx stores the list of the vectors nearest to its center. At search time, a query decodes its own cell in every copy and reads the stored lists of these cells. The best hits become start points, whose precomputed neighbor lists the search reads in a fixed number of waves. ## 2 Background and Motivation 2.1 GPU Vector Search 

The first GPU index family connects every vector to a few dozen neighbors in a proximity graph, in which a query starts from entry vertices and repeatedly expands the closest un-visited ones. CAGRA [53] builds a fixed-degree graph for the GPU and searches it with one thread block per query, which makes it the standard GPU baseline in cuVS [50]. Other GPU graph indexes such as GGNN [25], SONG [72], and BANG [64] follow the same expansion loop with differ-ent graphs and memory layouts. For billion-scale datasets, FlowANN [71] extends CAGRA with a tiered graph that keeps part of the graph in host memory of one GPU server. The second family, inverted file (IVF) indexes, clusters the dataset and scans the inverted lists of the clusters nearest to a query. The IVF-PQ index of Faiss [37] and cuVS [50] com-presses every vector by product quantization [39], whereas IVF-RaBitQ [60] replaces the codes by RaBitQ [22] with an error bound per distance. Inverted multi-indexes [8] and opti-mized product quantization [23] refine the coarse quantizer and the codes. In all of these indexes, a query reads every code of every probed cluster before an exact refinement of its candidates, which makes its cost grow with the cluster size rather than with the number of true neighbors. 22.2 Chained Rounds of Graph Search 

The expansion loop of a graph search is a chain of dependent rounds, because the vertices that one round expands are the best results of the previous round. Although CAGRA com-putes the distances of all neighbors of the expanded vertices of one round in parallel, the next round can start only after a sort of the candidate list [53]. A higher recall target needs a longer candidate list and more rounds per query, while the GPU threads of a block wait at the end of every round for the slowest distance. Figure 1(a) shows the consequence on Deep1B, where the throughput of CAGRA falls by 2.1 × from recall 0.989 to 0.995. The chain also limits the use of the memory of the server. A graph of one billion vertices with degree 32 occupies 119 GiB beside 179 GiB of half-precision vectors, which fills 298 GiB of the 372 GiB of HBM of four H100 GPUs with one copy of the CAGRA index. Additional memory cannot shorten the chain, as every round still depends on the last. In contrast, a search whose steps are independent could spend the memory on precomputed results and finish in a constant number of parallel steps per query. 

## 2.3 Search by Precomputed Lists 

A search with a constant number of steps needs candidates that do not depend on an earlier round. Precomputed lists of nearest vectors provide them, as long as a query finds its list by a computation on the query alone. A space partition gives such a computation, in which the query decodes its own cell and reads the list stored for that cell. Because the volume of a ball in hundreds of dimensions concentrates near its surface [11], the partition must be built in random low-dimensional projections. Random projections preserve distances in a few dozen dimensions up to a small dis-tortion [38]. Within each projection, a lattice quantizer such as 

E8 partitions the space into cells of nearly spherical shape [14]. Formally, the E8 lattice consists of points in R8 whose coordi-nates are either all integers or all half-integers and sum to an even integer: E8 = {x ∈ Z8 ∪ (Z + 12 )8 | ∑8 

> i=1

xi ∈ 2Z} [14]. Its Voronoi cells center the densest packing of equal balls in eight dimensions [14, 65]. The vectors that share the cell of the query in a projection are therefore mostly near the query. Earlier CPU and disk indexes such as C2LSH [21], QALSH [31], and SuCo [70] enumerate the cell members of each random projection at query time and count them on the CPU. Their candidates are the members of the cells of a query, whose number varies from query to query and from cell to cell, whereas GPUs require fixed-shape parallel searches. 

## 2.4 Technical Challenges 

Turning precomputed lists into a GPU search raises the fol-lowing technical challenges: • Most cells that queries fall into are empty at fine spacing, as a product of multiple E8 blocks has far more cells than a dataset has vectors. The lists must hence belong to cells that hold data and be found without a search for nearby cells at query time. Fragments at several spacings, each populated one with a stored fragment ripple, meet both conditions (§§3.2 and 3.3). • Part of the neighbors lie outside every cell of the query, a share that grows with the dataset, and must be reached without a chain of dependent rounds. Waves over neigh-bor rows, ranked by an int8 sketch, reach them in a fixed number of steps (§§3.4 and 3.5). • The lists of all copies overlap and differ in length, so their union needs deduplication and ranking with few kernel launches per wave. Fixed-shape CUDA graphs and fused merge kernels provide both (§§4.1 and 4.2). • At one billion vectors, the projected copies, ranking sketches, neighbor lists, and raw vectors together ex-ceed aggregate GPU HBM, while host memory serves random vector lookups at a small fraction of HBM band-width, and the lists of hundreds of copies threaten to make the build slow. OctEx places every part of its in-dex in HBM or host memory by its access frequency (§§4.3 and 4.4) and builds all fragment ripples of a bil-lion vectors in minutes by sorting the members of every fragment (§4.5).

For the same reason, the sketch rows and the vector ripples stay in HBM, split by rows over all GPUs when one GPU cannot hold them. We also call the vector ripples the neighbor rows. In contrast, the final rerank reads only R half-precision vectors per query. When the dataset exceeds the HBM of the GPUs, the half-precision vectors consequently live in host memory.

Each copy of the dataset projects every vector x ∈ RD into 8b dimensions and partitions the projection into products of

The b decoded points form the fragment of x in copy c at spacing f , as Section 3.1 defines it. The spacing controls the size of a fragment and with it the distance up to which vectors share the fragment of the query. A fine spacing gives small fragments that separate near from far vectors sharply but leave most queries in fragments without data. A coarse spacing gives fragments that almost always hold data but mix near and far vectors. To obtain both, OctEx decodes every vector at L spacings f1 < · · · < fL, from which a query uses the finest spacing whose fragment holds a stored list. The probability that a vector shares the fragment of the query decreases with their projected distance. Because the shift of a copy is uniform, the query falls at a uniform position within its own cell. A vector then shares the fragment of the query with the probability that this position stays inside the cell after a move by their projected difference, which is the share of the cell that overlaps its own translate by this difference. The cell of a lattice is convex, so this overlap can only shrink as the difference grows in any direction [14]. The b blocks of a fragment have independent shifts, which multiplies their probabilities. A fragment therefore holds near vectors with higher proba-bility than far vectors. Every copy draws its own rotation and shift, which makes the events of different copies independent. Over C copies, the probability thus turns into an expected number of hits, which makes the union over copies contain the nearest vectors with a probability that rises with C.

## 3.3 Stored Ripples 

Because the fragment of a query holds no data in most copies at fine spacing, OctEx stores for every populated fragment the list of vectors nearest to its center, its fragment ripple. For each copy, spacing, and fragment that at least pmin vectors fall into, the build computes the list of the l vectors nearest to the center of the fragment in the projection of the copy, sorted by distance. A query that falls into a stored fragment reads the first r ≤ l entries of its list. A query whose fragment is not stored at the finest spacing instead moves to the next coarser spacing, where fragments hold more data. The stored list approximates the list of the vectors nearest to the query, given that the query lies near the center of its fragment. The approximation weakens as the offset ε of the query from the center grows. Let τr denote the projected distance of the r-th entry of the list to the center. By the triangle inequality, a vector whose projected distance to the query is below τr − ε lies closer to the center than τr, so that the first r entries contain every such vector and cover this radius in full. The reach τr − ε of a list depends on the spacing and the list length, while the pruning threshold pmin sets its memory. The offset ε is at most the covering radius of the fragment, which is the largest distance from a point of its cell to its center. For a product of b cells of E8, this radius is √b, since the covering radius of E8 is 1 [14]. Before the division by the spacing 

f , the offset in the projection is thus at most f √b, which a finer spacing reduces. A longer list instead increases τr.The pruning threshold costs little coverage, since a fragment that holds few vectors also receives few queries when the queries follow the data distribution. As a result, dropping such a fragment below pmin moves only those queries to the next spacing, at a small loss of recall. 

## 3.4 Waves over Neighbor Rows 

OctEx reaches the neighbors that the first round misses by waves, each of which adds one dependent step. The first round of stored ripples finds most of the nearest neighbors but misses those that fall into other fragments in almost every copy. The vector ripples of Section 3.1 serve this purpose: the neighbor row of every vector lists its nearest vectors in the full space. In each wave, the S best entries found so far read their neighbor rows. Since a wave reads S rows in parallel and depends only on the ranking of the previous wave, a search with W waves has W + 1 dependent steps in total, however long its candidate pool. A neighbor row that lists only the nearest neighbors of its owner misses vectors whose own neighbors are near the owner but which no near vector lists. OctEx therefore builds each row from k f forward neighbors, up to kr reverse entries, and the remaining forward neighbors, where a reverse entry of 

u is a vector v that lists u among its first ks forward neighbors. Reverse entries give every vector a path back from the vectors that consider it near, along which a wave reaches every vector from the start points near it. From one hundred million vectors on, OctEx builds the neighbor rows by its own search, while smaller datasets use exact rows. The first version of the rows comes from the first round alone, with every base vector as a query and the vector itself removed. Each later version runs the full search with waves over the reverse rows of the previous version, which raises the quality of the rows with each refinement.

The sketch stores for every vector its first principal coordinates in eight bits with one scale per coordinate. Each sketch distance reads one row of 64 to 128 bytes. The exact rerank thus needs only a few dozen entries whenever the sketch ranks the true neighbors among its best entries. The best S entries by sketch distance become the start points. ## 4 Implementation 

The implementation of OctEx consists of 11,425 lines of code. At its core, a CUDA C++ library of 5,452 lines forms the module cuvs::neighbors::octex of cuVS [50]. Its search path takes 2,716 lines with 10 CUDA kernels and covers the loading of an index onto the GPUs and the search of a batch of queries. The builder takes another 2,736 lines with 16 kernels and computes the copies, the fragment ripples, the sketch, and the neighbor rows on the GPUs, with the radix sort of CUB for the fragment ripples of billion-vector datasets and the merges, and the IVF-Flat index of cuVS for the ripples of smaller ones. The C and Python interfaces take 701 and 1,322 lines. Unit tests take 1,421 lines. Command-line tools, examples, and further tests take the remaining 2,529 lines. The source code of OctEx is available on GitHub 1. The Python package is published on PyPI as octex 2. The release offers five interfaces: • C++: build , load , and search in the style of cuVS, with queries in host or device memory. • C: cuvsOctexBuild , cuvsOctexLoad , and 

cuvsOctexSearch on DLPack tensors, following the C API of cuVS. • Python: octex.build , octex.load , and 

Index.search on NumPy, CuPy, or PyTorch arrays, or any array with DLPack or the CUDA array interface, without copies. • Drop-ins: from octex import cuvs as cagra for the CAGRA calls of cuVS and from octex import faiss for the GPU index calls of Faiss, each replacing one import in existing code. • Command line: octex_build and octex_bench ,which build indexes and run the measurements of Section 5. 

## 4.1 One CUDA Graph per Batch 

OctEx captures the search of a batch on each GPU as CUDA graphs [51], one for the first round and one for the exchange of start points and all waves. Every kernel of the graph has a fixed shape: the projection is one matrix multiplication over all copies; similarly, the decoding, gather, and rerank kernels assign a fixed number of threads to every query. Queries that need fewer entries than the maximum skip work inside the kernels instead of changing the launch shape, which spares the host any synchronization inside a search. Every step is a CUDA kernel or a cuBLAS call in C++, with no framework in the search path. Every per-query structure inside the graph takes a size that follows from the maximum number of entries of the query. The deduplication of a union uses an open-addressing hash set in the shared memory of the block [6]. Such a set is one fixed array, in which an entry that finds its slot taken moves on to the next free slot. Its size is the next power of two above 1.5 times the maximum number of entries of the query, at most 32,768 slots. The gather kernel inserts the list entries of a query into this set and splits them evenly over the threads of the block by a prefix sum of the list lengths. One lane per 16 sketch coordinates of each new entry then computes its sketch distance. Similarly, the kernel that keeps the S start points sorts the next power of two above S + R keys of distance and identifier by a bitonic network [10], instead of a fixed size of 1,024 keys. The exact rerank selects the R best entries by

## 4.2 Fused Merge Kernels 

Two fused kernels merge the start points of a query and select those of the next wave, each in one thread block per query. The merge kernel radix-selects the S sketch-nearest of the new entries of a query in one thread block and then sorts their keys of distance and identifier together with the kept start points by a bitonic network in shared memory. Because the same identifier always carries the same sketch distance, a repeated identifier becomes an equal key and collapses during the sort. The selection kernel inserts the identifiers that earlier waves expanded into a hash set in shared memory and takes the first 

S kept entries, in order, that the set does not contain. Both kernels return the same start points as a merge by sorting, up to the order among equal sketch distances. Since each kernel keeps its intermediate keys in shared memory, a wave issues one launch for the merge and one for the selection per batch. The merge also removes the duplicates that the start points of successive waves would otherwise carry into the next wave. 

## 4.3 Multi-GPU Execution in One Process 

OctEx drives all G GPUs of a server from one host thread in one process. Each GPU holds a 1 /G share of the copies and answers every query of the batch with them, which runs the first round of each query on all GPUs at once. A peer-gather kernel inside the CUDA graph then merges the start points of each query from the other GPUs over NVLink, after which each GPU runs the waves for 1 /G of the queries. With CUDA events ordering the two graphs of all GPUs without host barriers, the host issues the whole batch in one pass over the GPUs. The wave graph of each GPU starts only after the first-round graphs of all GPUs have recorded their events, which keeps the exchange of start points out of the critical path of the host. Small datasets can instead replicate the whole index on every GPU, which then answers its own share of the queries without exchange. OctEx splits the sketch rows and the neighbor rows by rows over the G GPUs and reads them through peer pointers. The half-precision vectors are split in the same way when they would otherwise crowd out copies. Every kernel receives an array of device pointers to the row ranges of each split part. ## 4.4 Tiered Placement by Access Frequency 

OctEx places every part of its index by how often a query reads it. The first round reads Cr list entries per query. Ev-ery wave reads the sketch rows and the neighbor rows of thousands of entries at random positions. These parts stay in HBM, which serves random reads of short rows two orders of magnitude faster than host memory over PCIe. When the copies, sketch rows, and neighbor rows do not fit together, OctEx shrinks them instead of moving any of them to the host. The sketch kernels accept any width that is a multiple of 16 and leave the extra lanes of the last group idle. Each copy can also keep only the first entries of every list, which are the nearest to the center of the fragment. The final rerank reads only R half-precision vectors per query and is the only step that reads host memory. When these vectors do not fit into the HBM that the other parts leave, OctEx keeps them in host memory. It allocates them as anonymous memory and fills them from the file in sequential chunks, without a mapping of the file. With this placement, PCIe carries only the final rerank, and every round before it stays on the HBM and NVLink of the GPUs. To verify this split, we sample the hardware counters of the four H100 GPUs at 20 kHz over 30 batches of 10,000 queries on Deep1B with 512 start points and four waves. Figure 3 plots the raw samples of one batch on one GPU. Through the fragment ripples and all four waves, the SMs stay busy and the GPU reads the sketch rows and neighbor rows of its peers over NVLink at up to 65% of its bandwidth. PCIe stays at most 1% busy in these phases. The final rerank alone reads host memory, for 3.5 ms of the 33-ms batch at up to 13% of the PCIe bandwidth. ## 4.5 Fast Build by Sorting and Sketch Ranking 

The build of a billion-vector index spends its time on two nearest-neighbor problems: the fragment ripples of hundreds of copies and the neighbor row of every vector. Both grow with the dataset, the first also with the number of copies. Oc-tEx solves the first by sorting instead of searching and the second by ranking without full vectors, and it hides the I/O of the build behind the GPU work. The fragment ripples need no search over the dataset. The pass that hashes the vectors of a copy also computes the projected distance of every vector to the center of its fragment. A radix sort by this distance, followed by a stable radix sort by fragment key, orders the members of every fragment by distance, and each stored fragment keeps its first l members as its ripple. A copy thus costs one streaming pass over the dataset and two sorts per spacing. At one billion vectors, every stored fragment holds at least pmin = 100 members, many more than the l = 16 entries of a ripple. Smaller datasets hold few vectors per fragment and instead search their ripples with the IVF-Flat index of cuVS [50]. At one billion vectors, the neighbor rows come from two passes of the search of OctEx over all base vectors: the first round alone, then two waves over the rows of the first pass (§3.4). Both passes rank by sketch distance only and skip the final rerank, so that they read no half-precision vectors from host memory. The builder loads the index once for both passes and replaces only the neighbor rows between them. It writes the lists and the sketch in a background thread while the passes run and page-locks the dataset in place instead of copying it into new pinned memory.

## 5.3 Ablation and Sensitivity 

We ablate two design choices of OctEx, the reverse edges of the neighbor rows and the system optimizations of the search, and vary the two parameters that set its reach, the start points and the copies. Each panel of Figure 5 varies one of them on the largest dataset at which it matters. Both design choices pay off: the reverse edges halve the missed neighbors at a small cost, and the fused merge kernels and the CUDA graphs raise the throughput on every dataset. Figure 5(a) ablates the reverse edges of the neighbor rows on Deep100M with two waves. A row without reverse edges lists only the nearest vectors of its owner, whereas a row of Oc-tEx of the same length also lists vectors that count the owner among their own nearest. The reverse edges cut the missed neighbors from 2.2% to 0.9% at read depth 32 and by more than half at every read depth, while the throughput drops by only 2.6% to 3.7%. Figure 5(b) ablates the two search kernel optimizations one at a time and reports the throughput relative to the full search at the same recall. Without the fused merge kernels, which select the start points in shared memory, the merge must sort all candidates in global memory. The through-put then falls to 23% on SIFT1M, 37% on Deep100M, and 65% on Deep1B. Without CUDA graphs, the host launches every kernel and synchronizes between the first round and the waves, which costs 9% on SIFT1M, whose batches take only 1.4 ms, and at most 2.3% on the larger datasets. The start points, the copies, and the waves set the reach of OctEx, and at one billion vectors the waves take most of its time. Figure 5(c) varies the start points on Deep1B, each line from three to five waves. Every further increase extends the frontier toward higher recall. With the waves fixed at five, tuning only the start points loses at most 1.9% of throughput against the best configuration at every recall target from 0.97 to 0.995, which makes them the main tuning parameter of a loaded index. Figure 5(d) varies the copies on Deep100M and shows that copies and waves can replace each other: 64 copies with two waves reach both higher recall and higher throughput than one wave over 192 copies. Figure 5(e) splits the batch time by phase. The waves, which include the final rerank, take 12% to 48% of it up to one hundred million vectors but 88% on Deep1B. As a result, the cost of a wave decides the throughput at one billion vectors.

## 6 Related Work 

Graph and inverted file indexes. Proximity-graph indexes such as HNSW [47], NSG [19], and DiskANN [34] expand the best unvisited vertices of a query round by round. CA-GRA [53], GGNN [25], SONG [72], and BANG [64] run this loop on GPUs. FlowANN [71] tiers a CAGRA graph across GPU and host memory, whereas PathWeaver [42] pipelines a sharded CAGRA search across GPUs. PipeANN [26] aligns the same loop with SSD reads. Their graphs are neighbor ta-bles refined in the spirit of NN-Descent [17], with the reverse links that HNSW [47] and NSG [19] add for connectivity. In-verted file indexes in Faiss [18,37] and cuVS [50] instead scan the codes of the probed lists, compressed by product quanti-zation [23, 39], inverted multi-indexes [8], or RaBitQ [22, 60]. Milvus [66], Manu [27], Starling [67], FusionANNS [63], and Helmsman [32] distribute or tier such indexes across ma-chines, memory, and storage. SPANN [12] and SOAR [62] spend memory on recall by assigning a vector to several lists, but every query still scans the lists that it probes. OctEx reads the same kind of neighbor table as a graph walk and spends memory as SPANN and SOAR do, yet it expands all start points of a query at once in a fixed number of waves and reads precomputed lists instead of scanning partitions. 

Random projections. Locality-sensitive hashing [4, 15, 33] and its multi-probe variants [46] hash vectors into the cells of random projections, where Jégou et al. [35] use the E8 lattice as the quantizer. C2LSH [21] and QALSH [31] count colli-sions over one-dimensional projections, as SuCo [70] does over subspaces. GPU implementations [55, 69] accelerate the bucket lookups of such indexes. All of them store which vectors fall into each bucket and return the members of the buckets of a query, whose number varies with the population of each bucket. OctEx keeps the random projections and the 

E8 quantizer of these indexes [35] but reads a fixed number of entries per copy, which gives every query the same work on the GPU. 

Approximate Voronoi diagrams. The approximate Voronoi diagrams of Har-Peled [29] and Arya et al. [5] store an answer per cell of a partition, so that a query reads the answer of its own cell instead of searching. Their number of cells, however, grows exponentially with the dimension. OctEx stores such answers only in low-dimensional projec-tions: for every populated fragment, the list of its nearest vectors, which reaches beyond the fragment by the radius of Section 3.3. Its waves then reach the neighbors that no copy holds, which no stored answer per cell provides. 

Scheduling irregular search on GPUs. Irregular graph workloads face the same imbalance as a query whose lists differ in length. Hong et al. [30] assign whole adjacency lists to warps, whereas Merrill et al. [48], Davidson et al. [16], and Gunrock [68] spread the lists of a frontier evenly over the threads by prefix sums and searches, in the manner of merge path [24]. The gather kernel of OctEx follows the second ap-proach for the lists of a query. Selecting the best candidates is a second recurring step, which Faiss performs by warp-level k-selection [37] and Dr. Top-k by delegate-centric selection [20]. The fused merge kernels of OctEx instead build on GPU hash tables [6], radix sorting [2, 59], and bitonic networks [10], which also remove duplicate candidates. Persistent-thread designs [28] keep one kernel resident and schedule the work inside it. Nimble [43] schedules the kernels of deep learn-ing models ahead of time to cut their launch overhead. With fixed kernel shapes, OctEx goes further and replays the whole search of a batch as CUDA graphs [51], whereas persistent kernels and per-query launches decide their work at run time. 

## 7 Conclusion 

In this paper, we present OctEx, a GPU index that reaches high recall in a fixed number of parallel steps. Our design is inspired by the observation that a query’s nearest neighbors are often found in the same fragments of a lattice, which we exploit by storing the nearest vectors of every fragment as ripples. OctEx replaces the search rounds with stored ripples: a query reads the precomputed lists of its lattice fragments in hundreds of random projections at once and then the neighbor rows of its best hits in a few waves. We open-source OctEx as a CUDA module that builds the index of one billion vectors in 44 to 47 minutes, runs each batch as CUDA graphs over all GPUs, and moves only the vectors of the final rerank to host memory; moreover, we provide Python and C interfaces and drop-in replacements for Faiss and cuVS to facilitate its integration into existing systems. We evaluate OctEx on nine datasets of up to one billion vectors, where it outperforms the state-of-the-art GPU graph systems and inverted file indexes at every recall from 0.95 to 0.995, by up to 46.4 × over CAGRA and by 5.4 × to 343 × over the other baselines. 12 References
