Abstract 

Graph-based nearest neighbor search algorithms like HNSW, DiskANN, NSG, and others have surged in popularity in recent years due to their excellent empirical performance in comparison to space-partitioning methods like IVF data structures and Locality Sensitive Hashing (LSH). However, in contrast to LSH and related methods, obtaining strong worst-case theoretical guarantees for graph-based search has proven challenging. In this paper, we prove a strong lower bound that helps explain the lack of progress towards such guarantees. We show that no standard graph-based method (any that runs a variant of greedy search on a flat or hierarchical graph) can achieve sublinear query time for approximate nearest neighbor search in Euclidean space, even for an arbitrarily large approximation factor. On the other hand, we prove that sublinear query time is possible if we allow for Steiner points in the search graph. We obtain bounds comparable to LSH, showing e.g., that graph-based search with Steiner points can solve c-approximate nearest neighbor search in Euclidean space with query time ˜O(n1/(c−1) ). We complement this result with initial experiments exploring how Steiner points can be incorporated into practical graph-based methods. 

# 1 Introduction 

High-dimensional nearest neighbor search is a fundamental, and notoriously difficult, algorithmic problem. The goal is to pre-process a vector database X = {x1, . . . , x n} ⊂ Rd so that we can efficiently answer “nearest neighbor queries”. That is, given any q ∈ Rd, the goal is to quickly find a closest (or near-closest) vector to q

in X. Closeness is commonly measured by Euclidean distance or cosine similarity, but other metrics are also studied [Indyk and Motwani, 1998, Jayaram et al., 2024, Dhulipala et al., 2024]. Nearest neighbor search has been studied for decades, becoming increasingly important as the problem now serves as a backbone for document and multimedia search systems based on machine-learned vector embeddings [Lewis et al., 2020, Bruch, 2024, Douze et al., 2026, Simhadri et al., 2025]. While it can be solved naively in O(dn ) time by simply comparing q to every vector in X, in many settings, the database 

X can involve billions or trillions of vectors with 100s to 1000s of dimensions. The goal is thus to design data structures that permit search in time sublinear in n. While infeasible for exact search, sublinear-time is known to be possible if we allow for approximation [Kleinberg, 1997, Kushilevitz et al., 1998]. The most well-known sublinear-time technique with provable guarantees is the Locality Sensitive Hashing 

(LSH) framework of Indyk and Motwani [1998]. Focusing on Euclidean distance for concreteness, LSH methods return, for any query q and chosen approximation factor c ≥ 1, a vector ˜x ∈ X such that, with high probability, ∥˜x − q∥2≤ c · min x∈X ∥x − q∥2. Indyk and Motwani [1998] achieve this bound in ˜O(dn 1/(c−1) )

time using a polynomially sized data structure and polynomial preprocessing time. This was subsequently improved to ˜O(dn 1/c ) and later to ˜O(dn 1/c 2+o(1) ) [Datar et al., 2004, Andoni and Indyk, 2008]. Even stronger bounds can be obtained via “data-dependent” LSH variants [Andoni et al., 2014, Andoni and Razenshteyn, 2015]. Such bounds are striking: e.g., when c = 2 , n1/c 2

< 200 even for a data set with n = 1 billion points. Moreover, in addition to strong theoretical guarantees, LSH methods are relatively simple to implement and work well in practice [Lv et al., 2007, Andoni et al., 2015, Aumüller et al., 2020]. Closely related

## 1.1 Graph-Based Nearest Neighbor Search. 

Despite the success of space-partitioning methods like LSH, a new class of graph-based nearest neighbor search algorithms has recently emerged as a competitor. Graph-based methods include Microsoft’s DiskANN [Krishnaswamy et al., 2024], the Hierarchical Navigable Small World (HNSW) method [Malkov and Yashunin, 2020], and many related algorithms [Malkov et al., 2014, Fu and Cai, 2016, Harwood and Drummond, 2016, Fu et al., 2019]. These methods build on ideas dating back to the 1990s [Arya and Mount, 1993, Clarkson, 1994, Navarro, 1999, Kleinberg, 2000, Krauthgamer and Lee, 2004]. The basic idea is to construct a sparse search graph G with a node corresponding to each point in X. Queries are served by running greedy search in 

G: we start at a node s, and traverse the graph by moving to neighbors that reduce the distance to the query. Graph-based methods have frequently topped nearest neighbor search benchmarks, offering improved accuracy vs. runtime tradeoffs [Aumüller et al., 2020, Simhadri et al., 2022, Manohar et al., 2024, Simhadri et al., 2025]. Naturally, these empirical results have spurred interest in theoretically explaining the success of graph-based methods [Laarhoven, 2018, Prokhorenkova and Shekhovtsov, 2020, Peng et al., 2023, Xie et al., 2025, Gollapudi et al., 2025, Al-Jazzazi et al., 2025, Har-Peled et al., 2026]. However, all existing theoretical bounds only hold under strong dataset assumptions (uniformly random, sufficiently dense, etc.), or require an exponential dependence on the data dimension d, or some other measure of intrinsic dimension like doubling dimension [Arya and Mount, 1993, Clarkson, 1994, Indyk and Xu, 2023, Lu and Tao, 2025]. These bounds only imply sublinear query time when d is a small multiple of log n. A major open question is if such an assumption is necessary to obtain performance guarantees for graph-based methods. Concretely: 

Is there any graph-based nearest neighbor search method that, like LSH, can support search queries in o(nd ) time for worst-case, high-dimensional datasets? 

# 2 Our Contributions 

To answer the question above, and specifically to prove any query-time lower bounds, we first need to formalize what a “graph-based search method” is. In this work, we consider a generic “flat search graph” model that captures all common methods that we are aware of (DiskANN, NSG, FANNG, EFANNA, etc.), with the exception of HNSW [Malkov and Yashunin, 2020], which performs search on a hierarchy of graphs. We will discuss separately how to extend our results to HNSW and other hierarchical methods later. Details on our flat search graph model are included in Section 3. To summarize, we consider methods that pre-processes the dataset, X, to produce a directed search graph, G. Upon receiving a query, q ∈ Rd,the method runs generalized greedy search on G. Specifically, it maintains a list of visited nodes, initially populated with a starting node s. At each step, it pops the node off the list that is closest to q, and adds that node’s out-neighbors to the list. The algorithm proceeds until some stopping condition is met, at which point it returns the closest node to q seen so far. Various stopping conditions lead to algorithms that are popular in the literature including the ubiquitous beam search method [Subramanya et al., 2019]. See Section 3 for detailed pseudocode. The query-time of a graph-based method is the time required to execute generalized greedy search and return an approximate nearest neighbor for the query, i.e. to find some ˜x such that, for approximation factor c ≥ 1, ∥˜x − q∥2≤ c · min x∈X ∥x − q∥2.1

## 2.1 Lower Bound for Graph-Based Search 

Our first result explains the lack of progress on worst-case guarantees for graph-based search. We prove that no algorithm implemented in the model above (with any G, any s, and any stopping condition) can solve the approximate search problem under the Euclidean distance in worst-case sublinear time, for any approximation factor. This is in stark contrast to what is achievable with space-partitioning methods like LSH.

Low-Dimensions. Theorem 1 rules out graph-based search methods that achieve worst-case sublinear query time akin to the ˜O(dn 1/c 2+o(1) ) runtime of LSH [Andoni and Indyk, 2008]. However, our hard instance requires

We partially address the low-dimensional setting by proving a query time lower bound of Ω( n1/2−γ ) for any constant γ > 0, even when d = O(log n). This lower bound is in a more restricted setting, applying to methods that use pure greedy search , meaning that search terminates as soon as the node removed from the search list fails to make progress towards q. Moreover, the lower bound holds for methods that succeed for all queries q ∈ X (instead of for any query with high probability). Nevertheless, it gives evidence that graph-based search cannot match LSH, even in low dimensions. Recall that for q ∈ X, the optimal nearest neighbor has distance 0, so LSH can achieve arbitrarily small query time by taking the approximation factor 

c sufficiently large. Our Ω( n1/2−γ ) lower bounds shows this is not possible with pure greedy graph search. 

Theorem 2. There is a set of n points X ⊂ Rd for d = O(log n) on which no graph-based method using pure greedy search can succeed for all queries q ∈ X in o(dn 1/2−γ ) time for any fixed γ > 0.

The hard instance for Theorem 2 is simply a random point set in O(log n) dimensions. We build on a recent average degree lower bound for the related problem of constructing navigable graphs [Diwan et al., 2024]. In short, we show that the starting node in G must have degree Ω( n1/2−γ ) for greedy search to succeed on all queries in X. Therefore, even running the very first search step already takes Ω( dn 1/2−γ ) time. We remark that some graph-based methods achieve worst-case query time of roughly 2O(d) [Arya and Mount, 1993, Clarkson, 1994, Indyk and Xu, 2023, Lu and Tao, 2025]. Theorem 2 shows that, at least for pure greedy search, the exponential dependence on d is inherent: as soon as d = Θ(log n) the runtime must depend polynomially on n. Extending such a result to generalized greedy search is an interesting open question. Alternatively, it would be interesting to obtain runtime bounds, e.g., of the form O(dβ √n), which does not match LSH, but can still be significantly sublinear, even for d = Ω(log n).

## 2.2 Sublinear Runtime Guarantees via Steiner Points 

In spite of the lower bounds in Theorems 1 and 2, we argue that a simple modification of existing graph-based search frameworks does allow them to match the theoretical guarantees of LSH. In particular, graph-based search becomes significantly more powerful when the search graph G is allowed to include Steiner nodes that do not correspond to points in the dataset. We show: 

Theorem 3. There exists a polynomial time algorithm that, given any n-point dataset X ∈ Rd, constructs a search graph G over X and ˜O(n1+1 /(c−1) ) additional Steiner points, such that generalized greedy search on G run for ˜O(n1/(c−1) ) steps starting from an appropriate start node, solves c-approximate Euclidean nearest-neighbor search in ˜O(dn 1/(c−1) ) time, with high probability. 2

To explain it, we first recall the general approach taken in LSH methods. The central object in such methods is a locality sensitive hash function, which has the property that two points are more likely to have the same hash value if they are close to each other. This allows one to restrict the nearest neighbor search problem: we hash the query point q and just search for a near neighbor among the ≪ n points in the dataset that hash to the same value. Repeating this restricted search a sufficient number of times, and returning the best result from the repetitions, ensures that an approximate nearest neighbor to q is returned with high probability. Datar et al. [2004] employs an LSH function based on random projecting points to a one-dimensional line, 

v ∈ Rd. This line is divided into equal-length segments (“buckets”), and the hash value of a point is the index of the bucket that it lands in. Without loss of generality, v can be shifted to lie far away and on one side of all points in X without changing the hash function. Referring to the example in Figure 1, x2 and x12 hash to bucket 1, x1, x 3, and x4 to bucket 2, etc. Our key observation is that this hash function can be implemented using greedy search with Steiner points. Here we give a stylized explanation of how to do so – our actually construction is somewhat more intricate. We place Steiner points s1, s 2, . . . , along v at the center of each bucket. By the Pythagorean theorem, the closest Steiner point to any xj ∈ X is the point at the center of the bucket that xi hashes to. We add an edge to xi from that Steiner point. We then place an additional starting node, s0, far away from X. We add out-edges from s0 to all other Steiner points. In the first step of greedy search, we will navigate from 

s0 to the Steiner point, si, corresponding to the query q’s hash value. Search will then proceed to points in X that also hashed to the same bucket (and are thus connected to si). By locating v far away from the point set, we ensure that for all non-trivial queries q (i.e., for which not all points are c-approximate nearest neighbors), any si is further from q than any xj . This ensures that greedy search properly progresses from si

to the database points, instead of exploring other Steiner nodes. The above construction, which is visualized in Figure 1, is the key ingredient used in implementing an LSH search method within the framework of graph-based search. The full implementation requires further ideas. For example, rather than directly connecting the starting node to a Steiner point for each hash bucket, we need to leverage additional Steiner points to implement a more efficient binary search for the hash bucket. We also chain together several basic search graphs to implement repetition, which is required to find an approximate nearest neighbor with high probability. Details are given in Section 5.

In combination, the lower bounds of Theorems 1 and 2 and the upper bound of Theorem 3 show that, at least in theory, Steiner points can significantly increase the power of graph-based nearest neighbor search. In Section 6, we explore the practical implications of this finding. We augment ParlayANN’s [Manohar et al., 2024] implementation of DiskANN with Steiner points chosen in a very simple way: we cluster the dataset into k = cn clusters for some small constant c < 1 using k-means, and add the cluster centroids as Steiner points. We build a search graph using the standard DiskANN algorithm, but over the dataset augmented with these Steiner points. We find that even this very simple method demonstrates improved performance on several standard data sets. We note that other works have considered the direct or indirect use of Steiner points during search graph construction, often to improve performance on out-of-distribution queries [Jaiswal et al., 2022, Chen et al., 2024, Yue et al., 2026, Baranchuk et al., 2019]. Our findings suggest that further explorations in this direction may be worth consideration. 

# 3 Problem Formalization and Greedy Search 

Before moving on to our main technical sections, we restate the problem studied in this work more formally, and provide pseudocode for the generalized greedy search algorithm that our lower bounds cover. 

Nearest Neighbor Search. In the approximate nearest neighbor (ANN) problem, we are given a dataset 

X = {x1, . . . , x n} ⊂ Rd and a distance function D : Rd × Rd. For a query q ∈ Rd, the goal is to approximately minimize min i∈1,...n D(xi, q ). Typical choices of D include the Euclidean distance D(x, y ) = ∥x − y∥2=(Pdj=1 (x[j] − y[j]) 2)1/2 or inverse cosine similarity D(x, y ) = 1 − ⟨x,y ⟩∥x∥2∥y∥2 . Practical systems typically aim to return the top k closest vectors to q from X for k ≥ 1. For simplicity, this paper focuses on the k = 1 case, in line with most prior theoretical work in the area. 

Greedy Graph-Based Search. We consider methods that solve the nearest neighbor search problem by running generalized greedy search (Algorithm 1) on a directed search graph G = ( {1, . . . , n }, E ). G has one node corresponding to every data point in X. We use the notation NG(i) to denote the set of out-neighbors of a node i in G. NG(i) contains all j for which the ordered pair (i, j ) ∈ E.

The only change is that the output ˜x is the closest non-Steiner node to q that has been popped off the queue. If no node in X is popped before termination, a default output is returned, e.g., ˜x = x1.

Our first result answers this question in the negative: for worst-case inputs, no graph-based search method with finite approximation factor can solve approximate nearest neighbor search in sublinear time when D is the Euclidean distance. As discussed, our hard instance is a set of n vectors in Rn. We prove a weaker lower bound (Theorem 2) for O(log n)-dimensional points in Appendix A.

## 5.2 Implementing Table Repetition 

Lemma 1 implements a single LSH table via greedy search. To simulate the full procedure of Theorem 5, we must perform lookups in L independent tables. Doing so is simple. We let our global starting node s be the starting node for the search graph simulating the first table via Lemma 1. Each terminal Steiner node 

sg(q), as well as the designated failure node sF for the first search graph are connected via an out edge to the starting node for a search graph simulating the second table, and so on. Dataset points have no out edges. As we do within each search tree, we choose the lengths of the Steiner points so that those in later trees are always closer to q than those in earlier trees, assuming ∥q∥2≤ Q. Additionally, all data points are closer to q than all Steiner points. Thus, each newly discovered data point is popped before the next table’s root. After progressing through all search trees, one representative from each nonempty queried bucket has been popped. By Theorem 5, if there is a data point within distance R of q, at least one point within distance cR 

has been popped with probability at least 1 − δ. Overall, we have the following, proven in Appendix B.4: 

Theorem 6 (Fixed-radius Search Upper Bound) . Fix R > 0, c > 1, δ ∈ (0 , 1) , and any Q < ∞. There is a constant ρ depending only on c with ρ < 1 

> c−1

such that, for any n-point dataset X ⊆ Rd, there is a randomized polynomial time algorithm constructing a directed graph G on X and ˜O(n1+ ρ log(1 /δ )) additional Steiner nodes, and a Steiner starting node s, with the following property. For every fixed q with ∥q∥2≤ Q and 

min x∈X ∥x − q∥2≤ R, generalized greedy search initialized at s pops at least one point x ∈ X with ∥x − q∥2≤ cR 

within T = ˜O(nρ log(1 /δ )) steps, with probability at least 1 − δ. All nodes in G have out-degree at most two, so running generalized greedy search for T steps requires O(T ) distance computations and takes ˜O(T d ) time. 

## 5.3 Reduction from ANN to Fixed Radius Search 

We conclude by describing how to use the fixed-radius search result of Theorem 6 to give our final c-approximate nearest neighbor search result of Theorem 3. A full proof is given in Appendix B.5. We first observe that queries with sufficiently large norm, relative to the size of the dataset, are easy – allowing us to remove the bounded norm restriction of Theorem 6. Let R∗ = min x∈X ∥x − q∥2 and let 

diam( X) = max i̸=j ∥xi − xj ∥2. If R∗ ≥ Rmax for Rmax = diam( X)/(c − 1) , then every x ∈ X satisfies

We therefore only need to handle queries with R∗ < R max . All such queries satisfy ∥q∥2≤ Q for Q = max x∈X ∥x∥2+Rmax . We will use Q as the query norm bound in Lemma 1 and Theorem 6. With this observation in place, we use a standard reduction from Indyk and Motwani [1998] between fixed-radius and c-approximate search. We build fixed-radius data structures for a geometrically increasing sequence of radii R0 < R 1 < · · · < R m, where Ri = γiR0 for some constant γ > 1. We return the point found by the smallest-radius data structure that does not return FAIL. The smallest radius with Ri ≥ R∗ satisfies 

Ri < γR ∗. With probability at least 1 − δ, the structure at this radius does not return FAIL. Recalling that we let ∆ = diam( X)/r denote the aspect ratio of the dataset, we have 

Rmax /R 0 = 2( c + 1)∆ /(c − 1) . The successful terminal nodes and the failure node of the last table at radius Ri have out-edges to the root of the first table at radius Ri+1 , and Steiner points at smaller radii are placed so that, for every query with ∥q∥2≤ Q, they are farther from q than all Steiner points at larger radii. A single run of generalized greedy search pops the bucket representatives from every table at every radius. Since the search outputs the closest data point popped, its output is at least as good as the point found at the smallest radius. The graph size and query time grow by a factor of O(log ∆) , the number of radii. One small issue still remains: the reduction to fixed-radius search costs a factor of γ in the approximation guarantee. To absorb this loss, we build each fixed-radius structure with a slightly smaller approximation factor c0 ∈ (1 , c ) and set γ = c/c 0, so that our final approximation factor is γc 0 = c, as desired. However, from Theorem 5 we have that the number of tables required by Indyk and Motwani [1998] is O(nρ log(1 /δ )) where

> c−1

. Each structure therefore still uses L = O(n 1

# 6 Empirical Evaluation 

We conclude with an initial exploration into the empirical implications of our theoretical results. In particular, we study how augmenting existing graph-based search methods via the addition of Steiner points into the search graph can improve query time vs. accuracy for nearest neighbor search. 

Experimental Set Up. We evaluate a very simple approach: we construct Steiner points as k-means cluster centroids of the dataset vectors, with k ∈ { 0.05 n, 0.15 n, 0.25 n}. The centroids are appended to the original dataset and we build a Vamana (DiskANN) search graph [Subramanya et al., 2019] over the augmented point set using ParlayANN’s [Manohar et al., 2024] open source implementation. We use degree bound R = 32 ,pruning parameter α = 1 .2, and build beam width L = 128 . We give additional results with R = 64 in Appendix C. Crucially, the Steiner points are added as nodes in the search graph, but are not returned in the output of greedy search. We compare the performance of these search graphs with that of a baseline graph, where no Steiner points are included. In particular, we run beam search with varying beam widths on the augmented and baseline graphs, in order to produce a runtime vs. accuracy curve for each graph. We measure accuracy as Recall@ k for 

k ∈ { 1, 10 , 100 } against ground truth k-nearest neighbors computed via brute-force. Recall@ k is the fraction of the true k-nearest neighbors appearing in the algorithm’s top-k output, averaged over queries. It is a standard accuracy metric for ANN benchmarks [Aumüller et al., 2020, Simhadri et al., 2025]. For each beam width, we plot recall against the average number of total distance computations per query, which is a common proxy for the query runtime. Full implementation details are given in Appendix C. 

Results. Accuracy vs. runtime curves for the GloVe-200 and NYTimes datasets [Aumüller et al., 2020] using cosine similarity for the distance metric are shown in Figure 2. On GloVe-200, Steiner-augmented graphs outperform the baseline, except at Recall@100. E.g., the search graph with .05 n Steiner nodes achieves the same Recall@1 as the baseline using roughly 25% fewer distance computations. We see similar gains for NYTimes – here the Steiner augmented graphs consistently outperform, even at Recall@100. In Appendix C we give results on further datasets and in further settings. We frequently, although not always, see improvements with Steiner augmented graphs. Nevertheless, our experiments suggest that further study of how Steiner points can be effectively incorporated into graph-based search is warranted. Even the very simple k-means-based approach we consider can sometimes lead to significant performance gains. While we do not necessarily recommend adopting this approach in general, it represents a promising initial baseline for Steiner point construction.

## C.1 Experimental Setup 

Search procedure. We build the Vamana (DiskANN) [Subramanya et al., 2019] graph over the augmented point set using ParlayANN’s [Manohar et al., 2024] parallel C++ implementation. At query time, we run beam search with varying beam widths W on the augmented graph. Steiner nodes participate fully in the beam (they can be expanded and their edges traversed) but they are excluded from the final result set. We ran beam search with varying beam widths using either Euclidean distance or inverse cosine similarity as our distance metric, dependent on the dataset. For Steiner-augmented graphs, the total number of distance computations includes overhead of evaluating distances to Steiner nodes during beam search. By default, ParlayANN randomly permutes the dataset, hence Steiner points are at random positions in the order of insertion. Steiner points were generated using FAISS’s k-means implementation. 

Parameters. We evaluate each configuration across a geometric progression of beam widths W =4, 8, 16 , 32 , . . . , doubling initially and later scaling by a factor of 1.5 until the total number of distance computations reaches a fraction 0.1 of the dataset size. We vary the maximum degree as R ∈ { 32 , 64 }, and set build beam width L = 128 , and pruning parameter α = 1 .2 across all experiments. For querying, we subsample 1000 queries uniformly at random from the test datasets for all experiments. 

Dataset sources and preprocessing. GloVe-200, NYTimes, SIFT, and Fashion-MNIST are sourced from ANN-Benchmarks [Aumüller et al., 2020]. YFCC is sourced from the NeurIPS’21 Big-ANN bench-mark [Simhadri et al., 2025]; we uniformly subsample 1M vectors from the full 10 M-vector dataset. We summarize all datasets in Table 1. 

Licenses. All software and datasets used in this work are available in the public domain. ParlayANN [Manohar et al., 2024], used for graph construction, is released under the MIT License, as is FAISS, which we use for 

k-means clustering. Among the datasets, GloVe is released under the Public Domain Dedication and License (PDDL v1.0); SIFT is released under Creative Commons (CC) BY 4.0; Fashion-MNIST is released under the MIT License; and NYTimes is distributed via ANN-Benchmarks [Aumüller et al., 2020] and is licensed under CC BY 4.0. The YFCC embeddings are sourced from the NeurIPS’21 Big-ANN benchmark [Simhadri et al., 2025], licensed under CC BY 4.0. 

Infrastructure. All large-scale experiments were run on the Unity high-performance computing cluster at the University of Massachusetts Amherst. Each job was allocated a single node with 64 CPU cores and 64 GB of RAM on the cpu partition, running Ubuntu 24.04 with Python 3.11.7. Graph construction was performed using ParlayANN, a parallel C++ implementation of the Vamana algorithm that leverages all available cores. 

Runtime. For the 1M-vector datasets (GloVe, SIFT, YFCC), graph construction and search evaluation across all beam widths for 1,000 queries took 6–7 hours per configuration. For the smaller datasets (NYTimes at 

290 K, Fashion-MNIST at 60 K), each configuration completed in 1–2 hours. Each dataset required 6 SLURM jobs (three Steiner percentages × two degree bounds), bringing the total compute for the full experimental sweep to approximately 300 CPU-hours. 

Ground truth. Ground truth nearest neighbors for computing recall values were computed by brute-force search. Since this was a single-time computation, the total computation time required was insignificant 21 compared to the time required to run the full experimental setup. 

## C.2 Additional plots and results 

Results on GloVe-200 and NYTimes. We show the results for R = 32 in the main paper, Figure 2. The plots for R = 64 are in Figure 3 and Figure 4, where Steiner augmentation still improves over the baseline except for Recall@100 on GloVe, with smaller margins. For NYTimes, similar to R = 32 , Steiner points help for Recall@100 as well. 

Results on SIFT. On SIFT (Figure 7, Figure 8, Figure 9, and Figure 10), the baseline Vamana graph already achieves near-perfect recall (above 0.99 for Recall@1 and Recall@10) at low beam widths. All three Steiner configurations slightly underperform the baseline, with the gap narrowing as the Steiner percentage increases ( 25% > 15% > 5% ). The effect is small in absolute terms (less than 1 percentage point) but consistent across beam widths and degree bounds. Understanding precisely why Steiner augmentation does not yield improvements in this high-recall regime is an interesting direction for further investigation. 

Results on Fashion-MNIST. On Fashion-MNIST in Figure 5 and Figure 6, the baseline already exceeds Recall@1 of 0.95 at the smallest beam width tested. All Steiner configurations closely track the baseline for Recall@1 and Recall@10, with the 25% configuration showing mild degradation in Recall@100. As with SIFT, the baseline recall is sufficiently high that there is limited room for improvement. 

Results on YFCC. YFCC in Figure 11 and Figure 12 exhibits the most pronounced gap between the Steiner-augmented and baseline graphs. The baseline reaches Recall@1 above 0.97 at low beam widths, while all Steiner configurations fall short at matched computation budgets, with differences of 1–3 percentage points. Among the three configurations, higher Steiner percentages perform closer to the baseline ( 25% > 15% > 5% ). The cause of the degradation on YFCC, which is notably more severe than on SIFT or Fashion-MNIST despite similarly high baseline recall, remains an open question.
