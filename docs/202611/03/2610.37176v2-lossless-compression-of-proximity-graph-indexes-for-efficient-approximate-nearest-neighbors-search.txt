## Abstract 

Graph-based Approximate Nearest Neighbor (ANN) indexes, such as HNSW, are the backbone of modern vector search. However, their massive memory footprint creates a severe infrastructure bot-tleneck. While vector quantization is widely adopted to reduce the memory required to store data vectors, the graph’s edge structure increasingly dominates total memory usage. Compressing these adjacency lists for online search presents a critical system chal-lenge: because decompression occurs during query processing, i.e., while navigating the graph, memory savings cannot come at the expense of query latency. In this paper, we present a compression technique explicitly tailored for live HNSW routing. By applying offline graph permutations to artificially induce structural locality, we enable the use of SIMD-accelerated integer compressors, such as Stream VByte, for rapid on-the-fly decompression. Evaluated across diverse real-world workloads, we significantly outperform the current state of the art in both compression ratio and query la-tency. Our experimental results show that we achieve compression ratios up to 52 .7% with negligible penalties in latency compared to the uncompressed baseline. Furthermore, our technique has been effectively deployed in production at eBay, achieving up to 44 .3% reduction in edge storage with no degradation in query latency.

## 1 Introduction 

Modern retrieval systems represent data such as text, images, and products as high-dimensional vector embeddings generated by neural models, including Large Language Models. Retrieval then consists of finding the vectors in a large collection that are most similar to a given query under metrics such as Euclidean distance, dot product, or cosine similarity [33, 53]. Since exact nearest neigh-bor search requires comparing the query against all indexed vectors, its computational cost becomes prohibitive at large scales. Approx-imate Nearest Neighbor (ANN) methods trade exactness for faster retrieval, and are now a fundamental component of large-scale re-trieval systems [4, 53]. Among the many ANN indexing strategies proposed in the literature, proximity graph methods—most notably Hierarchical Navigable Small World (HNSW) [41]—have emerged as one of the dominant approaches due to their strong trade-off be-tween retrieval accuracy and query latency [4, 53]. These methods construct a graph over the vectors by connecting nearby points, enabling efficient graph traversal during query processing. How-ever, they introduce two main sources of memory overhead: storing the vectors and the graph structure. These indexes are typically de-signed to reside entirely in main memory to guarantee low-latency query processing, making memory consumption a primary bottle-neck in large-scale deployments [51]. While vector quantization techniques [1, 25, 34] are widely adopted for vector compression, the graph structure itself can account for a large fraction of the overall index size, especially when compact vector representations are used [51]. As a result, graph compression is particularly relevant for modern ANN systems. At the same time, compressing graph arXiv:2610.37176v2 [cs.IR] 2 Nov 2026 CIKM ’26, November 07–11, 2026, Rome, Italy Francesco Benocci et al. 

connectivity is particularly challenging in the online in-memory setting, where adjacency lists must be decoded during query exe-cution, and any additional decompression overhead directly affects retrieval latency. In this line, recent work has begun to explore lossless compression of ANN indexes across a wide spectrum of settings, including online and offline scenarios, inverted file (IVF) indexes [33], graph indexes, and quantized vector codes [51]. How-ever, the current literature still lacks an in-depth exploration of graph compression, which we address here. In this work, we provide a thorough exploration of the structural properties of proximity graphs and graph compression techniques for online in-memory ANN search. We focus on HNSW [41], one of the most widely adopted graph-based ANN indexes [4, 5]. It is available in research libraries such as FAISS [18], hnswlib [40], and kANNolo [15] (that we employ here), and deployed in produc-tion by vector database companies including Elasticsearch, Qdrant, Weaviate and Pinecone. Our contribution revolves around two key components. First, we employ offline node permutation techniques [29, 39] to improve the compressibility of adjacency lists (Section 2.3). Second, we apply Stream VByte [36] compression, which at query time achieves neg-ligible decompression overhead thanks to its byte-aligned layout and SIMD decompression (Section 2.2). We evaluate our proposed technique on a broad set of workloads, distinguishing our work from prior studies that present evidence on small, dense vector datasets (Sift1M [38], Deep1M [6], and FB-ssnpp [47]). Our bench-marks comprise both classical and neural datasets, over dense and sparse vectors, using Euclidean distance and inner-product sim-ilarity, and focus on both compressed and uncompressed vector representations, across datasets ranging from 1M to ≈9M vectors. The proposed system is currently deployed in production for large-scale ANN retrieval at eBay, whose in-house search engine, Cassini [52], uses HNSW for vector retrieval. We integrated our graph compression framework into Cassini’s production pipeline to relieve its memory constraint. Since its deployment in early 2025 we measure a 44 .3% smaller HNSW graph at identical ANN recall, with no measurable latency degradation under live workloads, which lets eBay extend HNSW to more search use cases. Our main contributions are as follows: 

• We provide a deep empirical exploration of the HNSW graph structure over three public datasets and a private products dataset from eBay (eBayProducts7M) to ground the choice of the ad-jacency list compression algorithms. Our analysis finds that reference-based compressors such as WebGraph [9] are not suit-able for compressing ANN graphs, and that node IDs do not reflect the graph’s structural topology, highlighting the need to adopt graph permutation techniques [29, 39] before compressing adjacency lists. 

• We introduce a compression framework that effectively reduces edge storage while incurring negligible to zero slowdown. We then experimentally show the effectiveness and efficiency of our proposed technique across a wide range of datasets. Our approach reduces edge space by up to 52% (Sift1M) while maintaining query latency within a few percent of the uncompressed baseline and achieving up to 11% lower latency on eBayProducts7M at 98% Accuracy@10. 

• We will release a public implementation of our compression framework within the kANNolo Rust library [15]. 

## 2 Background and Related Work 2.1 ANN Search 

Given a dataset of 𝑁 vectors in a 𝐷 -dimensional vector space, the 

𝑘 -Nearest Neighbors (kNN) problem consists of retrieving the 𝑘 

closest points to a query vector under a given similarity measure [14]. Exact solutions quickly become computationally prohibitive as both 𝑁 and 𝐷 grow. In particular, classical data structures such as kd-trees [7] suffer from the curse of dimensionality [31], leading to a degradation toward linear scan in high-dimensional settings. To overcome these limitations, Approximate Nearest Neighbor (ANN) methods trade small accuracy losses for huge efficiency gains [22], significantly reducing query time and memory access costs [4]. 

HNSW. The most effective class of ANN indexes is represented by graph methods [4, 5, 53], which organize the dataset as a proximity graph by connecting nearby points to facilitate space exploration. The search for a query vector follows a greedy strategy [5]. Among graph-based methods, the Hierarchical Navigable Small World (HNSW) graph [41] has emerged as one of the most effec-tive and widely-adopted ANN indexes due to its robustness and the strong trade-off between accuracy and efficiency that it offers. HNSW organizes vectors into a multi-level proximity graph where higher, sparser levels provide long-range connections to facilitate navigation, while lower levels improve local search accuracy. The lowest level, referred to as the ground level , contains all the dataset points. During the search for a query, higher layers are used to quickly locate the query neighborhood, and the ground level is then used to refine the results. Three standard parameters regulate the effectiveness and ef-ficiency of HNSW: 𝑀 controls the maximum number of graph connections per node, efConstruction regulates the size of the can-didate pool during index construction, and efSearch controls the exploration breadth during query processing. In our implementa-tion, query processing is further controlled by a distance-adaptive early-termination threshold 𝜆 [2]. 

Vector Quantization. ANN indexes require storing the dataset vectors and typically operate in-memory to enable low-latency re-sponses. Therefore, scaling these methods to large, high-dimensional datasets poses a challenge. To overcome this limitation, much of the literature has focused on quantization methods [1, 25, 27, 34] that reduce the space required for vector data by mapping continuous values to discrete ones. Product Quantization (PQ) [34] is one of the most widely adopted quantization techniques [18] in ANN search. It divides vectors into subspaces, quantizing each independently via 𝜅 -means, achieving compression ratios above 90% at modest accuracy cost. 

## 2.2 Graph Compression 

In ANN graph indexes, adjacency lists can be arbitrarily ordered. However, by enforcing a sorted order and treating them as monoton-ically increasing sequences of integers, we can leverage powerful encoding algorithms to significantly reduce memory footprints. Lossless Compression of Proximity Graph Indexes for Efficient Approximate Nearest Neighbors Search CIKM ’26, November 07–11, 2026, Rome, Italy 

Integer Compression. Elias-Fano [20] is a quasi-succinct encod-ing that splits each integer into high and low-order bits: the lower bits are stored explicitly, while the upper ones are stored in a com-pact unary representation. It compresses sorted adjacency lists and supports direct access through a Select operation, which is neces-sary for decompressing adjacency lists during query routing, since neighborhood lookups are unpredictable and are served on the fly. Alternatively, a sorted list of integers 𝑆 = [𝑥 1, . . . , 𝑥 𝑛 ] can be transformed via gap encoding (or delta encoding), yielding e𝑆 =

[𝑥 1, 𝑥 2 − 𝑥 1, . . . , 𝑥 𝑛 − 𝑥 𝑛 −1], i.e. by storing the differences between consecutive elements [46, 56]. Ideally, gap encoding shifts the distri-bution of integers toward much smaller values, reducing the overall entropy of the sequence. Entropy coders such as Huffman [30], Arithmetic coding [43, 48, 49] and Asymmetric Numeral Systems (ANS) [19, 51] are near– optimal in space, but due to bit-by-bit decompression introduce unacceptable latency that real-time systems cannot afford. Tradi-tional universal codes (e.g., Elias Gamma or Delta [21], Fibonacci [3], Zeta [10]) assign fixed, short bit sequences to small integers, yet are not byte-aligned, which slows decoding. Consequently, mod-ern latency-sensitive compression pipelines typically adopt byte-aligned or word-aligned integer compressors, which sacrifice a small amount of compression ratio in exchange for faster decoding. Variable Byte (VByte) [54] encodes each integer in a variable number of bytes, with continuation bits marking whether more bytes follow. It benefits from gap encoding, since the resulting small integers often fit in a single byte. However, its decoding process involves data-dependent branches and byte-by-byte control flow, limiting instruction-level parallelism and efficient SIMD utilization. 

Stream VByte (SVB) [36] redesigns the layout of VByte to better match modern hardware. Instead of interleaving control informa-tion with the data, it separates the compressed sequence into two streams: a control stream and a data stream . Each control byte en-codes the lengths of multiple consecutive integers; for instance, encoding 32-bit values, a single control byte describes four inte-gers using 2 bits per value, where each 2-bit pattern specifies the number of bytes required to represent the corresponding integer (from 1 to 4 bytes). The data stream then stores the concatenated variable-length representations. The decoder therefore reads con-trol bytes independently and access the data stream branch-free. With block-level indexing the layout also supports partial decoding and efficient skipping, which fits graph-based ANN search, where only a subset of overall neighbors array is accessed per query. 

List-level Compression. Another dimension along which redun-dancy can be exploited is at list level, by representing significant por-tions of one list as a reference to another. Boldi and Vigna observed that this type of redundancy is pervasive in web graphs, where many websites share a large portion of outgoing links [9]. They proposed WebGraph, an algorithm that leverages both intra-list and inter-list redundancies to achieve state-of-the-art compression ratios. To mitigate decompression costs, they bound the reference chains by restricting copies to a fixed window of preceding lists. While list-level reference compression has become the de facto 

standard for archiving web and social graphs, its applicability to proximity graphs remains largely unexplored. The distinct topo-logical properties of such graphs—combined with the strict latency requirements of their routing algorithms—raise open questions re-garding whether exploitable list-level redundancies exist in this domain, and whether the decompression overhead of reference chains can be tolerated during online search. 

Previous Work. A recent work by Severo et al . [51] from Meta investigates lossless compression techniques tailored to ANN data structures, representing the first systematic study of vector identi-fier compression for ANN indexes. They show that a substantial fraction of the memory footprint of ANN structures is due to identi-fiers and graph links rather than vectors, and that these components can be significantly compressed without affecting retrieval accuracy, while highlighting the tension between compression effectiveness and online decoding costs, especially in graph traversal workloads where adjacency lists must be accessed frequently during search. Their approach rest on the observation that, in both IVF [33] and graph-based indexes, IDs ordering within lists is irrelevant to search correctness. This invariance enables additional compression opportunities by treating adjacency lists and posting lists either as sorted sequences or directly as sets, allowing the use of suc-cinct representations and entropy coding techniques. For the online graph scenario bit-packing (i.e., storing each identifier of 𝑁 total ele-ments using ⌈log 2 𝑁 ⌉ bits) and Elias–Fano [20] provide the best com-pression and fast random access. On IVF indexes, more advanced entropy-oriented approaches, including Asymmetric Numeral Sys-tems (ANS) [19, 51], further exploit the statistical distribution of identifiers and gaps, achieving stronger compression ratios. 

## 2.3 Graph Permutations 

Default ordering of vertices in real-world graphs, dictated by in-gestion order, rarely aligns with topology, masking redundancies and leading to suboptimal compression. By finding an opportune bijection of the vertex IDs—a process known as graph permutation 

or reordering—we can artificially induce structural locality and significantly improve the effectiveness of encoding algorithms. Formally, optimizing for gap encoding can be formulated as the NP-hard minimum linear arrangement (MLA) problem, which seeks a node permutation 𝜙 : 𝑉 → 𝑉 that minimizes the sum of edge lengths, Í(𝑢,𝑣 ) ∈ 𝐸 |𝜙 (𝑢 ) − 𝜙 (𝑣 )| [26]. Because exact solu-tions are intractable, modern compression pipelines rely on graph partitioning heuristics. These algorithms cluster the graph into densely-connected regions and assign consecutive IDs to the nodes within each block [8, 17, 37]. In the broader graph partitioning literature, two primary paradigms exist, which we leverage to enable effective gap compression: 

• Recursive Bipartitioning (RB): Algorithms in this family re-peatedly divide the graph into two balanced partitions until the desired number of blocks is reached. The most prominent algo-rithms in this class include Metis, PaToH, and Scotch [12, 35, 45]. Enhanced Graph Bisection (EGB) [39] is a specialized variant of RB algorithms that employs a gain-based refinement procedure explicitly designed to optimize the distribution of adjacency-list entries for compression. 

• Direct 𝑘 -way Partitioning: Alternatively, algorithms in this family partition the graph into 𝑘 blocks directly. Recent advances have established that direct 𝑘 -way methods generally outper-form recursive approaches in both partitioning quality [50] and CIKM ’26, November 07–11, 2026, Rome, Italy Francesco Benocci et al. 

execution speed [28]. KaMinPar [29], which employs a highly scalable multilevel scheme, is specifically designed to partition graphs into a large number of blocks (e.g., 𝑘 > 1024), a task where traditional partitioning algorithms often struggle to find feasible solutions or suffer severe performance degradation. This capabil-ity is critical for edge compression, as it enables the fine-grained ID grouping required to minimize adjacency list gaps. By leveraging these partitioning techniques, highly scattered ad-jacency lists can be transformed into localized sequences with small gaps, unlocking the theoretical bounds of integer compression. 

## 3 Methodology 

We structure our methodology into two parts. First, we provide a preliminary analysis of the proximity graph’s structural proper-ties. Second, we describe our compression technique, which builds directly on those findings. 

## 3.1 Preliminary Analysis 

Consistent with our focus on practical deployments, we restrict our analysis to low-degree HNSW configurations. In our benchmarks, low-degree graphs already achieve high recall; increasing 𝑀 beyond this regime buys little accuracy while costing memory and, through longer adjacency lists, query latency. Furthermore, HNSW pruning rules keep the edge count well below the cap. Prior work evaluates degrees from 16 to 256 [51]. Low-degree graphs are therefore a more challenging and practically relevant target: adjacency lists are shorter, leaving less redundancy to exploit, and the per-list decompression cost is more pronounced relative to the work done per query. 

Impact of Edges on Index Size. Proximity graph indexes have two main sources of memory overhead that both scale linearly with dataset size: the stored vectors and the graph edges. While vector compression is well-studied and widely deployed, the contribution of edge storage to the overall index size is often underestimated. Figure 1 shows that graph edges constitute a substantial fraction of the total index size, making their compression critical to reducing memory footprint. The analysis above fixes our pipeline. First, we apply the EGB algorithm to permute the node IDs, storing the compacted permuta-tion mapping. Second, we gap-encode the adjacency lists, compress them using Stream VByte and compress the index’s offset array using Elias–Fano to guarantee maximum overall memory efficiency. 

## 3.2 Implementing our Compression Framework 

We implement our compression framework, alongside all evaluated baselines, within the HNSW index of the kANNolo [15] library. kANNolo represents a highly optimized, uncompressed baseline, thus ensuring that any measured compression overhead is genuine and not masked by inefficiencies in the underlying implementation. ## 4 Experiments 

We conduct our experiments on four datasets spanning a diverse range of settings: image descriptors, text passages encoded with both dense and sparse models, and product embeddings (Table 1). 

• Sift1M [38] is arguably the most adopted dataset for ANN bench-marking [4], consisting of 1M 128-dimensional image descriptors produced using the Scale-Invariant Feature Transform (SIFT) [38] algorithm. It is evaluated with Euclidean distance on 10K queries. 

• Ms Marco-v1 [44] is a collection of ≈ 8.8M text passages widely adopted in Information Retrieval benchmarks, with a query set of 6,980 queries ( dev.small ). We produce two different embedding sets and evaluate both under inner product similarity. 

– SnowFlake [55] is a lightweight state-of-the-art dense en-coder producing 1024-dimensional vectors. As its encoding of MsMarco exceeds 30 GB, we compress it via Product Quanti-zation (PQ) with 𝑀 = 128 subspaces, achieving a 32 × reduction in storage. Since heavy compression degrades accuracy, we adopt a two-stage search: the top-𝑘 ′ (𝑘 ′ > 𝑘 ) candidates are retrieved using PQ-encoded vectors, then reranked with the uncompressed representations to select the final top-𝑘 . This mimics the practical scenario in which the reranking step ac-cesses uncompressed data from disk, while the compressed vectors and the graph structure are kept in main memory. 

– CoCondenser [23] is a sparse encoder built on the BERT [16] vocabulary, resulting in output vectors that live in an ≈ 30K-dimensional space. The vectors have 120 non-zero components on average, while queries have 42. We employ the dotvbyte 

algorithm [11] to store the components and 8 bits floating point values to store the values. 

• eBayProducts7M: this is a set of roughly 7M 96-dimensional product embeddings from eBay. The embedding model was fine-tuned for product search from an in-house variant of BERT spe-cialized for the e-commerce domain. The query set consists of 10K query embeddings.

Plain represents the uncompressed baseline. Bitpacker provides a lightweight compromise, reducing memory usage by 1 .3×–1 .6×

while keeping latency close to the uncompressed baseline across all datasets. Elias–Fano is dominated on both axes: it is consistently the slowest method while compressing no better than Bitpacker. Zeta achieves the smallest memory footprint across all datasets, with reductions from 1 .7× (CoCondenser) to 2 .3× (Sift1M) compared to Plain. However, we pay this compactness with slow downs up to ≈ 27% on Sift1M and ≈ 14% on eBayProducts7M. Stream VByte yields the best operating point across all four benchmarks, combining strong compression with efficient decoding. Its mem-ory footprint remains close to that of Zeta, within roughly 4–10%, while its query latency matches or even improves over Plain on CoCondenser, eBayProducts7M, and SnowFlake, with only a marginal overhead on Sift1M. Stream VByte is therefore the only method that simultaneously approaches the compression ratio of Zeta and the query performance of the uncompressed baseline. Considering the graph permutation as a fraction of the total build time (graph construction plus permutation), reordering ac-counts for just 3.5% on CoCondenser, 7.8% on SnowFlake, 15% on eBayProducts7M, and 30% on Sift1M. Permutation time de-pends on the number of edges and their distribution, while graph construction time depends on efConstruction and on vector dimen-sionality. Sift1M is where this fraction peaks: low dimensionality and no need for a high efConstruction . Because index construction is a one-time, offline procedure, permutation’s overhead represents a highly favorable trade-off for the memory reductions and latency improvements demonstrated by our system. 

## 5 Conclusions and Future Work 

In this work, we study the problem of compressing the adjacency lists of proximity graphs for ANN search. An offline node permu-tation induces structural locality in adjacency lists, substantially lowering their entropy under gap encoding. We then compress the reordered, delta-encoded neighbor sequences with Stream VByte, organized into adjacency-list-aligned blocks for fast SIMD decod-ing at query time. We evaluate our framework on four diverse benchmarks spanning images, dense and sparse text embeddings, and product embeddings. Across all datasets, Stream VByte pro-vides the best operating point, with a compression ratio up to 2 .1×

with zero to negligible penalties in latency compared to the un-compressed baseline. Beyond public benchmarks, the framework has been deployed in production at eBay, reducing HNSW graph memory by 44 .3% with no measurable degradation in query latency. As per future work, we plan to extend our framework beyond HNSW to other graph-based ANN indexes such as Vamana [32] or NSG [24], assessing how the compression-latency trade-off shifts across different graph structures. A second direction is to co-design graph and vector compression in a unified, compression-aware index, jointly optimizing edge encoding and vector quantization to unlock further memory savings, particularly in regimes where both components contribute comparably to the overall index size.
