ABSTRACT 

Large language model (LLM) agents increasingly use skills to access domain knowledge and complete real-world tasks. We observe that even when a skill specifies the rules and procedures needed to complete a task, agents may still fail to apply them effectively during autonomous execution, a phenomenon we call skill Enable But Underuse (EBU). This issue is particularly consequential in medical tasks, where overlooking even a single critical constraint can lead to a plausible but incorrect result. To systematically evaluate and address this phe-nomenon, we introduce MedSkill-E 2, a framework for evaluating and evolving skills for medical agents. We construct EBU-Bench, a challenging benchmark covering 12 medically relevant task groups and their associated skills, to evaluate agents’ ability to use available skills autonomously. We quantify the EBU gap as the difference in skill-use accuracy between autonomous use and explicitly guided reading and application of the same skill. On EBU-Bench, all evaluated model– harness combinations exhibit EBU, with a mean gap of 43.94 percentage points (pp). For the same model, skill-use accuracy under autonomous use differs by up to 38.96 pp across harnesses. Motivated by these observations, we propose EBU-Evolve, which uses execution feedback from autonomous and explicitly guided skill use to distill effective guidance into reusable skill instructions. Relative to their pre-evolution versions, the revised skills improve skill-use accuracy during autonomous execution by 33.33–55.17 pp and continue to yield improvements when transferred across multiple models and harnesses. Our results show that skill-use accuracy under autonomous execution varies substantially across har-nesses even when the model and skill are held fixed, and model rankings also change; EBU can persist even after a skill is opened. EBU-Evolve distills effec-tive guidance into reusable skill instructions, improving skill-use accuracy under autonomous execution on unseen tasks within the same skill, and still yields im-provements when the evolved skills are transferred to other models and harnesses. 

1 INTRODUCTION 

Large language model (LLM) agents use skills to access domain knowledge and apply it to specific tasks. A skill organizes the methods, tool-use instructions, and operating procedures needed for a class of tasks into a reusable resource, so that agents need not work out a solution procedure anew for each problem. This support is particularly important in medicine and related research: clinical calculations require accurate application of formulas and their constraints, while evidence review requires adherence to explicit assessment criteria. Skills offer a direct way to standardize these operations. Skills have also begun to emerge for healthcare and the life sciences (Xu et al., 2026). These observations raise a question: in real medical applications, how much assistance can an available skill provide, and how much of that benefit can an agent realize when solving tasks autonomously? We further find that some previ-ously unsuccessful tasks can be completed when the agent is directed to use a particular skill and given corresponding execution requirements. We call this additional guidance “operational support” and use task performance under this condition as an empirical reference for the benefits achievable with the skill. We term the phenomenon in which the same skill supports task completion under operational support but its corresponding benefits remain unrealized during autonomous execution skill Enable But Underuse (EBU). EBU may not be widespread across medical tasks, but when it occurs, agents produce plausible results that do not comply with the specified procedures, warrant-ing dedicated study. We therefore identify tasks exhibiting EBU during benchmark construction 2and investigate two questions: how underuse on these tasks varies across models and harnesses, and whether revising reusable skill instructions can improve autonomous execution. To study this problem systematically, we introduce MedSkill-E 2, a framework for evaluating and evolving skills for medical agents that comprises EBU-Bench and EBU-Evolve. EBU-Bench con-tains 331 tasks and their associated skills, combining tasks from public benchmarks with tasks au-thored around research questions. Evaluation is organized around reusable skills, each intended for a class of problems. We select task–skill pairs from this collection and evaluate nine ad-vanced language models under two harnesses. Mean skill-use accuracy is 38.38% when models use skills autonomously and 82.32% with operational support; on EBU-Bench, all evaluated model– harness combinations exhibit EBU. Even with the model and skill held fixed, performance under autonomous use varies substantially across harnesses. Operational support externally identifies a skill to use and provides general execution requirements for the task, but such guidance is usually unavailable in advance in practical settings. Notably, these supplementary instructions do not disclose any domain knowledge or operational steps specific to the task. We therefore propose EBU-Evolve to distill effective guidance from operational support into reusable skill instructions. The method revises a skill using paired execution feedback from the same task under autonomous use and operational support, and selects a revised version based on task performance under autonomous use. The revised skill can then be used directly for autonomous task solving without additional operational support for individual tasks. Experiments show that the revised skills improve overall skill-use accuracy under autonomous use on test tasks excluded from revision and selection, and continue to yield improvements when transferred across models or harnesses. 

2 EBU-B ENCH 

2.1 BENCHMARK CONSTRUCTION 

EBU-Bench is a targeted challenge benchmark for skill underuse: it evaluates whether agents put available methods into practice. It combines tasks from public benchmarks with tasks authored around research questions; the public-benchmark task–skill pairs pass through three screening stages. 

Stage 1: task collection and capability screening. We examine 11 public benchmarks and eval-uation resources covering clinical calculation, medical image understanding, literature processing, biological data analysis, and clinical system operations (Khandekar et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2026c; Fallahpour et al., 2025; Mitchener et al., 2025; Jiang et al., 2025; Zhu et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026a; Nair et al., 2026; Fa et al., 2026; Workman et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026b). Tasks with the necessary data and tools are solved without a skill, and those not fully solved form the candidate pool. 

Stage 2: skill pairing and effectiveness validation. For each task we retrieve and screen candidate skills from public skill libraries and methodological resources, organizing public methodological material into reusable procedural documents where necessary. Each candidate is then provided on its own with a prompt to perform its task-relevant operations, and pairs that can be completed under Operationally Supported Skill Use are retained. 

Stage 3: screening for an autonomous-use gap. With the skill description provided alongside the task, the agent decides on its own whether and how to use the skill. For the same task and skill version, we retain tasks that are not completed under autonomous use but are completed under Operationally Supported Skill Use. After the three screening stages, the retained public-benchmark tasks come from MedCalc-Bench and BixBench. To broaden coverage, we additionally author and expand tasks for literature screen-ing, variant functional evidence, and enrichment review from common research questions, relevant domain skills, and public research materials; these require applying the skill’s method under the specific conditions of the problem, such as screening studies against inclusion criteria, judging the applicability of functional evidence, or choosing the background for enrichment analysis. Varying problem conditions and resources yields 218 task pairings. Task inputs and objectives. Each task includes a problem description, the required data, the as-sociated skill, and the tool environment needed for completion; the agent performs the requested calculations, analyses, or judgments and submits a final answer. Under Natural Skill Use, the asso-ciated skill is provided through the harness’s standard mechanism, and the agent decides on its own whether to open it and how to use it (Figure 1d). 

Task scoring. Each task has a fixed scoring program: numerical answers are checked against pre-defined tolerances or intervals, categorical and structured answers against their rules. An answer scores 1 if it passes and 0 otherwise, and the mean score over the evaluated tasks is the skill-use accuracy. Scoring depends only on the final result; the agent chooses its own procedure. We also record from the execution trace whether the agent opens the associated skill. 

The EBU gap. Operationally Supported Skill Use uses the same task and skill as Natural Skill Use but explicitly requires the agent to read the skill and perform its task-relevant operations, i.e., the operational support introduced above. With Pnatural (m, h ) and Psupported (m, h ) the skill-use accuracies of model m under harness h in the two conditions, we define ∆EBU (m, h ) =

Psupported (m, h ) − Pnatural (m, h ). Using Operationally Supported Skill Use as an empirical ref-erence, the EBU gap measures the task gains not yet realized under Natural Skill Use for a given model and harness. We report both accuracies and retain any negative differences; across harnesses, the gaps of the same model are averaged with equal weight. Skill-opening records and cross-harness differences support further analysis but do not enter the gap. 

Organizing tasks around skills. Each associated skill corresponds to multiple tasks that apply the same method to different inputs and problem conditions (Table 1), so that a method is evaluated across multiple instances; for skills with enough tasks, the tasks can be split into training, validation, and test sets for EBU-Evolve (§3). 

EBU-Bench Lite. Because running the full benchmark across models, harnesses, and conditions is costly, we use a 154-task subset that keeps all 12 task groups: all 113 public-benchmark tasks plus one representative task per source-question–skill combination among the 218 authored tasks (41 tasks), which reduces the repeated weight of variants of the same source question. Representatives are selected independently of the evaluation results reported here (rules in Appendix A.4). Unless otherwise stated, the benchmark experiments use EBU-Bench Lite. 43 EBU-E VOLVE 

Figures 1a and 1b show that general encouragement to use skills (Prompted Skill Use) remains in-sufficient to fully realize the benefits of an available skill. We propose EBU-Evolve, which derives concrete, generalizable revisions from the differences between executions of the same task under Natural Skill Use and Operationally Supported Skill Use, and distills them into persistent skill in-structions. This addresses EBU on the individual task and, at the same time, enables agents to use the revised skill effectively on similar tasks that exhibit the same form of underuse, thereby realizing its value under Natural Skill Use. Figure 1c presents the overall EBU-Evolve workflow. When evolving a skill, we divide its tasks into three sets: the training set provides evidence for revision, the validation set determines the skill version, and the test set evaluates the selected version on tasks that took part in neither this revision nor the selection. Specifically, we use paired executions to determine what to revise, jointly revise the description and the body, and select the version with the highest mean task score under Natural Skill Use. Evolution leaves the model parameters and the skill’s accompanying reference files, code, and data unchanged; only the skill’s instruction text is updated. 

Distilling skill instructions from paired executions. A single failure is insufficient to determine the direction of revision, whereas a successful execution of the same task under Operationally Sup-ported Skill Use provides a reference. We therefore collect, for the same training task, the exe-cution traces and scores under both conditions, using the same skill version, model, and harness. Building on trace-based reflective optimization (Agrawal et al., 2026), we pass these paired execu-tions, together with the original skill and its reference materials, to a reflection model, which judges whether the agent accessed the relevant rules during autonomous execution and whether it applied them under the current task conditions, and proposes revision rules accordingly. Only revision rules supported by the original skill are used to generate candidates (Appendix B.1). We call this pro-cess intervention-to-instruction distillation : the improvements brought by operational support are written persistently into the skill. 

Jointly revising the description and body. Distilled instructions must be accessed and correctly applied to improve autonomous performance. With progressive loading, the always-visible descrip-tion indicates applicability; the body and references are read after opening. Clarifying rules therefore also requires guiding access to them. One edit produces a coordinated description and body, com-bined with the original into body-only, joint, and description-only candidates. Each is reviewed separately and compared with the original on the same Natural Skill Use validation tasks. Only a strict improvement replaces the original (Appendices B.2–B.4). 

4 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

4.1 MODELS AND EXECUTION ENVIRONMENTS 

We evaluate nine models: GPT-5.5, Claude Opus 4.8, Gemini 3.8 Flash, DeepSeek V4 Pro, GLM-5.2, Atria-Dawn-Preview (Atria), Kimi K2.7 Code, Qwen3.8 Max, and Qwen3.8 27B FP8, each under two harnesses, Claude Code and Codex, which differ in resource discovery and tool interaction (request identifiers and harness versions in Appendix C). Benchmark evaluations are executed through BenchFlow: each run uses an independent container and session with the data, computational tools, and file-operation interfaces required by the task, and external retrieval and data downloads are disabled to prevent answer leakage. All nine models are evaluated on the same 154 tasks described in §2.2; results of three models on the full benchmark of 331 tasks are reported in Appendix D.2. 4.2 SKILL PROVISION AND EVALUATION CONDITIONS 

We ask not only whether a skill is provided but how much additional prompting an agent needs before using it, and define four conditions per task (Figure 1a). The EBU gap is the difference between Natural Skill Use and Operationally Supported Skill Use (§2.2); No Skill and Prompted Skill Use measure performance without the associated skill and the change induced by a general reminder. 5Under the conditions that provide a skill, the skill’s name and description enter the context through the harness’s native mechanism, and the body and reference files are read on demand. Natural Skill Use adds no task-level prompting, while retaining the harness’s built-in resource-use instructions. Prompted Skill Use adds only general encouragement to use the skill, without specifying solution steps, and tests whether merely reminding the agent suffices to realize the skill’s benefits. Oper-ationally Supported Skill Use further specifies the reading and execution requirements, providing a reference for the gains that remain unrealized. The three conditions with a skill use the same resource version and differ only in the support appended to the task prompt. Operationally Supported Skill Use reuses a prompt fixed for each task group: for tasks derived from public benchmarks it requires reading the associated skill in full and applying its steps to the current inputs, and for authored tasks it requires reading the entry file and the reference materials it points to. Neither supplies the answer to the current task; because the two differ in specificity, we also report results by task source (Appendix C.4). 4.3 EXECUTION PROTOCOL AND RESULT AGGREGATION 

For each model, harness, and condition, we retain one run record for each of the 154 tasks, yielding 11,088 retained run records; all skill-use accuracies use 154 as the denominator. The initial time limit is either 900 or 3,600 seconds, depending on the batch. Runs that time out under the 900-second limit are rerun with a time limit of up to 7,200 seconds, and the rerun replaces the original record. Runs that time out or fail to submit a valid answer, including answers in a format the scorer cannot parse, receive a score of 0. Appendix C provides the detailed configuration, rerun scope, and distribution of invalid runs. 4.4 EVALUATION SETUP FOR EBU-E VOLVE 

EBU-Evolve requires multiple tasks under the same skill to provide revision feedback and test the revisions. Because the task–skill pairs retained from public benchmarks do not support this split, we expand research questions from the literature into four skill groups: Abstract screening, Clinical evidence screening, Variant functional evidence, and Enrichment review. Training, validation, and test sets follow §3, with disjoint task IDs (splits and execution settings in Appendix B.7). We evolve skills separately from the execution feedback of GLM-5.2, GPT-5.5, and Atria under Claude Code, with Claude Opus 4.8 performing reflection, editing, and review; each validation task is repeated three times and the version is selected by mean task score. At test time, the selected version is compared with the original skill under Natural Skill Use without operational support. To test reuse across configurations, each source’s frozen version is also given to the other two source models and to DeepSeek V4 Pro, and tested with the same source model under Codex; all gains are relative to the original skill in the target configuration, and overall accuracies are aggregated over tasks. The method comparison evaluates, under a common GLM-5.2/Claude Code configuration, the arti-facts of the prompt-optimization method GEPA, the skill-optimization method SkillOpt, and EBU-Evolve; the first two are run with standard feedback (Std) and with paired evidence from the same task (Paired), under the same budget and editing permissions. Further experiments examine editing scope, description and body resources, component contribu-tions, and optimization stability. Both generic trace-reflection rewriting (hereafter, generic rewriting) and EBU-Evolve revise reusable skill instructions. Generic rewriting uses feedback from Natural Skill Use traces. EBU-Evolve pairs these traces with Operationally Supported Skill Use traces from the same training tasks, prioritizes cases in which support improves the task score, and diagnoses which skill rules the agent missed or misapplied. Autonomous skill access depends on the model–harness combination. Cross-harness differences are concentrated in Natural Skill Use: the mean accuracies of Claude Code and Codex are 26.48% and 50.29%, a difference of 23.81 pp, whereas under No Skill and Operationally Supported Skill Use they differ by only 0.94 and 1.59 pp. For the same model, Natural Skill Use accuracy differs by up to 38.96 pp across harnesses (DeepSeek V4 Pro), and the Spearman correlation between the harnesses’ model rankings drops from 0.90 under No Skill to 0.51 under Natural Skill Use, consistent with the ranking changes Skill-Use observed for skill-use scores (Han et al., 2026a). The same difference appears in resource access. A skill counts as opened once the target skill is read or loaded. Under Natural Skill Use the opening rates of Claude Code and Codex are 12.45% and 65.42%, whereas both approach saturation under Operationally Supported Skill Use (96.90% and 99.64%; Appendix E.1). Across the 18 combinations, opening rate and Natural Skill Use accuracy correlate at 0.91 (Pearson); Kimi K2.7 Code and Claude Opus 4.8, with similar opening rates under both harnesses, also show the smallest accuracy differences (3.90 and 1.95 pp). Thus, autonomous skill utilization is a property of the model–harness combination; results and rankings under one harness do not transfer directly to another. Opening a skill does not eliminate underuse. Pairing runs by model, harness, and task, we de-compose the net EBU gap by the change in opening status from Natural Skill Use to Operationally Supported Skill Use. Of the 56.64 pp gap of Claude Code, 54.69 pp comes from pairs that go from unopened to opened and only 1.95 pp from pairs opened under both conditions; for Codex the parts 7are 18.33 and 13.13 pp, the latter 42.0% of its 31.24 pp gap. Yet on same-task pairs in which the skill is opened under both conditions, Operationally Supported Skill Use still raises accuracy from 69.23% to 85.21% for Claude Code and from 63.97% to 84.15% for Codex (counts in Appendix E.1), so opening records alone cannot tell whether a skill’s benefit has been realized. An Atria/Codex trace on a Delta gap task shows this directly: the model reads the skill’s rule AG −

12, calls it “unambiguous”, yet submits an answer computed from the memorized ∆AG − ∆HCO 3;under Operationally Supported Skill Use the same model follows the resource and answers correctly (Appendix E.3). In medical tasks governed by a specification, a model may thus read the given rule and still substitute a familiar definition, producing a plausible result that does not comply with the provided specification. 

Prompted Skill Use closes only a small part of the EBU gap. Prompted Skill Use encourages the model to use relevant resources without naming them. Relative to Natural Skill Use, accuracy rises from 38.38% to 46.65%, only 8.26 pp, closing 18.8% of the mean EBU gap (by aggregate net successes) and leaving 35.68 pp to the 82.32% of Operationally Supported Skill Use, which is higher in all 18 combinations. Under the same budget and editing permissions, the skill revisions produced by EBU-Evolve are more effective than existing prompt-optimization and skill-optimization methods. In the common GLM-5.2 test configuration, EBU-Evolve raises Natural Skill Use accuracy from 41.38% to 96.55%, a gain of 55.17 pp, against 2.30 and 18.39 pp for the best configurations of GEPA 8(Agrawal et al., 2026) and SkillOpt (Yang et al., 2026), across all task types (Figure B.1a). Supplying the same paired feedback to the reflection modules of GEPA and SkillOpt (Paired) adds 2.30 and 10.34 pp over their standard settings (Std), so the Paired configuration also enriches the revision evidence of existing methods. Two ablations test the EBU-Evolve design: decomposing the frozen GLM-5.2 artifact to separate the contributions of the description and body revisions, and comparing against generic trace-reflection rewriting to test the overall EBU-driven design. Revising the description yields a larger gain with less added text, while jointly revising the description and body gives the highest skill-use accuracy (Appendix B.8.1, Figure B.1b). Description-only editing retains 85.42% of the joint net gain. In separate controls, fixing the evolved description while removing body and reference access reduces accuracy from 91.95% to 50.57%; changing only the description with these resources fixed raises opening from 62.07% to 100%. EBU-Evolve also outperforms rule frontloading without execution feedback (Appendix B.8.1). Thus, description revision improves access, while body and reference resources support task completion. 

EBU-Evolve achieves higher accuracy than generic rewriting across independent optimiza-tions. In the initial comparison, EBU-Evolve attains 96.55% accuracy, compared with 88.51% for generic trace-reflection rewriting (Appendix B.8.2, Figure B.1c). To examine whether this advantage persists across independently optimized skills, we repeat reflection, editing, review, and validation selection three times. EBU-Evolve outperforms generic rewriting in all three runs, with a mean ac-curacy advantage of 10.73 pp. Abstract screening contributes the largest gain; across the remaining three task groups, the aggregate advantage is positive in every run and averages 5.47 pp (Appen-dices B.8.4–B.8.5). These results show that the advantage recurs across optimization runs, with its magnitude varying by task group. 

Paired evidence, gain-based case selection, and utilization diagnosis jointly improve revision quality. Controlled ablations show that paired evidence raises mean accuracy; adding either se-lection or diagnosis improves it further, with the full method scoring highest (Appendix B.8.3). Thus, given same-task comparisons, selecting cases with score differences and identifying utiliza-tion problems helps produce effective revisions: selection focuses reflection on relevant cases, while diagnosis guides changes to access or rule application. These results support the effectiveness of EBU-Evolve’s design: distilling instructions through paired evidence, case selection, and utilization diagnosis improves autonomous skill utilization. 

Qualitative analysis reveals two EBU mechanisms. Comparing traces of the same training task under the two conditions, one mechanism is failing to acquire the rule, e.g., answering after reading only the main SKILL.md without opening the reference file needed for the judgment; the other is recognizing the task condition but not carrying it into the computation, e.g., excluding assay-eligible but unannotated entities in Enrichment review (Appendix B.9, Figure B.4). 

The evolved skills state the applicability conditions and execution order of existing rules more concretely, at a limited context cost of 227 added tokens per skill on average. Rewriting also corrects the object of computation rather than merely inducing more reading: on a held-out Enrich-ment review test task, the original and evolved skills read the same files, yet only the evolved skill keeps all 12 eligible entities and answers correctly. As in §5.1, reading a skill does not guarantee following it; problems diagnosed in training are corrected on tasks outside the revision. EBU-Evolve improves Atria-Dawn-Preview’s overall Natural Skill Use accuracy while a few tasks regress (Figure 3a). Three independent optimizations with Atria-Dawn all improve overall accuracy, with source-cluster analysis indicating uncertainty as task-source composition varies. Regressions involve both missing necessary materials and misapplying evidence-strength rules after reading, showing that aggregate gains can coexist with task-specific access and application errors (Appendix B.8.6). 

6 RELATED WORK 

Agent evaluation and skill benchmarks. Evaluation of LLMs and LLM agents covers interactive environments (Liu et al., 2024b; Merrill et al., 2026) and specialized domains such as code repair, scientific data analysis, medical calculation, bioinformatics, and electronic health records (Jimenez 9et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2025; Khandekar et al., 2024; Mitchener et al., 2025; Jiang et al., 2025; Fa et al., 2026). Skill benchmarks instead evaluate the resources an agent is given: SkillsBench quantifies the benefit of a skill by paired evaluation of the same task with and without it and reports that this benefit varies across harnesses, a design we follow (Li et al., 2026); SWE-Skills-Bench uses the same comparison (Han et al., 2026b), and SkillLearnBench tests whether generated skills transfer across instances of the same task (Zhong et al., 2026). Unlike these with/without designs, EBU-Bench holds the task and skill fixed and contrasts Natural Skill Use with Operationally Supported Skill Use, quantifying the part of a skill’s benefit the agent does not realize on its own. 

From resource availability to effective use. Providing information does not guarantee that a model uses it: performance depends on where the information sits in the context (Liu et al., 2024a), and tool-use research separates whether a tool is invoked from how it is used (Ross et al., 2025), while selective skill-invocation methods learn when to invoke a skill (Chen et al., 2026). For skills, con-trolled comparisons of loading, selection, and retrieval conditions show that explicit invocation in-structions raise the reading rate while prescribed steps may still be skipped (Liu et al., 2026b; Ying et al., 2026); finer analyses decompose skill use into triggering, adherence, and boundaries, dis-tinguish tested from satisfied constraints, or compare outcomes on tasks where the skill is actually retrieved (Han et al., 2026a; Tan et al., 2026; Cho & Park, 2026). The harness also affects perfor-mance and cost (Yao et al., 2026), and agents may follow repository context files without gaining accuracy (Gloaguen et al., 2026). Access, adherence, and task-level benefit therefore need separate measurement; EBU-Bench measures underuse by the difference in skill-use accuracy rather than by reading or adherence, and examines its variation across harnesses with the model and skill fixed. 

Improving skills from execution feedback. Retaining execution experience as an external resource is one route to continual improvement, from verbal reflections, cross-task rules, and programmatic skill libraries to prompt and context optimization with natural-language feedback (Shinn et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024; Yuksekgonul et al., 2025; Agrawal et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026); tool documentation and skill descriptions can likewise be rewritten from interaction problems or routing errors (Qu et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2026).For skills themselves, execution feedback has been used to discover new skills, summarize lessons, and select revised versions under validation feedback (Alzubi et al., 2026; Ni et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026c). These methods contrast different execution conditions: SkillGrad pairs current successes with failures under the initial skill (Wang et al., 2026a); SkillGen derives procedures from successes and failures without a skill and verifies the net benefit of the newly generated resource (Ma et al., 2026); and SkillAudit audits same-task traces with and without a skill to guide revision (Gao et al., 2026). EBU-Evolve instead contrasts two use conditions of the same skill: successful executions under operational sup-port locate the methods omitted or misapplied during autonomous use, and revisions are selected by Natural Skill Use outcomes. 

7 CONCLUSION 

We introduce MedSkill-E 2 to evaluate and improve medical agents’ skill underuse. We define Enable But Underuse (EBU) as the phenomenon in which a skill supports task completion under operational support but its benefits are not fully realized autonomously. EBU-Bench combines public resources and tasks derived from real research questions through multistage screening, skill matching, and executable scoring. Its 331 challenging tasks span 12 groups across clinical calculation, biomedical data analysis, evidence-based literature screening, and functional evidence assessment. Comparing natural and supported use of the same tasks and skills quantifies unrealized benefits, providing a common basis for studying underuse. On the 154-task Lite benchmark, nine frontier models under two harnesses show a mean EBU gap of 43.94 pp. This gap varies across harnesses with the model and skill fixed; paired traces reveal failures in both instruction access and rule application. EBU-Evolve’s intervention-to-instruction distillation incorporates guidance from these comparisons into descriptions and bodies, improving Natural Skill Use on held-out tasks of the same skill and trans-ferring across models and harnesses. Component ablations and independent optimizations support this approach; mechanism comparisons show that descriptions and body resources complement each other in effective skill utilization. In biomedicine, these findings distinguish knowledge availability from correct method execution. MedSkill-E 2 converts omitted conditions and misapplied rules into reusable, inspectable skill revi-10 sions, offering a path to improving medical evidence screening, assessment, and analysis. Expert review and validation in real medical workflows could test effectiveness across broader tasks, sup-porting more reliable execution in biomedical agent research and evidence processing. MedSkill-E 2 focuses on biomedical tasks. Stage 1: No Skill screening. This stage has two rounds. The first round evaluates all runnable entries without providing a skill, using DeepSeek V4 Pro (GPT-4o for chest X-ray tasks), and records 2,966 tasks that do not reach full marks. The second round re-evaluates these candidates with GPT-5.5 (Codex, reasoning effort medium), removes tasks that are fully solved, and keeps 1,293. For non-binary scores such as F1, “not fully solved” means a score below full marks. 

Stage 2: skill pairing and effectiveness validation. Candidate skills come from a locally curated skill set, GitHub repository search, the Bioconductor index, and skill marketplace listings, 15,368 entries after merging. Two rounds of filtering by domain relevance, documentation completeness, and maintenance status retain 1,996 entries, of which 1,154 are native agent skills; the retrieval pool actually used for pairing contains 1,478 entries. Pairing proceeds in three steps: the model first proposes 3–6 required capabilities from the task (without solving it), lexical retrieval over skill names and descriptions then keeps at most 25 candidates, and finally the model re-ranks them and returns 1–3 skills. The retriever is not given reference answers at this stage. Each candidate skill is then provided on its own, the agent is asked to read it and perform the relevant operations, and pairs that can be completed under Operationally Supported Skill Use are kept. Some BixBench skills were organized into procedural documents from public methodological material and re-validated for effectiveness. 

Stage 3: screening for an autonomous-use gap. For each task–skill pair, we compare Natural Skill Use with Operationally Supported Skill Use using the same skill version, and retain tasks that are not completed under the former but completed under the latter. Screening primarily used GLM-5.1, with one comparison planned per task–skill pair. For the 113 retained tasks, the screening records contain 163 comparisons satisfying this criterion: 74 tasks have one such comparison, and 39 have multiple comparisons. The additional comparisons arose mainly from retries following API errors, program failures, or premature termination.

A.5 COMPARISON WITH RELATED SKILL BENCHMARKS 

Existing skill benchmarks evaluate the task gains from providing skills, the process of skill use, and reuse across instances. EBU-Bench builds on paired task-outcome evaluation and focuses on how much of the benefit of the same skill is realized: it holds the task and skill version fixed, compares Natural Skill Use with Operationally Supported Skill Use, and measures the task gains that remain unrealized during autonomous execution. Successful-use validation establishes an empirical reference for diagnosis. Related benchmarks use different construction checks to establish the relationship between tasks and skills. SkillsBench combines end-to-end oracle verification with actual with/without-skill runs: the reference solution must pass all tests, tasks must be tested with agents under both conditions, and tasks are screened for measurable skill-related gains (Li et al., 2026, §3 and Appendices B.4–B.7). Whether the oracle must use the paired skills, and whether every task must be fully solved in the with-skill condi-tion, are not specified. SWE-Skills-Bench manually screens a subset of public skills and explicitly loads the designated skill in its paired evaluations (Han et al., 2026b). Skill-Use validates the ex-ecutability, cross-component consistency, and scoring reliability of skill–task–rubric triples (Han 20 et al., 2026a). During construction, OpenSkillEval checks that task specifications and instructions are information-complete and consistent with the source materials, filtering inconsistent instances; subsequent skill-augmented executions compare final artifact quality (Ying et al., 2026, §§2.1 and 3.1). SkillLearnBench uses the same or a stronger model to confirm at least one successful com-pletion per instance with the human-authored skill set (Zhong et al., 2026, §3.1); this construction check applies to human-authored reference skills, while generated skills are assessed in the subse-quent evaluation. 

EBU-Bench requires successful task completion with the paired skill under operational sup-port for inclusion, establishing execution evidence of successful use for each retained task–skill pair. It then retains tasks that are not completed under Natural Skill Use, focusing on cases with demonstrated successful use but unrealized gains during autonomous execution. Beyond checking task specifications and source-material consistency, this validation directly tests whether the paired skill can support completion of the current task, providing a successful-use reference for the sub-sequent natural-use evaluation. The resulting challenge set supports targeted study of how skill benefits are realized across models and harnesses. 

Measuring the gap from providing a skill to realizing its benefits. SkillsBench lets agents dis-cover and use one or more mounted skills without naming them in the task, and measures their gains through with/without-skill comparisons. SkillLearnBench further compares human-authored and generated skills across instances of the same task. SWE-Skills-Bench loads the full skill through a native command, while OpenSkillEval explicitly requires skill use; both compare against a no-skill condition. Skill-Use holds skill content fixed in its on-demand-reading versus full-preloading ex-periment, pairing runs by task and model under Codex to study the effect of access conditions, and additionally tests distractor skills. EBU-Bench combines comparisons of use conditions for the same skill with final task outcomes. It defines four conditions for each task; the three skill-provided conditions—Natural, Prompted, and Operationally Supported Skill Use—share the same skill version, with one matched skill per task. Under Natural Skill Use, the agent decides whether to read the skill and how to execute it. Operationally Supported Skill Use explicitly requires reading the skill and performing its task-relevant operations. No Skill and Prompted Skill Use provide references for performance without the skill and the effect of a general reminder. Skill-use accuracy is the mean binary score of final answers, and the EBU gap is ∆EBU (m, h ) = Psupported (m, h ) − Pnatural (m, h ), where m and h

denote the model and harness, and P is the mean task score under the corresponding condition; the main evaluation uses 154 Lite tasks. Performance under operational support thus provides an empirical reference, and the EBU gap quantifies task gains that remain unrealized under natural use. 

The EBU-Bench metric extends task-performance evaluation to the diagnosis of unrealized skill benefits. SkillsBench, SWE-Skills-Bench, OpenSkillEval, and SkillLearnBench assess skill effects through verifier scores, executable checks, artifact quality, or task accuracy; Skill-Use’s SU score focuses on triggering, compliance, and boundary constraints. EBU-Bench instead uses supported-use accuracy on the same tasks and skills as an empirical reference for the gains left un-realized under autonomous use. Even when providing a skill already improves performance, the evaluation can reveal how much additional benefit remains unrealized. Paired execution traces connect this gap to skill access, rule acquisition, and rule application, informing revisions to skill descriptions and execution guidance. 

Paired-trace analysis connects skill access with task gains. Existing benchmarks provide several process-level observations. SkillsBench reports invocation rates and performance on the invoked subset (Appendix K, Table 14). Skill-Use gates SU by triggering and compares triggering rates, compliance, and boundary adherence under on-demand reading and full preloading. Before its main experiments, OpenSkillEval reports reading rates of 48% and 94% under default and forced-use conditions. SkillLearnBench reports trajectory alignment and skill usage rate alongside accuracy, while SWE-Skills-Bench reports behavioral counts in selected skill-level case studies in §4.2. 21 B METHOD DETAILS OF EBU-E VOLVE 

This appendix gives the reflection inputs, candidate construction, and selection rules of EBU-Evolve as shown in Figure 1c. B.1 PAIRED EVIDENCE AND REFLECTION INPUTS 

For the original skill s0, we run Natural Skill Use and Operationally Supported Skill Use under the same model, harness, and execution limits. Both sides receive the same task inputs and skill resources; the latter additionally appends a predetermined operational-support prompt. The prompt requires reading or applying the existing resources and does not supply the answer to the current task. For the tasks authored around research questions, the prompt requires reading the skill entry file and consulting the necessary reference materials it points to. The reflection model receives the following materials:      

> Material Content and use Original skill and reference materials Provide the rules, applicability conditions, and operational basis for the revision Overview of all training pairs Records both sides’ task scores and access facts, showing improved, unchanged, and degraded pairs Selected complete pairs Keep the prompt, tool calls and returns, final answers, task scores, and access records for analyzing specific changes Training access diagnosis Distinguishes instructions not read, instructions read but misapplied, and access status unknown

When selecting complete pairs, we prioritize cases whose Natural Skill Use score improves, include unchanged or degraded cases when the input limit allows, and fill the remainder in a predetermined order. If the context budget is exceeded, the number of cases is reduced while every selected trace is kept in full; the overview of all training pairs is still supplied alongside the complete cases. The overview may be cited for scores and access facts, whereas specific revisions must be supported by complete cases and the original materials. The reflection model maps the differences observed in the paired executions to existing rules; pairs without improvement are used to test the scope of the proposed revisions. Access status is judged from explicit operations in the execution records, not inferred from answer correctness or the appearance of file names; it is left unknown when records are missing. If the agent did not read the relevant instructions, the reflection model considers whether the description needs adjusting; if it did, the model further examines how the rules were applied. The reflection output includes the pairs supporting the change, counterexamples to consider, ci-tations to the original materials, reusable revision rules, and suggested locations. Revision rules state which applicability conditions or execution steps should be clarified, moved earlier, or reor-ganized, covering a rule’s applicability conditions, execution steps, and how its results are used. Citations must be locatable in the original skill package. Neither reflection nor editing receives per-task prompts, answers, traces, or scores from the validation or test sets. B.2 CANDIDATE CONSTRUCTION WITH A SHARED BODY 

Revision rules take effect only if the agent reads and correctly applies them during autonomous execution; candidates therefore consider both the description and the body. Let the original skill be s0 = ( d0, b 0), with description d0 and body b0. From the original materi-als, distilled revision rules, and training utilization diagnosis, the editing model produces a revised description d′ and body b′. We construct three candidates: 

sbody = ( d0, b ′), sjoint = ( d′, b ′), sdescription = ( d′, b 0).

The body-only and joint candidates share the revised body; the joint and description-only candidates share the revised description. This construction compares editing scopes from the same edit. All 22 candidates preserve the other metadata and reference resources and are reviewed separately. Those passing review enter validation-set selection. The description tells the executing agent when the skill applies and which requirements are essential, while the body organizes the complete rules and steps. Added content must preserve the applicability conditions of the original method and must not store training-task identifiers, specific values, or answers. B.3 CANDIDATE REVIEW 

Editing is limited to the body of SKILL.md and the description where permitted; other metadata, reference files, code, and data remain unchanged. Every candidate is subject to an edit-size cap, computed as the number of lines added plus lines deleted relative to the parent version, where re-placing a line counts as one deletion and one addition. Passages designated for preservation must remain locatable in the candidate. A source check verifies newly added identifiers, existing dependencies, and links to reference files. An independent semantic review further checks whether the changes are supported by the original materials, whether the applicability conditions of a rule have been altered incorrectly, and whether the description still addresses the executing agent. Proposals that fail the review are not evaluated. A candidate that fails the review may be repaired once according to the review comments; the repair does not use feedback from the validation or test sets. B.4 VALIDATION -S ET SELECTION 

Selection is performed under Natural Skill Use, reproducing how a skill is read and executed by the agent on its own in practice. The original skill and the eligible candidates are evaluated under Natural Skill Use on the same validation set, with the same model, harness, and per-task execution limits. A candidate must com-plete all scheduled tasks and repetitions with a valid score each time; candidates with incomplete evaluations do not enter the selectable set, and missing scores are not filled with zeros. To stabilize selection, each task is repeated K times ( K = 3 unless otherwise specified); scores are first averaged within a task and then across tasks:
