## ABSTRACT 

Modern data management systems help organize large text collec-tions for downstream tasks such as finding relevant studies and extracting clauses from contracts. A common way to make long documents searchable at scale is to split them into chunks and index each chunk as a separate record. Chunking can separate text from its identifying details. The indexing challenge is to keep each chunk independently searchable while preserving the context needed to find and rank it. Existing methods either encode that context in chunk vectors or attach it as a text prefix. Context encoded only in vectors is unavailable to text rerankers. Prompted prefixes serve both vector search and reranking, but are costly to generate at index-ing time and can underperform contextual encoders. This motivates us to train lightweight language models to generate more effective prefixes at lower indexing cost. We propose ConTra ( Context That Travels ), a reinforcement learning framework whose reward com-bines feedback from frozen embedding and reranking models with factual verification and length control. ConTra generates one con-cise prefix per chunk during index construction and stores the aug-mented text and its embedding for reuse across queries. To evaluate contextual retrieval across workloads and under interference, we introduce RealContextBench, a benchmark with five application collections and three controlled conditions that isolate the effect of distracting text. On ConTEB and RealContextBench, Con-Tra-2B outperforms the prompted 9B baseline using Anthropic’s method and the PPLX-4B contextual encoder, with a margin of up to 3.15 points over their best overall scores, while indexing short documents 7 .74 × and 2 .15 × as fast as those baselines, respectively. Post-trained only on ConTEB, it still achieves the highest overall reranking score on RealContextBench and is less sensitive to random and topically similar distractors. Code, datasets, and trained models are available at https://github.com/SStan1/ConTra. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Data management systems organize text collections, which can span millions of source documents [20], to support downstream tasks such as finding relevant studies [27], extracting clauses from contracts [35], and generating literature digests [27]. To make long documents searchable at scale, systems often split them into smaller units, called chunks [6, 20], that can be stored as separate records and indexed for retrieval [37, 39]. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) systems are commonly used to retrieve chunks from the index [19, 52]. The retrieved chunks can be returned directly as search results or used as evidence to answer questions [44] and check factual claims [27, 28]. For RAG over large collections, the index must retrieve the evidence needed for an accurate answer [44,

52] while remaining affordable to build and fast to query [20]. In a multi-stage retrieval pipeline, a system first uses a vector index to shortlist chunks by embedding similarity [20, 37]. To improve ranking accuracy, the system often uses a reranker to score each shortlisted chunk’s text for relevance to the query and reorder the candidates by that score [20, 22]. However, conventional chunking methods can separate a passage from the context that identifies it [44]. For example, consider a query for a company’s operating income in a particular fiscal year [13]: the financial table containing the figure may rely on a preceding heading to identify the company and year. If that heading falls in another chunk, vector search may retrieve a similar table for the wrong company or period, and a reranker reading the table alone lacks the evidence to correct the error. To improve retrieval quality at scale, systems must address the fundamental data management challenge of keeping chunks independently searchable while preserving the document context needed to find the right candidates and rank them correctly. Such a challenge of preserving document context has attracted considerable attention from data scientists [7, 12, 44] as well as from AI companies such as Perplexity [10], Voyage AI [41], and Anthropic [2]. Two lines of work address this challenge by encoding document context into chunk vectors or preserving it explicitly in text. Figure 1 illustrates how these approaches differ in the context available to the vector index and the reranker. The first line of work incorporates surrounding document information into chunk vectors via late chunking [12]. Building on this design, InSeNT [7] trains encoders to distinguish chunks from the same document, and PPLX [10] adapts an autoregressive large language model for con-textual encoding. These methods can improve candidate selection by changing only the vector representation of each chunk. How-ever, this line of work faces several limitations. PPLX, for example, requires substantial additional pretraining to enable bidirectional attention, adding to the time and computation required to train the encoder. Furthermore, a reranker that reads the original chunk text cannot use document context encoded only in the chunk vector. The second line of work preserves document context explicitly in text. Anthropic’s method prompts a large language model to read the source document and generate a prefix for each chunk, supplying the augmented text to both retrieval and reranking [1]. The prefixes can be reused across queries, a benefit of enriching the index offline [5]. The upfront cost is the substantial time and computation needed to generate a prefix for every chunk during index construction. Prompted prefixes have also underperformed leading contextual encoders in prior retrieval evaluations [10]. However, those evaluations primarily examined single-stage re-trieval, where chunks are ranked by embedding similarity alone, overlooking multi-stage retrieval. In Section 3, we use controlled experiments to measure the contribu-tion of prefixes at each stage and demonstrate this potential. To realize this potential at a lower indexing cost, we introduce ConTra ( Context That Travels ), a novel reinforcement learning framework for training lightweight language models to write con-cise, chunk-specific prefixes for both candidate selection and rerank-ing (Section 4). At indexing time, the trained model generates one prefix per chunk. ConTra prepends the prefix to the original text and stores the augmented text and its embedding under the original chunk identifier. At query time, the vector index uses the embedding to select candidates, and the reranker reads the same augmented text to refine their order. During training, frozen embedding and reranking models evaluate each sampled prefix by scoring the aug-mented chunk against the training query. A frozen judge model checks whether the source supports the information added by each prefix, and a length penalty limits prefix length and reduces com-petition among chunks from the same document. Together, these signals let small models generate accurate prefixes that distinguish the target chunk. To accommodate different indexing budgets across deployments, we train ConTra-0.8B, ConTra-2B, and ConTra-4B. Existing benchmarks such as ConTEB [7] emphasize cases where document context is needed to identify relevant chunks. In practice, document collections mix self-contained and context-dependent chunks, and surrounding text can introduce competing entities or topics. Evaluating contextual retrieval therefore requires testing both how it uses helpful context and how it handles distracting text. To this end, we introduce RealContextBench (Section 6), a contextual chunk retrieval benchmark comprising five application collections and three controlled context conditions. The applica-tion collections span web search, technical question answering, story comprehension, meetings, and financial analysis, with human annotations mapped to relevant chunks. In the controlled condi-tions, we hold queries, target chunks, and relevance labels fixed and present each chunk on its own, with randomly sampled text, or with topically similar distractors generated by a large language model. RealContextBench enables systematic comparisons of how retrieval systems represent and use document context, inform-ing the design of document indexes that support reliable search across diverse collections and workloads. Our contributions are summarized as follows: 

• Our controlled experiments reveal the substantial potential of readable prefixes in multi-stage retrieval by showing that their gains in candidate recall and reranking are complementary. Build-ing on this finding, we propose ConTra, a reinforcement learn-ing framework combining online embedding and reranking eval-uation, factual verification, and length control to train prefix generators across multiple model scales. By encouraging informa-tive, source-supported, and concise prefixes, ConTra improves contextual retrieval quality while lowering indexing cost. 

• We introduce RealContextBench, a benchmark for comparing how contextual encoders and prefix-based methods index docu-ment context. It measures end-to-end retrieval quality across five application collections and isolates the effect of random and top-ically similar distractors in three controlled context conditions that vary the surrounding text while holding the search task fixed. Our benchmark enables researchers to evaluate how in-dexing decisions about document context affect search reliability across diverse workloads. 

• Through extensive experiments, we show that ConTra delivers state-of-the-art end-to-end retrieval quality with substantially lower indexing cost and that RealContextBench reveals which methods remain effective across workloads and under interfer-ence. ConTra-2B outperforms the prompted 9B prefix baseline using Anthropic’s method and the PPLX-4B contextual encoder on both datasets, with a margin of up to 3.15 points over their best overall scores. Post-trained only on ConTEB, ConTra-2B still ranks first overall on RealContextBench after reranking. ConTra-2B also indexes short documents 7 .74 × as fast as the 9B pipeline and 2 .15 × as fast as PPLX-4B, while post-training with reinforcement learning reduces total indexing time by up to 52.3% at a fixed 2B model scale. 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

Retrieval Models and Pipelines. Evaluating complex relevance models across an entire large corpus is too costly at query time. 

> 2

Modern retrieval pipelines, as supported by frameworks such as PyTerrier-RAG and RAGPerf [20, 22], therefore combine fast can-didate retrieval with compute-intensive reranking to balance effi-ciency and accuracy [16]. Given a query, a retriever first selects a small candidate set using an embedding model, often paired with lexical matching in multi-way retrieval [11, 45]. While BM25 relies on term overlap and corpus statistics to match exact names and identifiers [33], queries and passages frequently express the same meaning with different words. Embedding models address this lexical gap by mapping queries and passages into a shared continuous vector space to capture se-mantic similarity [14, 15, 24, 45]. Early architectures build upon bidi-rectional BERT encoders [8, 21, 32], whereas recent models such as E5-Mistral and Qwen3-Embedding adapt pretrained LLMs [43, 50]. To combine different retrieval signals within a single backbone, M3-Embedding further unifies dense, sparse, and multi-vector re-trieval [4]. Because these retrievers only narrow the corpus down to a candidate set, a reranker must then judge fine-grained rele-vance by jointly evaluating the query and candidate text [29, 53]. Cross-encoders such as BERT rerankers and Qwen3-Reranker evaluate individual query–passage pairs independently [25, 50], while monoT5 formulates this judgment as relevance-token gen-eration [26]. Rather than judging pairs in isolation, RankGPT compares a group of candidates through listwise ordering [38], whereas RankRAG unifies reranking with answer generation [48]. Ultimately, the retriever determines which candidates reach the reranker, and the text the reranker reads determines what document context it can rely on to judge relevance. 

Contextual Retrieval. Contextual retrieval mitigates the informa-tion loss caused by partitioning documents into isolated chunks. The first paradigm encodes surrounding document context directly into chunk vectors. Late chunking achieves this by encoding an entire source window before pooling token representations within each chunk boundary [12]. Extending this approach, InSeNT trains contextual encoders using in-document negatives, encouraging models to preserve passage-specific details alongside shared docu-ment context [7]. Adapting this paradigm to autoregressive LLMs requires overcoming causal attention, which restricts tokens to preceding context. PPLX adapts an LLM backbone via diffusion pretraining on roughly 250 billion tokens to enable bidirectional attention, followed by contrastive retrieval training [10]. Similarly, Voyage-context-3 and Voyage-context-4 provide document-aware chunk embeddings via proprietary APIs [41, 42]. However, PPLX incurs substantial additional pretraining overhead, and storing con-text implicitly in vectors leaves downstream components reading raw text—such as rerankers—without access to that context. The second paradigm preserves context explicitly as readable text. An-thropic prompts an LLM to generate a concise prefix situating each chunk within its source document, prepending it to the passage for indexing and reranking [2]. Because prefixes are materialized at indexing time, they are reusable for retrieval and reranking across queries while remaining directly legible to rerankers. Neverthe-less, prompting large LLMs incurs high indexing latency and com-putational cost, whereas smaller prompted models often produce verbose or imprecise prefixes that limit retrieval gains. ## 3 OBSERVATION: THE ROLE OF PREFIXES IN CONTEXTUAL RETRIEVAL 

We expect retrieval to benefit when several stages can reuse the same readable prefix. Anthropic tried this approach in its contextual retrieval pipeline [2] but did not investigate it in depth. On ConTEB, we vary which stages receive the prefix to measure its contribution at each stage (Table 1). Our analysis shows that a readable prefix can serve multiple retrieval stages, with complementary gains in our experiments. Even with prefixes generated by an untrained 2B model, the resulting pipeline gives better final rankings than retrieving with PPLX-4B and reranking raw chunks. 

Controlled comparison of prefix placement. Using fixed prefixes generated by an untrained Qwen3.5-2B model with An-thropic’s prompt, Qwen3-Embedding-4B retrieves the top 15 candidates from either raw or prefix-augmented chunks. Qwen3-Reranker-4B then scores each candidate set using either raw or prefix-augmented text. We also evaluate both reranker views over fixed top-15 candidates retrieved by PPLX-4B. Reusing the exact same prefix per chunk across all settings isolates the effect of where the prefix is used. 

Prefixes improve embedding retrieval and supply more gold chunks to the reranker. Prepending document context before encoding helps the embedding model distinguish a relevant chunk from similar passages, strengthening initial retrieval ranking. This better ranking brings more gold chunks into the reranker’s candi-date pool, raising Recall@15 from 0.7691 to 0.8475. Even when the reranker reads only raw chunk text, the stronger candidate pool raises final nDCG@10 from 0.6483 to 0.6924. 

Prefixes directly help the reranker judge relevance. Even with-out bringing additional gold chunks into the shortlist, prefixes improve the reranker’s relevance judgments. We rerank the same candidates retrieved from raw chunks twice, changing only whether the reranker sees the prefix. Making the prefix visible raises final nDCG@10 from 0.6483 to 0.6940, showing that the added context

Higher candidate recall alone does not guarantee a better final ranking. PPLX-4B achieves strong initial retrieval at 0.9387 Recall@15 and 0.8210 embedding nDCG@10, bringing more gold chunks into the candidate pool than retrieving with prefixes from an untrained 2B model. Yet when the reranker reads only raw text, the PPLX-4B pipeline yields only 0.7310 final nDCG@10, trailing the 0.7585 achieved when both Qwen3-Embedding-4B and Qwen3-Reranker-4B read those prefixes. PPLX-4B encodes document con-text in the chunk vectors, so the information that helped select a passage is unavailable to a reranker reading the passage in isolation. Attaching the same readable prefixes to the fixed PPLX-4B candi-dates tests this explanation. Without retrieving a single additional candidate, the reranker can then rank the relevant chunks higher, raising final nDCG@10 to 0.8135. 

A readable prefix benefits both embedding and reranking, showing its potential. The prefix helps at either stage, and using it at both stages improves the ranking further. These complemen-tary gains allow even an untrained 2B prefix generator to yield better final rankings than PPLX-4B retrieval with raw-text rerank-ing, illustrating the potential of readable prefixes in multi-stage retrieval. 

## 4 CONTRA: LEARNING TO WRITE CONTEXT PREFIXES 

To realize the potential of LLMs in multi-stage retrieval, we intro-duce ConTra, a framework that trains them to generate more effec-tive context prefixes. For each training instance, the generator reads a source window and target chunk, samples candidate prefixes, and prepends each candidate to the original chunk. Frozen embedding and reranking models then evaluate how well the augmented text matches a training query. A factual judge verifies whether the added information is supported by the source and correctly attributed to the target, while length control discourages superfluous details. We combine these signals into a reward to update the generator. After training, the model generates prefixes during indexing, and the resulting text is reused for embedding retrieval and reranking across queries.

## 4.4 Policy Optimization 

We optimize ConTra using DAPO [47]. For each training input, the policy samples a group of 𝐺 = 16 candidate prefixes, which are scored by the frozen embedding model, reranker, and factual judge via Equation 5. Each rollout batch comprises 32 inputs, totaling 512 sampled responses, and only the generator parameters are updated. For prefix 𝑗 within sampling group G( 𝑗 ), its advantage is com-puted by centering its reward around the group mean [36]:

𝑅 𝑘 . (12) We omit group-wise standard deviation normalization so that the true magnitude of reward differences is preserved across comple-tions. Following DAPO, sampling groups in which all candidates receive identical rewards—yielding zero policy gradient—are dy-namically filtered and replaced during rollout. The policy is updated via a token-averaged clipped surrogate objective that reinforces above-average prefixes while suppressing below-average ones, us-ing importance-sampling ratio clipping bounds of [0.8, 1.28 ] and a dual-clipping threshold of 3 .0 for negative advantages. 

## 5 EXPERIMENTAL EVALUATION 

We evaluate ConTra’s retrieval quality on ConTEB and analyze the effects of reward components, retrieval model size, and candidate pool size. 

## 5.1 Experimental Setup 

Benchmark. We evaluate our method on ConTEB [7] because it is specifically designed to measure whether retrieval systems can leverage document-level context to disambiguate isolated chunks, and it also serves as a principal benchmark for contextual encoders such as PPLX [10]. Across its eight tasks, ConTEB combines broad domain coverage with distinct forms of contextual dependence. Specifically, MLDR and SQuAD cover encyclopedic text [31], Narra-tiveQA spans stories and screenplays [17], COVID-QA tests biomed-ical articles [23], and ESG Reports draws from corporate sustain-ability disclosures, alongside Football, Geography, and Insurance. 

Baselines and retrieval components. To establish retrieval qual-ity without document context, we first include the general-purpose Qwen3-Embedding-4B model [50], which encodes each original chunk independently. We then compare against two categories of contextual retrieval methods: contextual encoders that directly em-bed surrounding document context into chunk representations, and LLM-based methods that generate explicit textual prefixes prior to standard encoding. Within the contextual-encoder category, we evaluate ConTEB’s ModernBERT-Large InSeNT checkpoint trained with in-document negatives using late chunking [7], Perplexity’s web-scale PPLX-context models at 0.6B and 4B scales where the 4B model sets the prior reported ConTEB state of the art [10], and Voyage AI’s API-based Voyage-context-3 together with its flagship successor Voyage-context-4 [41, 42]. In the textual-prefix category, we compare ConTra against prompt-based contextual retrieval following Anthropic’s method [2], instantiated with untrained Qwen3.5-0.8B, 2B, 4B, and 9B genera-tors [30]. Both ConTra and these prompted baselines prepend the generated prefix to the original chunk and encode the combined text using the same Qwen3-Embedding-4B model. To compare all methods with a common reranker, we rerank each method’s top 15 candidates with Qwen3-Reranker-4B, which receives original chunks for the context-free and contextual-encoder baselines and prefix-augmented chunks for ConTra and the prompted prefix baselines.

Over same-size Anthropic prompt baselines, ConTra increases final reranked nDCG@10 by 8.15, 9.40, and 3.55 points at 0.8B, 2B, and 4B, respectively.

## 5.3 Reward Ablation 

We remove reranking feedback, factual verification, and length control individually in Table 3, examining how each component shapes generated prefixes and final retrieval accuracy. 

Reranking feedback encourages specific details over generic summaries. Tasks such as Insurance test whether a generator can supply concrete identifying details rather than defaulting to broad topic summaries. Because the reranker jointly evaluates the query and augmented chunk, 𝑅 rank provides the fine-grained preference signal needed to retain exact attributes. Removing 𝑅 rank hits the 2B model hardest, dropping its Insurance nDCG@10 from 1.000 to 0.159, whereas 0.8B and 4B show smaller changes for opposite reasons. The 0.8B model lacks the capacity to parse long-range document structure and stays near 0.16 on Insurance regardless, while the 4B model already extracts country attributes without 

𝑅 rank and maintains 1.000. Sitting between them, the 2B model can locate country headings in the source document, and 𝑅 rank teaches it to keep those identifiers. 

Factual verification ensures prefix reliability for retrieval and downstream use. At 2B, removing 𝑅 fact causes a smaller drop in final retrieval scores than the other ablations, yet it markedly increases factual hallucinations. In a paired manual audit of prefixes for 200 matched target chunks, the factual error rate rises from 4.5% to 16.0% when 𝑅 fact is omitted. Because nDCG@10 scores annotated chunk identifiers rather than prefix text, fabricated prefixes that match query keywords can still rank the target chunk near the top and even inflate embedding retrieval scores at 0.8B and 4B while degrading final reranked nDCG@10. More importantly, in downstream RAG and question-answering pipelines where prefix-augmented chunks serve directly as evidence, unfaithful prefixes corrupt the retrieved context. Adding 𝑅 fact suppresses unsupported claims and keeps prefixes reliable across the pipeline. 

Length control prevents inter-chunk competition and re-duces compute overhead. Based on our observations, concise prefixes of roughly 16 tokens suffice to convey essential identify-ing cues, making lengthy descriptions unnecessary. Removing 𝑅 len 

causes the largest overall retrieval decline at every model size: at 4B, mean prefix length grows from 17.7 to 239.2 tokens while final nDCG@10 falls from 0.8465 to 0.7084. An MLDR document about a Thai wildlife sanctuary illustrates how verbose prefixes induce inter-chunk competition: the full 4B model writes distinct prefixes highlighting each passage’s specific topic, whereas removing length control produces lengthy prefixes that repeat the sanctuary’s shared location and protected status. Prepending identical background to multiple chunks makes neighboring passages overly similar and causes non-target chunks to match the same query terms. Enforc-ing brevity avoids this confusion while reducing autoregressive decoding steps and token overhead during indexing and reranking.

## 6 REALCONTEXTBENCH 

While ConTEB specifically targets scenarios where document con-text is essential to disambiguate or answer a query, real-world retrieval corpora present a more complex landscape. First, practical collections naturally mix self-contained passages—which already contain complete answers independently—with context-dependent chunks that rely on surrounding text; indiscriminate contextualiza-tion risks diluting the crisp representation of self-contained chunks. Second, surrounding text is not always helpful: neighboring pas-sages in long documents frequently introduce competing entities, topic shifts, or topically similar distractions that can pull represen-tations away from the target and induce false matches. Existing benchmarks rarely evaluate whether contextual retrieval preserves precision on self-contained passages or resists interference from noisy surroundings. To bridge this gap, we introduce RealContext-Bench, combining five realistic application corpora with controlled distraction conditions on NanoBEIR to assess both practical utility and robustness against contextual interference. 

## 6.1 Dataset Construction 

We cover five application domains: web search, technical question answering, story comprehension, meetings, and financial analy-sis. For each collection, we retain original questions, split docu-ments into non-overlapping chunks at natural boundaries, and map human-annotated answer spans or supporting evidence to gold chunks. This construction naturally yields a mix of self-contained

Natural Questions (NQ). NQ comprises real Google search queries paired with human-annotated answers on Wikipedia pages [18]. We preserve page order and map annotated answer spans to chunks, using short-answer spans to pinpoint evidence whenever a long answer spans multiple chunks. 

TechQA. TechQA features technical support questions from IBM forums grounded in IBM Technotes [3]. Using its answerable de-velopment queries, we split source documents into chunks and designate the chunk with the highest overlap with the annotated answer span as gold. 

FairytaleQA. Although both FairytaleQA [46] and the NarrativeQA task used in ConTEB [17] evaluate narrative comprehension over stories, the two datasets differ inherently in their task design. Nar-rativeQA was intentionally constructed to encourage non-local questions by having annotators write queries from plot summaries without viewing the full text, making the answers less likely to come from a single passage in the full narrative. By contrast, FairytaleQA consists of expert-written questions covering story elements as well as causal and emotional relationships, paired with annotated supporting sections aligned to natural story breaks and spanning both single-section and cross-section queries. Following this origi-nal structure, we preserve narrative order and map FairytaleQA’s expert-annotated supporting sections directly to retrieval chunks, retaining a natural mix of local and multi-section questions that reflects realistic reading comprehension. 

QMSum. QMSum pairs meeting transcripts with human-written queries and annotated supporting utterances for summariza-tion [51]. We use its specific queries as retrieval questions and construct gold chunks from supporting utterances while preserving speaker labels and turn order. 

FinanceBench. FinanceBench contains specialist-curated finan-cial questions, answers, and supporting evidence from corporate filings [13]. We preserve readable table formatting during chunking and select the chunk with the greatest overlap with the annotated evidence as gold, supporting queries over company names, report-ing periods, and numerical figures. 

NanoBEIR context conditions. NanoBEIR is a small-scale bench-mark of 13 BEIR tasks spanning domains such as news, scientific literature, and question answering [40, 49]. Because standard BEIR passages are largely self-contained, NanoBEIR provides an ideal testbed to operationalize the two failure modes motivated above: whether contextualization dilutes self-contained passages or allows noisy neighbors to corrupt target representations. To isolate the effect of surrounding text from corpus differences, we hold queries, target chunks, and gold annotations strictly fixed while varying only surrounding context across three controlled conditions: the original 

condition presents each target chunk alone as a clean baseline; the 

random condition prepends two randomly sampled passages before it and appends two after it; and in the topical condition, GPT-6 Luna generates four independent passages on the target’s narrow topic, each describing an unrelated subject, case, or event. Every contex-tual method receives identical surrounding passages. Specifically, random neighbors simulate abrupt topic shifts—such as unrelated sections or web boilerplate—testing whether a model blindly ab-sorbs off-topic context and shifts the target representation away from its core semantics.

Contextual interference and indexing cost favor standalone ConTra in noisy or resource-constrained settings. Conversely, standalone ConTra outperforms the hybrid pipeline across all three NanoBEIR conditions at both model sizes, reaching 0.7102 compared with 0.6729 for PPLX retrieval under random interference at 2B. Be-cause the prefixes and reranker are identical, this difference reflects the candidate retriever’s sensitivity to noise: dense contextual vec-tors incorporate surrounding distractors and miss target passages, whereas ConTra’s selective prefixes keep chunk embeddings fo-cused on the target. Furthermore, the hybrid retains ConTra prefix generation while replacing the fast Qwen embedding stage with the more expensive PPLX encoder, increasing indexing computation. In practice, the hybrid setup is best suited for clean corpora benefiting from PPLX candidate retrieval where maximizing recall justifies additional indexing cost, while standalone ConTra offers a simpler, lower-overhead, and interference-resistant pipeline for general and noisy retrieval environments.

ConTra-2B substantially reduces indexing latency relative to 9B and PPLX-4B. Prefix caching yields substantially higher reuse on short documents. Because vLLM hashes and reuses key–value atten-tion states only across requests sharing an identical leading token sequence, cache efficiency depends directly on how source docu-ments are windowed. For short documents, each complete source fits within a single context window, allowing every chunk from the same document to share an identical leading source prompt and reuse precomputed key–value states, yielding an 81.9% cache-hit rate. Conversely, long documents must be partitioned across multiple distinct context windows, shifting leading tokens across windows and reducing the cache-hit rate to 24.9%. This higher pre-fix reuse eliminates most redundant source prefill across chunks and accounts for the larger relative speedup on short documents. 

RL training accelerates inference at fixed model scale. At 2B, short-document indexing time drops from 7.87 s to 3.75 s, driven by a 71.1% reduction in generated output length from 82.4 to 23.8 tokens per chunk, compared with 79.7 tokens for prompted 9B. ## 7.3 Disk Storage 

Retaining generated prefixes adds a lightweight text payload to the index without altering its vector layout. Specifically, each prefix-augmented chunk is encoded into a single 2,560-dimensional 

float32 vector, keeping vector count and dimensionality un-changed. Measuring the UTF-8 size of stored prefixes and sepa-rators relative to raw chunk text plus dense vectors, the median of three saved runs adds 36.1 KB on FairytaleQA and 98.2 KB on Natural Questions. The corresponding original text-and-vector payloads are 3.77 MB and 12.46 MB, so the additions represent only 0.96% and 0.79%, respectively. ConTra thus retains a standard single-vector representation while adding less than 1% in text payload to keep readable context available at retrieval time. 

## 8 CASE STUDIES 

While aggregate ranking metrics quantify overall retrieval accu-racy, inspecting individual predictions reveals how generated pre-fixes reshape retrieved evidence in practice. Figure 2 examines two representative cases from ConTra-2B illustrating complementary behaviors across distinct contextual regimes: recovering missing entity information into an adjacent passage in Natural Questions, and foregrounding existing defining features within an isolated passage in NanoBEIR. 

## 8.1 Answer Information in a Non-Gold Chunk 

The first query asks for the Winter Olympics theme composer, whose annotated answer is Leo Arnaud. ConTra-2B ranks an adja-cent passage first while the annotated gold chunk falls outside the top five, leaving the top-1 passage with no credit under chunk-ID relevance labels. However, because that passage already describes the composition’s Olympic use and sits adjacent to the gold chunk, ConTra’s prefix extracts the composer’s full name from the sur-rounding page and prepends it directly, supplying the complete answer at rank 1. Standard chunk-level evaluation can thus un-derestimate ConTra’s effectiveness, pointing to stronger utility in downstream question answering and RAG pipelines. 

## 8.2 Emphasizing Relevant Information Without Context 

The second case examines an isolated NanoBEIR passage without surrounding context, directly illustrating the self-reinforcing be-havior observed in Section 6.2. The query asks for a definition of rumination syndrome, and the target passage already states the con-dition’s name, alternative term, and primary symptom. As shown in Figure 2, ConTra-2B distills and brings these defining attributes together in the prefix ahead of the original text. With the candidates retrieved by PPLX held fixed, prepending this prefix elevates the gold passage from rank 4 to rank 1.

## 9 CONCLUSION 

Our controlled analysis shows that textual prefixes enrich con-textual chunk embeddings, bringing more gold chunks into the candidate pool. With candidates held fixed, the prefix also helps the reranker judge their relevance. Motivated by these comple-mentary gains and the strong potential of learned prefix genera-tion, we introduce ConTra, which optimizes prefix generation via reinforcement learning with embedding and reranking feedback, factual verification, and length control. Across ConTEB and our newly constructed RealContextBench, ConTra preserves source-grounded details and inter-chunk distinctions while resisting con-textual interference—enabling ConTra-2B to match a prompted 9B generator on application collections and surpass it overall at a fraction of the indexing cost. Ultimately, ConTra uses one prefix per chunk throughout the contextual retrieval pipeline, improving retrieval accuracy, robustness, and indexing efficiency.
