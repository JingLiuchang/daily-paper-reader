## CypherMAS: Benchmarking Routing Reliability in LLM-Based Multi-Agent Systems 

Danil Kostromin 1, 2 , Andrey Sakhovskiy 1, Semen Budennyy 1,3 1 Sber AI 2 RTU MIREA 3 AIRI  

> Correspondence: andrey.sakhovskiy@gmail.com

Abstract 

Multi-agent systems (MAS) built on large lan-guage models (LLMs) distribute reasoning across specialized agents that must exchange messages and invoke tools reliably. Existing evaluations typically couple communication with open domain tasks, making it difficult to determine whether a failure arises from rea-soning, malformed tool use, lost messages, or forgotten requests. Prior benchmarks do not isolate communication execution or measure routing redundancy against a known optimal path. We introduce CypherMAS, a synthetic benchmark that isolates communication and routing in fixed multi-agent graphs. Agents in chain, star, bipartite, or ring graphs route cyphered messages to remote peers holding the matching decoders; since the optimal route is known, redundant LLM calls can be mea-sured and routing failures may be easily tracked. Across eleven open-weight models from 4B to 753B, small models fail at routing and tool use, dense models above 27B degrade with diame-ter, and successful runs exceed minimum LLM calls. These findings identify communication reliability as a distinct scaling bottleneck for multi-agent systems and provide a controlled di-agnostic for complex multi-agent systems. The source code is freely available 1.

1 Introduction 

LLM-based multi-agent systems (MAS) distribute reasoning across specialized agents that exchange messages, invoke tools, and track pending re-quests (Du et al., 2024; Kim et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2025; Zuo et al., 2025; Qiang et al., 2025; Su et al., 2025; Tan et al., 2026). Scaling to dozens of agents introduces failure points absent from single-agent inference: e.g., tool call hallucinations and pending requests loss. Recent work constructs task-specific MAS pipelines or automates their topolo-gies (Li et al., 2023a; Wu et al., 2023; Hong et al.,

Recent failure at-tribution research reduces MAS failure to a binary outcome (Cemri et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025a,d; Chen et al., 2026): a MAS run either produces the correct final answer or fails. This formulation ne-glects communication overhead. A system may succeed while routing messages through redundant loops, masking malformed tool calls with excep-tion handlers, or issuing several times the minimum number of transmissions, thus increasing inference cost. Quantifying this overhead requires a known minimum communication bound per agent. How-ever, the existing benchmarks evaluate on open-ended tasks, providing no theoretical redundancy estimates (Du et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2023; Hong et al., 2024; Kim et al., 2024; Zuo et al., 2025; Zhuge et al., 2024; Qiang et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2025; Su et al., 2025; Tan et al., 2026). A commu-nication reduction may merely reflect that a task lacks genuine multi-agent necessity, rather than demonstrating improved routing efficiency. We introduce CypherMAS, a synthetic bench-mark that provides exact lower-bound routing di-agnostics at the level of individual agents. The benchmark replaces a shared group goal with in-dividual objectives that require cooperation. As seen in Figure 1, each agent receives an encrypted message and a decryption table for another agent’s message. The table required to decrypt its own message is held by a peer. An agent succeeds only if its encrypted message reaches the correct peer, that peer decrypts it, and the decrypted text returns to the owner and is verified. All agents pursue this objective concurrently over a fixed, fully observ-able graph. Agents communicate only with direct neighbours, and message delivery, decryption, and verification occur through tool calls. As the graph and the location of each required decryption table are known, the shortest request and response path is known. 2 Related Work 

LLM-based Multi-Agent Systems. Existing re-search on MAS construction automation has fo-cused primarily on three key directions. Hand-crafted systems fix roles and communication pro-tocols a priori (Li et al., 2023a; Wu et al., 2023; Hong et al., 2024; Kim et al., 2024; Zuo et al., 2025; Tan et al., 2026; Su et al., 2025; Boateng et al., 2026), as seen in MetaGPT (Hong et al., 2024), AutoGen (Wu et al., 2023), and CAMEL (Li et al., 2023a). Partially automated systems opti-mize individual components, such as agent selec-tion (Liu et al., 2024b; Zhou et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2026), prompt design (Fernando et al., 2024; Guo et al., 2024; Chu et al., 2025), or inter-agent topology (Zhuge et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025c, 2026). While prior research explores communication redundancy and topology optimisation (e.g., Agent-Prune (Zhang et al., 2025b) or G-Designer (Zhang et al., 2025c)), these methods typically evaluate procedural tasks where the theoretical minimum communication is unknown. Consequently, they measure redundancy indirectly via changes in a shared final outcome. CypherMAS complements this by providing a ground-truth lower bound, en-abling exact per-hop analysis. Benchmarks for LLM Agents. Current MAS are frequently evaluated on general-purpose tasks, e.g., mathematics or code generation (Zhang et al., 2025c; Zhuge et al., 2024; Li et al., 2026). Dedi-cated agent benchmarks address tool use and rea-soning in complex environments (Liu et al., 2024a; Zhou et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024; Li et al., 2023b). They primarily focus on single agents operating within complex execution pipelines. These benchmarks couple tool execu-tion with semantic reasoning, which prevents the isolation of mechanical execution errors from open-domain reasoning capabilities. Multi-agent bench-marks extend to collaborative settings (Zhu et al., 2025; Lu et al., 2026). All these datasets lack agen-tic specificity and typically provide no theoretical guarantees on whether the multi-agent approach must be preferred over a single LLM and enable no algorithmic analysis of routing overhead. 

Failure attribution and MAS resilience. One line of MAS reliability attributes failures to spe-cific agents and steps. MAST (Cemri et al., 2025), Who&When (Zhang et al., 2025d), and Agent-Tracer (Zhang et al., 2025a) localise failures to spe-cific agents and steps. Alternatively, Huang et al. (2025) and Miao et al. (2026) evaluate topology degradation under faulty agents. ResMAS (Zhou et al., 2026) optimizes topologies and prompts to increase resilience. However, existing research re-duces system quality to a binary task outcome: all agents solve a single shared task collaboratively. Redundant routing loops, silently handled tool er-rors, and unnecessary LLM invocations do not alter the final answer and therefore go undetected.

Objective. Agent Ai must recover and verify the plaintext Pi of its own ciphertext Ci. If Aπ(i) ∈N (Ai), Ai sends Ci directly. Otherwise, Ai routes 

Ci through intermediate agents along a path to 

Aπ(i). The plaintext then returns along the reverse path. At the same time, each agent must decrypt incoming ciphertexts for which it holds the decoder and return the resulting plaintext to the requesting owner. The global task is complete only when all agents have verified their plaintexts. 

Design rationale. The task separates communi-cation execution from semantic reasoning through three modeling choices. First, knowledge incom-pleteness makes communication necessary: Ai can-not succeed without Aπ(i), so no agent can solve its task in isolation. Second, tool-mediated state changes make execution errors observable: for-warding, decryption, and verification occur through typed calls, which separates malformed tool use from reasoning over valid inputs. Third, fixed topology makes redundancy measurable. Let di =

d(Ai, A π(i)) be the shortest-path distance between 

Ai and its decoder holder in G. A successful request–response cycle for Ci requires at least 2di

neighbor-to-neighbor transmissions: di forward transmissions and di return transmissions. Because each transmission corresponds to a send_message 

invocation, 2di is a lower bound on message-level LLM calls for that agent. Summing over agents gives a run-level lower bound, and deviations from this bound quantify routing redundancy. 

3.2 Agent Interface and Execution Tools. Let Ai denote the calling agent. Agents interact through three tools: • send_message (Aj , m ) sends message m to agent Aj . The call is valid only if Aj ∈N (Ai). If Aj /∈ N (Ai), the tool returns an er-ror. Invoking this tool ends the agent’s current execution hop, preventing further reasoning or tool calls in the same turn. Agents may issue multiple parallel send_message calls within one step to send distinct ciphertexts. • decrypt_message (Ck) applies the decoder assigned to Ai. The call returns the correct plaintext iff k = π(i), otherwise returning an incorrect plaintext. Since π(i)̸ = i, no agent can decrypt its own ciphertext. Calls with 

k̸ = π(i) are recorded as decryption errors. • check_decryption ( ˆPi) verifies task comple-tion for Ai. If ˆPi = Pi, the tool returns accep-tance and completes the objective of Ai. If ˆPi̸ = Pi, the tool returns rejection and identi-fies Aπ(i), preventing infinite guessing loops and providing explicit routing feedback. 

Communication modes. We evaluate two exe-cution modes to isolate distinct aspects of LLM communication. In Strict Mode , agents bypass natural language generation for routing. They in-voke send_message using strictly typed actions: • decrypt_request (Ao, C o): requests decryp-tion of Co on behalf of Ao.• decrypt_response (Ao, P o): returns the de-crypted plaintext Po toward the owner Ao.• forward (Ao, C o, A next ): routes Co to the next hop Anext on the shortest path toward Aπ(o).The system formats these structured arguments into a standardized payload before delivery. This mode enforces a fixed message schema and isolates rout-ing errors from natural-language variation. In Free Text Mode , m is an arbitrary string. The agent selects a target neighbor Aj ∈ N (Ai) and sends a free-form natural-language query. This mode tests request formulation, routing instruc-tions, and response parsing without typed structural scaffolding. The complete system prompts for both modes are provided in Appendix B. For examples of two communication modes, see Appx. C. 

Execution protocol. Execution is synchronous and round-based. A central message queue stores all inter-agent messages. At the start of round T ,each agent receives messages sent by neighbors in round T − 1. Within a round, an execution hop processes the incoming queue, generates internal reasoning, and issues tool calls. The hop ends when the agent makes one or more send_message calls. 

3.3 Graph Topologies and Scaling Topologies. CypherMAS uses four predefined undirected graphs to isolate distinct structural ef-fects on routing. Chain and Ring explore commu-nication over growing distances: a Chain is a linear sequence where intermediate agents the diameter grows linearly with N . Ring closes the loop so that every agent has exactly two neighbors and, under maximum-distance decoder placement, all request paths have equal length N/ 2. Star and Bipartite 

bound the routing distance at two hops regardless of 

N : a Star routes all traffic through a single hub that must process high concurrent throughput, while a Bipartite graph restricts edges to connections be-tween two disjoint partitions.

3.4 Error Types Strict mode enables algorithmic analysis and deterministic classification of execution failures. 

CypherMAS diagnoses failure modes classifies into message- and decryption-centric. 

Message errors. Message errors count as invalid 

send_message calls. We record three types of er-ror: i. Misdirected cipher , where a ciphertext is sent via a non-shortest path to the decoder holder; ii. Misdirected decryption , where a plaintext is sent via a non-shortest path to the cypher owner; iii. 

Corrupted cipher , where the ciphertext argument differs from the exact received ciphertext. 

Decryption errors. Decryption errors count in-correct decrypt_message calls. We define two er-ror types: i. Wrong decryption , where an agent ap-plies decrypt_message to a ciphertext its decoder cannot decrypt; ii. Unsent decryption , where an agent decrypts a ciphertext but does not invoke 

send_message to return the plaintext.

5.1 Overall Performance Across LLMs Impact of Model Scale. Models below 27B pa-rameters fail at basic routing and tool use. Qwen3.5-4B and Qwen3.5-9B achieve SR below 0.10 on most topologies (Table 1). Above this threshold, performance rises sharply: Gemma4-31B reaches 1.00 in Strict mode on Star and Bipartite and ex-ceeds 0.85 on Chain and Ring. GLM-5.2 (753B total) attains near-perfect SR across configurations. 

GPT-OSS Failure The gpt-oss family illustrates an execution limitation rather than a reasoning deficit. Both gpt-oss models achieve near-zero SR across all configurations. Log analysis shows that they cannot issue multiple parallel send_message 

calls within one execution hop. When an agent re-ceives two or more actionable requests, it processes one and discards the rest, causing deadlocks. 5.2 Scalability and Communication Efficiency RQ1&2: Inference Cost and Graph Size. Mod-els that cannot resolve the protocol hit the 2,048-call cap before N reaches 25: Qwen3.5-4B on Ring exceeds 2,000 calls at N =9 and stays near the cap for N ≥14 . Low SR for small models therefore reflects unbounded looping, not gradual degrada-tion. On Star and Bipartite, routing distance is two hops regardless of N , and nearly all models stay close to the theoretical baseline. 6 Conclusion 

We introduced CypherMAS, a benchmark for rout-ing reliability and tool execution in fixed LLM multi-agent graphs. The results show that small models fail at both routing and tool invocation. Larger models maintain high success rates across chain, star, bipartite, and ring graphs, but success declines as the diameter of the graph increases. Successful CypherMAS runs may exceed the the-oretical minimum number of LLM calls and the effect of communication mode on this overhead is model-specific. The study enables the algorithmic quantitative evaluation of communication redun-dancy for multi-agent systems. Future work would introduce semantic noise and extend the methodol-ogy to more LLMs and graph topologies. 

Limitations 

CypherMAS isolates protocol execution from open domain reasoning. The results therefore apply to controlled routing behavior rather than full collab-orative problem solving. 

Synthetic messages. The benchmark uses de-terministic cipher routing. Real messages carry semantics, ambiguity, and variable formatting. Agents in production must parse instructions, ne-gotiate tasks, and recover from unclear requests. CypherMAS measures faithful execution of a known protocol. It does not measure performance when the protocol itself must be inferred from noisy natural language. 

Noiseless channels. All channels deliver mes-sages exactly as sent. No packet loss, delay, re-ordering, or corruption occurs. All measured errors come from the model. This enables attribution but excludes common infrastructure failures. The benchmark cannot assess recovery from timeouts, partial delivery, duplicate transport, or adversarial peers. 

Cost and latency. The evaluation counts LLM calls but does not optimize them. Large graphs require many calls. A single run can issue up to the 2,048-call cap, and every transmission is processed by its recipient in a later execution hop, so redun-dant traffic inflates token usage, latency, and cost (Appx. I, J). 

Fixed synchronous execution. The simulation runs in synchronous rounds. Agents process mes-sages and issue calls within a round. Production systems often run asynchronously. They face vari-able latency, concurrent external tools, preemption, and out of order events. Behavior under those con-ditions may differ. 

Limited scale and statistical power. The main experiments use graphs with up to 25 agents and three runs per configuration. This is enough to ex-pose clear trends but not enough to estimate small differences reliably. It also leaves open whether degradation beyond 25 agents is gradual or abrupt. 

Model coverage. The study evaluates eleven open weight models. It does not include proprietary frontier models. By design, CypherMAS implies many LLM calls for large topologies resulting in multiple paid API calls.

C Message Formats 

Both modes deliver messages through the same 

send_message tool and differ only in the argu-ments the model produces. Figure 9 shows one tool call per mode for the same situation: routing Bob’s ciphertext through his neighbor Alice toward its decoder holder Carol. In Strict mode the model fills a fixed schema. message_type instantiates one of the typed actions ( forward , decrypt_request ,

decrypt_response ); ciphertext_owner records on whose behalf the message travels, allowing inter-mediaries to forward ciphertexts they do not own; 

target_agent names the next hop on the shortest path; and ciphertext must reproduce the received string verbatim, which makes corrupted-cipher er-rors detectable at runtime. The harness formats these arguments into a standardized payload be-fore delivery; no natural-language generation is involved in routing. In Free Form mode the call reduces to a single 

content string. The model must express in prose everything the schema encodes in fields: the cipher-text, the identity of the decoder holder, the forward-ing request, and the request to return the plaintext. The receiving agent must parse this instruction, in-fer the destination, and act on it; failures therefore shift from schema compliance to request formula-tion and response parsing.

E Global Completion Rate 

SR in the main text measures per-agent success; the global completion criterion additionally requires 

all agents in a run to verify their plaintexts. Ta-ble 5 reports the resulting Global Completion Rate (GCR): the fraction of runs in which every agent completes its objective. GCR is strictly bounded by SR and is therefore substantially lower for most models: five of the eleven models never complete a single run despite nonzero per-agent SR in some configurations, and even the strongest models rarely complete Chain or Ring runs (GCR ≤ 0.33), where a single lost or stalled request suffices to fail the run. Complete runs concentrate on the bounded-diameter topolo-gies: GCR reaches 1.00 on Star and Bipartite only, and only for five models in Strict mode. The gap is largest for mid-tier models — Gemma4-31B com-pletes one third of Chain runs in Strict mode at SR 0.85, and Qwen3.8-27B raises Ring GCR from 0.00 to 0.33 in Free Text mode while its SR rises from 0.75 to 0.93. With three runs per configuration, GCR values are multiples of 1/3 and should be read as indicating presence rather than precise prob-ability of complete runs; SR remains the primary metric, and GCR quantifies system-level brittleness as N grows.

DeepSeek-V4-Flash on Ring issues more transmis-sions in Free Text than in Strict mode (691 vs. 600) yet costs less ($0.27 vs. $0.45), because its Strict-mode runs carry more input tokens per transmis-sion (10.5K vs. 6.2K); GLM-5.2 on Chain shows the converse, with transmissions and cost falling together (1,248 →573, $12.80 →$2.45). Raw cost must be read jointly with SR, since failed runs terminate early and are correspondingly cheap: the gpt-oss models are the least expensive in every configuration precisely because they dead-lock. Conversely, the two most expensive con-figurations in the study — GLM-5.2 on Chain in Strict mode ($12.80) and Qwen3.6-27B on Chain and Ring in Free Text ($12.67, $12.59) — are ex-actly the redundancy pathologies: transmission re-dundancy translates directly into monetary cost.
