# BRAG: Bayesian Retrieval Augmented Generation 

Javier Movellan , Manjot Bilkhu , Young-mok Jung , Sirajul Salekin , Henry Tran , Zhao Huang 

Apple Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) ranks memory chunks by semantic similarity to the query. Many methods improve on this by learning from feedback on what was retrieved, but each is a separate heuristic, with no account of when it is the right choice. We introduce BRAG, a generative model of retrieval with use. Each chunk generates the queries it answers from a mixture of von Mises–Fisher components anchored at its original embedding, and each chunk has a latent utility that drifts over time. The model gives the optimal inference, a closed-form filter that stores a few keys and two scalars per chunk and updates them online from the labels of a curator, and it frames retrieval as a control problem. It also shows when existing methods are optimal. Query averaging, Rocchio-style feedback and frequency reranking are special cases with fixed parameters, NUDGE is the case of one key per chunk with no dynamics, and LADER the limit of one key per past query. On two public long-term memory benchmarks, BRAG matches these methods on queries asked before, as expected, because when nothing changes over time the assumptions behind these special cases hold. On harder problems, where a new chunk makes an old one obsolete, BRAG outscores them. They all stay at plain-search level (Recall@5 0.67–0.71), while BRAG retrieves the new chunk 0.80–0.86 of the time. We also ran BRAG on three of NUDGE’s seven text benchmarks, and it matches or beats NUDGE on all three. On nine months of a personal agent’s memory (20,640 chunks), BRAG raises Recall@5 on held-out queries from 0.75 to 0.98 and Recall@1 from 0.44 to 0.97, with little change on chunks never queried.

# 1 Introduction 

AI agents operating over months or years accumulate ever-growing collections of conversations, documents, code, and other artifacts. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) systems (Lewis et al., 2020) index these artifacts by semantic similarity, so a query retrieves the chunks whose embeddings lie closest to it. But similarity is not what matters to the consumer of the retrieved content. What matters is whether a chunk holds the information the query asks for, and that changes with use. Some chunks are asked about again and again, and others go stale when newer chunks replace them. Libraries solved an analogous problem with two independent organizing systems. Access priority migrates books toward or away from the front shelf according to anticipated demand, and semantic organization 

shelves related books together. Bayesian models of borrowing history have long been used to predict the first (Burrell, 1985), and the same class of models has been shown to explain classical phenomena of human memory such as forgetting curves, learning curves and spacing effects (Anderson & Schooler, 1991). Building on this tradition, we introduce BRAG, a generative model of retrieval with use. Each query is generated by one chunk, the one holding the information it asks for. Each chunk has a time-dependent latent utility ,the probability that the query at time t is generated by it. The per-chunk query distribution is also a latent variable, parameterized by a mixture of von Mises–Fisher components with a prior centered on the chunk’s original embedding. Beliefs over utility are Beta distributions, updated online as evidence accumulates and relaxed toward a low-utility, high-uncertainty equilibrium in the absence of new evidence. The evidence comes from labels. Each query retrieves a set of chunks, and a label says which of them, if any, contains the information asked for. The label can come from the user’s behavior after the retrieval or 1arXiv:2611.31656v1 [cs.AI] 23 Nov 2026 from a curator LLM that checks the retrieved chunks. In BRAG a chunk’s semantic embedding is not a fixed address but a parameter of the distribution of queries the chunk generates, and that distribution is learned from use. The resulting filter has small memory and computational cost per chunk and is linear in corpus size, so it scales to production RAG systems. Retrieval is formulated as an optimal control problem given the posterior distribution over utility conditioned on the history of retrieval and the observed query. Many methods already learn from this kind of feedback, such as averaging past queries into a chunk’s vector, Rocchio’s relevance feedback (Rocchio, 1971), reranking by frequency or recency, NUDGE (Zeighami et al., 2025), which moves each embedding toward the queries it answered, and LADER (Jin et al., 2023), which scores documents by a kernel density over their past queries. Each is a separate heuristic, with no account of when it is the right choice. BRAG provides that account. Under its generative model these methods are special cases with fixed parameters, which says when each is optimal and lets the parameters be learned instead. We make four contributions. (i) A generative model of retrieval with use, in which each chunk generates queries from a von Mises–Fisher mixture and has a utility with Ornstein–Uhlenbeck dynamics, giving opti-mality conditions for both inference and retrieval. (ii) A closed-form recursive filter for the utility beliefs and an online EM adaptation of each chunk’s keys, at the cost of a few keys and two scalars per chunk. (iii) A unifying account of feedback-based retrieval, with query averaging, Rocchio, frequency reranking, NUDGE (one key per chunk, no dynamics) and LADER (one key per past query, no dynamics) as special cases. (iv) An empirical evaluation on two public long-term memory benchmarks, on three of NUDGE’s seven text benchmarks, on the TripClick search logs that LADER was tested on, and on nine months of a personal agent’s memory. BRAG matches the special cases when nothing changes over time, and outscores them when a new chunk makes an old one obsolete, with Recall@5 of the new chunk 0.80–0.86 against 0.67–0.71 for every method without dynamics. Section 2 lays out the generative model, §3 derives the recursive filter, §4 develops the online key adaptation, and §5 describes retrieval as control. §6 evaluates BRAG on public benchmarks and a personal memory corpus, §7 discusses related work, and §9 concludes. 

# 2 The BRAG Generative Model 

Each query is generated by one chunk, the chunk that holds the information the query asks for. We define the utility of a chunk at time t as the probability that the query at time t is generated by it. Time steps are taken small enough that at most one chunk generates a query in a step. We represent the internal beliefs about the utility of a chunk using a Beta distribution, in the expectation–concentration parameterization (e, n ) of the standard Beta-Binomial conjugate model (Gelman et al., 2013), where e is the expected utility and n can be interpreted as the effective sample size behind it. For example Beta(0 .8, 10) represents the belief distribution carried by n = 10 observed trials, including prior pseudo-observations, with a rate of e = 0 .8, i.e., “eight hits in ten looks”. A large n indicates that e is a high precision estimate that one new observation barely moves, and a small n indicates that e is only a tentative estimate that a single observation can move substantially.

# 5 Retrieval as a Control Problem 

The filter of §3 and the key adaptation of §4 are state estimators. They track beliefs and geometry but do not decide what to surface, which is a control problem. > Case 2 (

β → 1). For β > 0 the controller optimizes over the long run and must balance exploitation against exploration, since a chunk with low expected utility but high uncertainty may be worth surfacing in order to learn about it. As β → 1, posterior sampling (Thompson, 1933; Russo et al., 2018) is the standard approximation. 

> Case 3 (

0 < β < 1). For intermediate discount factors, approximately optimal control methods such as reinforcement learning can learn the retrieval policy, with the filter’s belief state and hit weights as the state. We evaluate only the greedy case (Case 1) here. 

# 6 Experiments 

We evaluate BRAG in four settings, retrieval of information accumulated between a user and a personal AI agent (§6.1), two public long-term memory benchmarks with many users that include scenarios in which new information makes old information obsolete (§6.2), three of NUDGE’s seven text benchmarks run with its code and splits (§6.3), and the benchmark of real search logs that LADER was tested on (§6.4). 

6.1 Personal memory  

> Corpus and queries.

The corpus is a RAG database of 20,640 chunks drawn from 2,742 markdown documents accumulated over 9 months of daily interaction between a user and a personal AI agent. The agent reviewed the transcript of the most recent ten weeks and selected 289 queries representative of common information needs, each with its correct target chunk. For every query it then wrote four additional queries targeting the same chunk. Because a chunk can be relevant to more than one topic, the five queries for a chunk are not only rewordings but may be distinct ways of asking about different topics.  

> Protocol.

For each chunk, one of its five queries is held out and never used for training. The remaining 1,156 queries are shuffled and spread over 44 simulated days. Each day the system retrieves chunks for that day’s queries, and the curator labels them. In all our experiments only positive labels are applied. A chunk marked as containing the information asked for takes the update step, and every other chunk takes only the predict step. A label is applied only if the labeled chunk is within the top 100 retrieved by that condition at the time of the query. This matches how the curator works in our deployed system. When no chunk among the top κ retrieved holds the answer, it widens the search up to the top 100 before giving up, so a chunk outside the top 100 can never be labeled. At the end of each day the labels are applied and the keys and beliefs updated (§3–§4). After day 44 we score the 281 held-out queries that have a correct chunk. Each condition is trained in its own run. We ran 14 runs per condition, each with a different shuffle, the same shuffles for every condition. Intervals are 95% confidence intervals for paired differences, from a query-level bootstrap. 6.2 Public benchmarks  

> Datasets.

LongMemEval-M (Wu et al., 2025) is a benchmark for long-term memory in chat assistants, with long histories of user–assistant sessions, and we use its 301 test questions. Its knowledge-update questions supply a changing-facts scenario, in which a fact is stated and later restated with a new value. PersonaMem-v2 (Jiang et al., 2025) is a benchmark for personalization, in which simulated users talk with an assistant across many sessions while their preferences change. We use 200 test personas and 3,288 test queries. Its 

forget scenario states a preference, and the user later asks the assistant to forget it.  

> Scenarios.

Asked again. A request that was asked and labeled before is asked again in new words, scored at the fifth ask. Never asked. A request with no feedback history. This is a control condition that tests whether learning on other chunks hurts retrieval for new chunks. Only PersonaMem-v2 has this case, since each LongMemEval-M request has its own history, which feedback on other requests cannot reach. Update arrives later (LongMemEval-M). The old value is learned from two queries, then a new value arrives that makes it obsolete. Forget request arrives later (PersonaMem-v2). A chunk with a fact is followed by a chunk asking to forget it, and the task is to retrieve the request. Both changing-facts scenarios are the same situation, in which a new chunk makes an old chunk obsolete. Labels come from gold evidence and are applied only to chunks plain search found in its top 10.

> Results.

Table 2 gives the results. On queries asked again, every feedback method, BRAG included, lifts Re-call@5 well above plain search (0.697 to about 0.858 on LongMemEval-M, 0.426 to about 0.66 on PersonaMem-v2), and they are within a few thousandths of each other. This is expected as these methods are special cases of BRAG with fixed parameters (§7), and when facts do not change the gain comes from moving keys toward past queries, which all of them do. On chunks never queried before, every learning method falls below plain search (0.444), and BRAG+’s gate recovers most of the loss (0.424). When a new chunk makes an old one obsolete, every method that learns only from past feedback stays at plain-search level (0.711 and 0.669–0.673), because past feedback says nothing about a chunk that just arrived. Only BRAG’s utility dynamics lift the new chunk, to 0.860 and 0.801 (0.851 and 0.850 for BRAG+).  

> Answer accuracy.

To test answers as well as retrieval, we gave the same LLM the chunks retrieved by plain search or by BRAG and graded its answers with LongMemEval’s official judge prompt. Accuracy rose from 0.552 to 0.702 on LongMemEval-M ( +0 .150 [+0 .118 , +0 .183 ]) and from 0.457 to 0.553 on PersonaMem-v2 (+0 .096 [+0 .086 , +0 .107 ]), and fell by 0.078 on never-asked queries.  

> Label noise.

The labels above come from gold evidence, but a deployed curator makes mistakes. It can mark a wrong chunk as useful or miss a useful one. With 30% extra wrong labels, BRAG’s Recall@5 on PersonaMem-v2 falls by only 0.002, and with 30% of labels missing, by 0.032. The labels above are also taken from plain search’s top 10. When each method learns only from its own top 10, a target it never retrieves gets no label, and BRAG’s Recall@5 falls by 0.053 on PersonaMem-v2 and not at all on LongMemEval-M. Appendix A.1 repeats the comparison with labels from an LLM curator, where every feedback method loses about the same.

6.4 Comparison with LADER 

TripClick (Rekabsaz et al., 2021) holds the click logs of a health web search engine, with 1.5 million medical abstracts and test queries grouped by how often they were asked (HEAD, TORSO and TAIL). We used LADER’s relevance labels and its training queries as feedback. Both methods start from a dense retriever trained on TripClick with LADER’s recipe, LADER on its inner-product version and BRAG+ on a cosine version trained the same way. LADER’s training-query neighbours were found by cosine rather than inner product and its softmax temperatures were set on validation, since with the published setting (inner product, temperature 1) its gains did not reproduce on our retriever. All settings were chosen on validation and the test split was read once. With the 20-candidate read BRAG uses in deployment, BRAG+ and LADER are within 0.014 NDCG@10 (0.311 / 0.303 / 0.308 vs 0.306 / 0.316 / 0.322 on HEAD / TORSO / TAIL). With 100 or more candidates LADER leads by 0.02–0.04, while BRAG+ gains little beyond 20. 

# 7 Related Work  

> Stochastic models of demand and memory.

The question of which stored items deserve fast access has a long quantitative history outside machine learning. Operations-research models of library circulation treat each book’s loan history as a Poisson process whose rate is itself uncertain and decays with age (Morse, 1968; Burrell & Cane, 1982; Burrell, 1985). A Gamma prior over the rate, updated by observed loans and aged by an obsolescence factor, is the closest ancestor of our per-chunk Beta belief with OU relaxation. The same rational-analysis argument was carried into cognitive science by Anderson & Milson (1989); Anderson & Schooler (1991), who showed that a memory that estimates each trace’s future need probability from its history of use reproduces the classical forgetting, learning, and spacing curves, and this estimate is the base-level activation of ACT-R. Bayesian accounts of recognition memory (Shiffrin & Steyvers, 1997) and learned spaced-repetition schedulers that regress a half-life of recall from practice history (Settles & Meeder, 2016; Mozer et al., 2009) share the same structure. Each estimates a latent, decaying probability of future use online from sparse binary outcomes. Cache replacement in computer systems solves the identical allocation problem with point statistics (recency, frequency, or a learned score, Megiddo & Modha, 2003; Liu et al., 2020) rather than a posterior. BRAG keeps both the estimate and its confidence, (e, n ), which is what allows a single observation to move a tentative belief far and a firm one little, and which the retrieval policies of §5 consume. In addition it provides a principle framework for policies that optimize long term exploration/exploitation tradeoffs.  

> Assumed-density filtering and dynamic bandits.

Projecting a non-conjugate posterior back onto a tractable family by moment matching is the assumed-density filtering of West & Harrison (1997); Minka (2001), and the specific Beta ↔logit-normal round trip we use for the predict step is the Laplace bridge (MacKay, 1998; Hobbhahn et al., 2022). On the decision side, choosing κ chunks per period from a corpus whose utilities drift is a restless (Whittle, 1988), combinatorial (Kveton et al., 2014; Wang & Chen, 2018) bandit with 8the query as context. The index policies of Gittins (1979) are standard solutions, and posterior sampling (Thompson, 1933; Russo et al., 2018) are the standard approximations. Beta–Bernoulli Thompson sampling with decaying evidence has been studied for exactly this non-stationary setting (Gupta et al., 2011; Chapelle & Li, 2011; Li et al., 2010). Our contribution on this side is not a new policy but the filter that produces the belief state those policies require, coupled across arms through the corpus-wide responsibility equation 4. The experiments use the greedy policy, and we leave exploration to future work.  

> RAG systems for agent memory retrieval.

Agent memory systems (Park et al., 2023; Packer et al., 2023; Zhong et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2025; Chhikara et al., 2025) decide what to store and how to organize it, as summaries, user profiles, or linked and rewritten notes. BRAG works at a different layer. It can run on top of any of them that retrieves stored items by embedding search, and it learns from use which items to bring back. Within that layer, learning from what happens after retrieval is the classical relevance-feedback problem. Rocchio’s rule (Rocchio, 1971) moves the query vector toward documents marked relevant. Our key update equation 6 is its dual, moving the document keys toward the queries they answered and keeping that move in the index. Click-based learning to rank (Joachims, 2002; Joachims et al., 2017) trains one shared ranker offline from logged interactions, correcting for the bias that only items shown receive feedback, but leaves each document’s representation untouched. Two methods adapt the index itself and are the closest to BRAG. NUDGE (Zeighami et al., 2025) moves each chunk’s embedding a bounded step toward the sum of the training queries it answered, with one step size chosen on validation, offline. In BRAG’s model this is key adaptation with one key per chunk ( K = 1 ) and an infinite utility lifetime, where the bound on the step plays the role of the prior on the key, a hard constraint where BRAG has a soft pull. BRAG’s step instead grows with the evidence behind each chunk. LADER (Jin et al., 2023) scores a document by its similarity plus a kernel density over the past queries that retrieved it, with counts that never decay and a hand-set weight. In BRAG’s model this is the limit of one key per past query ( K → ∞ ), with an infinite lifetime and no prior. BRAG lies between the two, with up to K keys per chunk, added only when the spread of its queries justifies it, concentrations and a prior learned from data, and a utility belief that fades when a chunk goes unused, which is what lets it retrieve a new fact that makes an old one obsolete (§6.2). ERM (Hu et al., 2026) also adapts the index. It expands each query with LLM-generated text and checks whether the expanded query retrieves a correct document. When it does, the expansion sentences that raised a retrieved document’s similarity to the original query are added to that document’s key. In BRAG’s model this is feedback-gated key adaptation with one key per chunk and no utility dynamics. The keys move toward generated text rather than toward the queries themselves. A separate line of work judges retrieved passages with an LLM at answer time. Self-RAG (Asai et al., 2024) and CRAG (Yan et al., 2024) critique and re-retrieve, and RankGPT (Sun et al., 2023) reranks. Each judgment is used once and discarded, and the index stays the same. Their judgment, whether a passage contains the information asked for, is the label BRAG learns from, so BRAG can serve as the memory under such loops, and its ranking needs no LLM call at query time. The estimation machinery is standard. It uses mixtures of von Mises–Fisher distributions (Banerjee et al., 2005; Mardia & Jupp, 2000) fit by EM, in its incremental (Neal & Hinton, 1998) and online (Cappé & Moulines, 2009) forms.

# 9 Discussion 

We presented BRAG, a generative model of retrieval with use, and the inference and control methods that follow from it. Each query is generated by one chunk, the chunk that holds the information the query asks for. Each chunk has a latent utility , the probability that the query at time t is generated by it, and a latent distribution over the queries it generates. The utility evolves under a minimalist model, a discrete-time Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process on its logit, so that in the absence of evidence it relaxes to a state of low expected utility and high uncertainty. Labels saying which retrieved chunk contains the information asked 10 for update the utility beliefs. Through the Laplace bridge between Beta and logit-Gaussian distributions, the filter propagates the beliefs directly in the Beta family, with intuitive, closed-form updates. The query distribution of each chunk is a mixture of vMF components. The standard RAG embedding is the prior on it, and the keys are updated by EM as labels arrive. Retrieval is then a control problem over the filter’s belief state, and the experiments use its greedy solution. BRAG defines a family of algorithms that covers previous heuristic methods as special cases. Query averaging, Rocchio-style feedback, frequency reranking, NUDGE and LADER are members of the family with fixed parameters and no utility dynamics. This helps explain when they work and when they stop working. When nothing changes over time their assumptions hold, and on queries asked before BRAG matches them on two public long-term memory benchmarks. When a new chunk makes an old one obsolete, every method without dynamics stays at plain-search level, while BRAG retrieves the new chunk 0.80–0.86 of the time against 0.67–0.71. We ran three of NUDGE’s seven text benchmarks, and BRAG matches or beats NUDGE on all three. On nine months of a personal agent’s memory, BRAG raises Recall@1 on held-out queries from 0.44 to 0.97, with little change on chunks never queried. The approach is easy to implement in standard vector databases, like SQLite, with all parameters and belief distributions stored in the database itself. The computational and memory cost is a few keys and two scalars per chunk, linear in the number of chunks, so it scales to production databases. BRAG is useful only if there are usage patterns to learn. A chunk used only once in the lifetime of the database gives the model nothing to learn from. NUDGE reports the same condition for its own method, with no gain on a benchmark where test queries never reuse a training answer. In our personal-memory experiment, chunks asked about four times over 44 days benefited substantially, while chunks never queried were essentially unaffected (§6). Several extensions are natural and left to future work. First, richer utility dynamics that can be learned from data, in particular the human-memory models closely related to BRAG whose dynamics are known to fit classical memory effects. Second, the controller. The policy here is greedy, and stronger policies could be learned with reinforcement learning, and the controller could also retrieve chunks of high expected utility given the ongoing conversation and add them to the context pre-emptively. Third, continuous-time versions of the model, which would allow on-demand adaptation rather than adaptation at fixed intervals.

# B Implementation details 

B.1 Database 

The database is implemented in SQLite with the sqlite-vec extension for approximate nearest-neighbor search. The core table ( memory_chunks ) stores 20,640 chunks drawn from 2,742 source documents, each with its text, source path, and topic metadata. A companion FTS5 full-text index ( memory_fts ) enables keyword search. Base embeddings are stored in a vec0 virtual table ( memory_vecs ) for cosine-similarity retrieval. BRAG adds three structures on top of this RAG baseline: 1. A chunk_posterior table holding each chunk’s Beta posterior parameters (e, n ), last-update timestamp, and class membership. 2. A primer_key_mode table storing K=2 adapted key components per chunk, each with a mixture weight 

ω. Both components are free to rotate via EM; neither is clamped at the base embedding. The original base embedding k0 is preserved in a separate column ( k0 ) for rollback. 3. A second vec0 index ( primer_keys_vec ) over the adapted keys (one row per chunk ×mode, 41,280 total), giving BRAG its own retrieval surface distinct from the RAG baseline. This yields three keys per chunk: the base embedding k0 (stored in memory_vecs , used for RAG retrieval) and two adapted keys k1, k 2 (stored in primer_keys_vec , used for BRAG retrieval). The retrieval API supports three modes: base (search k0 only — pure RAG), adapted (search k1, k 2 only — BRAG), and all (search all three keys, merge results). 

B.2 Embeddings 

All embeddings use nomic-embed-text-v1.5 (sentence-transformers), producing 768-dimensional L2-normalized vectors. Document embeddings use the prefix search_document: ; query embeddings use search_query: .The embedding model runs locally (no API calls). Chunks are created by a sliding-window chunker (300 words, 50-word overlap). Chunk identifiers are the first 12 hex characters of the MD5 hash of the source path and text.

> Candidate generation.

Given a query embedding q, retrieve the k=100 nearest neighbors by cosine distance. For RAG, this searches memory_vecs (one base embedding per chunk). For BRAG, this searches primer_-keys_vec (two adapted key components per chunk). B.4 Daily pass 

At the end of each simulated day, the following operations are performed: 1. Write labeled data. Each query from that day is written to a JSONL file with its good/bad chunk labels, query embedding, and timestamp. 2. Update beliefs. For every tracked chunk, the temporal decay is applied: e ← A·e+(1 −A)· ¯e, A = a∆t,where ∆t is days since last update. Then for each labeled chunk, an exact conjugate Bernoulli update is applied: good → (c=1 , n ′=1) , bad → (c=0 , n ′=1) . In the simulation, belief updates run as labels_-only ticks (the query’s retrieved set is empty), so the responsibility machinery ( γ, Wt) is bypassed and only the labeled evidence applies. 3. Rotate keys. For each chunk with at least one good-labeled query, batch EM fits a K=2 von Mises– Fisher mixture over the pool of all good query vectors accumulated across all days (not just today), plus one copy of the base embedding as prior. 20 EM iterations, no convergence test. Mode 0 is canonically reordered by weight after each fit to prevent label-switching across days. 4. Reindex. The updated keys are written to primer_keys_vec so subsequent retrievals use the new geometry.

B.6 Curation: how labels are produced 

Each query logged during normal use records the query text, its embedding, and the retrieved chunks with their cosine scores. A judge (human or agent) then determines, for each retrieved chunk, whether it contained the information the query was asking for. Chunks judged useful are labeled good ; those not useful are labeled 

bad . Ambiguous cases are abstained and enter neither category. Good labels feed both belief updates and 17 key adaptation; bad labels feed belief updates only (keys are never pushed away from bad queries). In the simulation, labels were produced by an LLM judge (Opus 4.6) without human verification.

# E Checking the approximations 

The inference of §3 uses three approximations: a posterior factorized across chunks, a Beta projec- tion, and the Laplace bridge between the logit-Gaussian of the OU dynamics and the Beta belief. To measure their cost we simulated data from BRAG’s own generative model (30 or 50 chunks, two keys each, 200 steps, 5 seeds, 55 runs) and compared BRAG with a joint particle filter over all chunks, with 65.5K particles (doubling to 131K moved posterior means by less than 0.003). Because the exact posterior is calibrated under the model, the distance between BRAG’s belief and that pos- terior measures both the approximation error and the loss of calibration. BRAG’s beliefs stay within 0.02–0.09 of the exact posterior. Factorizing across chunks adds a mean error of 0.002–0.007, at the particle filter’s own noise floor; the Beta projection adds 0.02 nats of KL divergence and the Laplace bridge another 0.01, with top-3 agreement with the exact filter of 0.975 (0.988 without the bridge). Recall@3 stays within ±0.015 of the exact filter. Taking the maximum over keys or averaging over them gives the same Recall@3 ( −0.004 ± 0.008 ); on PersonaMem-v2, replacing the nearest key with the full mixture changes Recall@5 by −0.003 [−0.004 , −0.001 ]. 20
