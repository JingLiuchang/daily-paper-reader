Abstract
Large language model (LLM) agents are evolving from passive chatbots into autonomous systems that operate
continuously in dynamic environments. Such agents need not only solve current tasks but also learn from
past interactions to improve future performance. However, existing streaming benchmarks often rely on short
task sequences or largely independent tasks, providing limited evidence of whether agents can effectively
reuse experience when later tasks depend on earlier outcomes. To address this gap, we introduce EvoLink-
Bench, a benchmark for evaluating self-evolving agents in long-horizon, interdependent task streams. It
covers daily office work, multi-turn dialogue, and AutoResearch-style workflows, with persistent workspaces
and dependencies across tasks. The benchmark includes two complementary levels: L1, which evaluates
the acquisition and revision of language-expressible knowledge, and L2, which assesses the development of
task-level capabilities through execution and feedback. Its evaluation further distinguishes within-task recovery,
persistent-state updates, and experience reuse in later tasks. We evaluate five agent frameworks across three

Introduction
Large language model (LLM) agents are rapidly evolving from stateless problem solvers into interactive, lifelong
assistants (Wang et al., 2024a; Xi et al., 2023). In real-world deployments, agents are expected to do more than answer
isolated queries; they need to assist users continuously over extended periods. During this process, interactions can
generate valuable experience, such as user preferences revealed through dialogue, corrections to tool usage, or newly
created task artifacts. This calls for self-evolving agents that can not only complete immediate tasks but also learn from
past interactions to improve future decision-making (Wang et al., 2023; Majumder et al., 2024). The ability to learn
continuously during service is therefore a key requirement for practical self-evolving agents.
Such continuous deployment creates a complex and highly interconnected environment. Unlike a sequence of inde-
pendent tasks, the user’s history, the external environment, and the agent’s workspace may all change over time. For
example, an initial data analysis task may produce a table that serves as the basis for a subsequent strategic decision,
while an automated research workflow may generate code and checkpoints that inform a later experimental hypothesis
(Lu et al., 2024). Moreover, later tasks may challenge earlier assumptions, require the agent to revisit a capability after
a long interval, or introduce unrelated tasks that compete for its attention. To operate effectively in such environments,
agents must not only retain and reuse relevant experience but also revise outdated knowledge and manage interference,
while maintaining the consistency of the persistent workspace on which future tasks depend.
As research on self-evolving agents continues to advance, evaluating their capabilities in realistic continual-service
settings has become increasingly important. Existing benchmarks cover individual agent capabilities and application-
specific settings (Yehudai et al., 2026), but address only parts of this broader problem. Short streaming benchmarks
typically use repeated tasks to evaluate local transfer and reflection (Jiang et al., 2026). Longer task streams can reveal
experience accumulation and cross-domain interference, but are often constructed by concatenating independently
specified tasks, without requiring later tasks to depend on earlier outputs or decisions (Wei et al., 2025; Yan et al., 2026).
Meanwhile, some stateful interactive benchmarks preserve tools and world states, but focus on narrower scenarios with
task-specific protocols (Trivedi et al., 2024; Yao et al., 2024; Cai et al., 2025; Fu et al., 2026). As a result, existing
benchmarks provide limited insight into whether agents can continuously improve through experience across diverse,
long-running interactions, particularly when later tasks depend on earlier decisions and artifacts.
To address this gap, we introduce EvoLink-Bench, a benchmark designed to evaluate self-evolving agents in long-
horizon, interdependent task streams. Figure 1 illustrates its overall design, including two evolution levels, a persistent
task environment, and a unified evaluation protocol. Unlike benchmarks that evaluate isolated tasks or simply
concatenate independent examples, EvoLink-Bench organizes tasks into persistent streams, where user context and
workspace artifacts carry across interactions. It covers daily office work, multi-turn dialogue, software and data tasks,
and automated research workflows. More importantly, tasks are connected through explicit dependencies: later tasks
may require agents to recall earlier facts and decisions or reuse previously generated code, data, and experimental
results. By varying the intervals between related tasks and introducing intervening tasks, the benchmark examines
whether agents can reuse and transfer experience, revise outdated knowledge, and handle interference over time.
EvoLink-Bench evaluates self-evolution at two complementary levels. L1 focuses on knowledge evolution, examining
whether agents can acquire, retain, apply, and revise information that can be expressed in language, such as facts,
preferences, and procedural rules. L2 focuses on capability evolution, examining whether agents can develop reusable
task-solving procedures through execution and feedback, including coding, data analysis, and research workflows. This
distinction captures two different aspects of learning: knowing what to remember and improving how to perform tasks.
To evaluate these processes, we introduce three persistence conditions: Reset, Frozen, and Evolving, which differ in
whether previous state is retained and whether post-turn updates are enabled. With bounded attempts, our evaluation
further separates within-task recovery from changes to persistent agent state and their potential benefits on later probe

with improved performance on subsequent tasks.
We evaluate five agent frameworks across three backbone configurations under these persistence conditions. The results
reveal that self-evolution is not a uniform source of performance gains: its effects depend on the agent framework,
the level of evolution, and the task distribution. In particular, knowledge evolution and capability evolution exhibit
different performance patterns, suggesting that improvements in retaining information do not necessarily translate into
better task-solving capabilities. Moreover, recovering from failure through retries is different from learning across
tasks, and changes to persistent state do not necessarily lead to effective experience reuse. These findings expose an
important gap between accumulating experience and turning it into lasting improvements, highlighting the need for
more comprehensive evaluation of self-evolving agents. Our main contributions are summarized as follows:
❶Evaluation Paradigm. We establish an evaluation paradigm for self-evolving agents in continuous-service settings,
distinguishing within-task recovery, persistent-state updates, and cross-task experience reuse. This provides a
systematic way to examine whether improvements extend beyond the current task.
❷Two-Level Benchmark. We introduce EvoLink-Bench, featuring two complementary levels of evolution: L1
for knowledge evolution and L2 for capability evolution. Its long-horizon task streams incorporate explicit task
dependencies, persistent artifacts, varying time intervals, and interference, covering diverse real-world workflows.
❸Empirical Findings. Through a comprehensive evaluation of agent frameworks under different persistence con-
ditions, we show that self-evolution benefits are highly dependent on the agent and task setting. In particular,
persistent-state updates do not necessarily lead to effective downstream reuse, and knowledge-level improvements
do not guarantee capability-level gains.

2.1
Continual Learning
Continual learning investigates how systems assimilate sequential experiences while preserving previously acquired
knowledge and transferring it to novel problems (Wang et al., 2024b). The central challenge lies in the stability-
plasticity dilemma: integrating new capabilities under limited resources without catastrophically forgetting older
ones. Representative approaches typically address this by either protecting critical parameters that support earlier
tasks, such as in elastic weight consolidation (Kirkpatrick et al., 2017), or by actively revisiting stored experiences
during subsequent learning phases (Rolnick et al., 2019). These mechanisms tackle interference from different angles,
establishing retention, adaptation, and transfer as complementary criteria for assessing a lifelong learner. The evaluation
protocols in continual learning largely dictate which learning dynamics are exposed. Traditional settings, such as task-,
domain-, and class-incremental learning, vary the availability of task identity and the specific boundaries a model must

recognize (van de Ven et al., 2022). Furthermore, continual learning extends into reinforcement learning, where task
boundaries are absent and experience replay supports learning from ongoing interactions (Rolnick et al., 2019).
For large language models (LLMs), continual learning spans across pretraining, instruction tuning, and alignment.
Given the prohibitive costs of repeated training, there is a strong motivation for efficient adaptation strategies (Wu et al.,
2024b). At the agent level, learning can also manifest through persistent textual states. For instance, CLIN updates a
memory of causal abstractions across trials without modifying the underlying model parameters (Majumder et al., 2024).
Building upon these perspectives, EvoLink-Bench evaluates both explicit knowledge revision and reusable task-solving
behaviors within a persistent workspace. Its protocol rigorously disentangles within-task corrections, retained execution
states, and post-turn learning updates, allowing the specific outcomes of each mechanism to be examined independently.
2.2
Self-Evolving Agents
Self-evolving agents augment the traditional perception-reasoning-action loop by integrating persistent updates derived
from ongoing interactions. This experience-driven lifelong learning process is typically organized around explo-
ration, long-term memory management, skill acquisition, and knowledge internalization (Cai et al., 2025). Existing
methodologies primarily diverge in what specific information they choose to retain and revise.
An adjacent line of work improves the current output through inference-time refinement. Self-Refine alternates feedback
generation and revision using the same LLM, without additional model training (Madaan et al., 2023). This establishes
output refinement as a distinct evaluation target: improving the current answer does not by itself demonstrate transfer to
a subsequent task. EvoLink-Bench therefore separates within-turn retries, post-turn updates, and later probes.
Memory-oriented methods focus on preserving observations, trajectories, and failure lessons to inform future decisions.
For example, Reflexion converts task feedback into verbal reflections stored in an episodic memory for subsequent
trials (Shinn et al., 2023). Similarly, ExpRAG retrieves past task experiences, while ReMem integrates reasoning, task
execution, and memory refinement into a cohesive action-think-memory cycle (Wei et al., 2025). These operations
enable the agent to maintain a continuous thread of experience, linking newly observed feedback directly to immediate
reasoning and future memory retrieval. Skill-oriented methods, conversely, abstract experience into reusable procedural
knowledge or executable routines. Voyager maintains a dynamically expanding library of code-based skills, improving
candidate programs through environmental feedback, execution errors, and self-verification (Wang et al., 2023). Once
validated, these programs serve as foundational building blocks for future tasks. The ELL framework similarly distills
recurring patterns into skills that are refined for novel scenarios (Cai et al., 2025). AgentSkillOS further addresses the
organization of these libraries by utilizing capability-tree retrieval and composing skills into directed acyclic workflows
(Li et al., 2026a). Another prominent line of research focuses on evolving the agent’s harness, which includes prompts,
contextual instructions, toolsets, control logic, and verification procedures. ACE treats the agent’s context as an evolving
playbook, using generation, reflection, and curation to accumulate strategies through structured updates (Zhang et al.,
2025). HarnessBank pairs a task-execution agent with a separate "evolver" that diagnoses failures and systematically
generates, recombines, and screens candidate harnesses (Luo et al., 2026). At this level, experience fundamentally
alters how the agent plans and invokes tools across interactions.
Despite these significant advances, existing evaluations fail to fully capture the complexity of self-evolution in truly
persistent environments, where later tasks may depend on earlier decisions and artifacts. To bridge this gap and advance
research in this setting, we introduce EvoLink-Bench, a benchmark specifically designed to evaluate self-evolving
agents within long-horizon, interdependent task streams.
2.3
Self-Evolving Agent Benchmarks
Evaluating self-evolving agents requires measuring an agent’s trajectory of improvement rather than its static perfor-
mance. This introduces a fundamental challenge in benchmark design: how to rigorously assess the accumulation,
adaptation, and transfer of knowledge without confounding these effects with the agent’s baseline capabilities. To
address this, existing benchmarks must navigate an inherent trade-off between isolating specific learning mechanisms
and simulating realistic, long-horizon environments. Consequently, recent evaluation frameworks have approached this
challenge by prioritizing different dimensions of the learning process.
Long-term conversational memory provides a complementary evaluation perspective. LongMemEval distinguishes
information extraction, multi-session reasoning, temporal reasoning, knowledge updates, and abstention (Wu et al.,
2025). These abilities inform the diagnostic scope of L1, while our L2 setting additionally evaluates execution and
the reuse of task artifacts. Separately, agent-evaluation research emphasizes cost control, adequate holdout tasks, and
reproducible evaluation practices (Kapoor et al., 2024); we therefore report cohort scope and budgets.

First released in 2023, AgentBench evaluates multi-turn task execution across eight interactive environments (Liu et al.,
2023). It provides a reference for task-solving ability, whereas sequential learning benchmarks additionally examine
whether experience improves performance on later tasks.
One line of evaluation studies experience reuse through sequential tasks and feedback. StreamBench retrieves feedback-
selected examples for later tasks and tests robustness across five shuffled task orders (Wu et al., 2024a). LifelongA-
gentBench links database, operating-system, and knowledge-graph tasks through shared atomic skills and evaluates
replay of successful trajectories in a fixed sequence (Zheng et al., 2025). Its task environments are reset between
tasks, while prior trajectories can be retained as experience. In Table 1, shared skills therefore count as procedural
reuse rather than required dependencies on earlier task outputs; a fixed sequence alone does not count as controlled
variation of task order. SEA-Eval employs repeated and orthogonal tasks to gauge local gain and stability (Jiang et al.,
2026). Other examples include GDPevo and BenchTrace, which inject controlled success, failure, and noise signals
for reflection analysis (Zhou et al., 2026; Huang et al., 2026). These settings examine learning from prior experience,
while EvoLink-Bench also includes downstream tasks that require earlier facts, decisions, or artifacts. To capture
broader temporal dynamics, a second approach emphasizes stream length and cross-domain interference. Evo-Memory
and AgentStream create heterogeneous streams with a shared evolutionary state, enabling the study of accumulation,
forgetting, and domain switching (Wei et al., 2025; Yan et al., 2026). FinEvo-Bench investigates procedural experience
reuse across financial cases using interleaved streams and paired non-evolving controls (Deng et al., 2026). Recent
domain-specific benchmarks like EvoPathBench and ScienceClaw-Eval further examine the evolution process by
evaluating persistent program improvements and rule adaptations across scientific disciplines (Lin et al., 2026; Zhang
et al., 2026). However, the constituent tasks in these benchmarks remain largely independent; while an earlier episode
might provide a useful strategy, a later request is generally still solvable even if the earlier episode is entirely removed.
Moving beyond sequence length and domain shifts, a third body of work focuses on environmental realism by evaluating
persistent states across interactive sessions. AppWorld, τ-bench, BFCL Multi-Turn, and RealUserSim ground agents
in executable tools, dynamic states, and user feedback (Trivedi et al., 2024; Yao et al., 2024; UC Berkeley Gorilla
Team, 2024; Salesforce Research, 2026). Frameworks like ClawArena, CL-bench Life, StuLife, and Trainee-Bench
couple decisions through long timelines, schedules, and hidden information (Ji et al., 2026; Dou et al., 2026; Cai
et al., 2025; Fu et al., 2026). EvoArena introduces versioned software and preference chains (Xu et al., 2026), while
PAST-Bench tests cross-session memory and procedural reuse through matched persistence controls (Xue et al., 2026).
These settings successfully demonstrate the value of persistent states, yet their temporal structures are largely dictated
by scenario-specific sequences rather than explicit, cross-task evolutionary dependencies.
EvoLink-Bench builds upon these foundations by including interdependent streams in which historical context is
necessary for specific downstream tasks, alongside controlled variations in task order, spacing, and interference. As
highlighted in Table 1, EvoLink-Bench uniquely combines cross-task dependencies, controlled scheduling, knowl-
edge/rule revision, and procedural experience reuse. This combination enables a rigorous evaluation of whether agents
can effectively revise and reuse experience as interdependent tasks arrive under conditions of delay and interference.

Problem Formulation: Persistent Streaming Self-Evolution
Existing self-evolution benchmarks often evaluate tasks independently or process each stream item only once, abstracting
away the persistent and iterative dynamics of real-world service (Wu et al., 2024a; Jiang et al., 2026; Yan et al., 2026).
To more faithfully capture these dynamics in realistic deployment, our formulation models self-evolution as continual
service over a long-horizon task stream Q = (q1, . . . , qT ). Each stream may pose a different type of problem, such as a
user request, an autonomous-workflow objective, or an environment-triggered event. We curate task order and arrival
patterns so that later items may depend on earlier outputs and both task occurrence and dependency structures vary
across streams, exposing agents to realistic temporal dynamics and inter-task dependencies. Processing qt defines a
stream turn, within a turn, the agent may make several attempts, each comprising multiple interaction steps, before the
stream advances. Retrying qt is within-turn adaptation, whereas improved behavior on later items constitutes cross-turn
self-evolution. Within-turn attempts share state rather than replay the initial condition.

Hierarchical Evolution Task Construction
To diagnose what an agent actually improves during continual service, we separate self-evolution into two complemen-
tary levels. L1 (language-expressible factual evolution) evaluates whether an agent can acquire, retain, and reuse
6

explicit knowledge, including facts, preferences, constraints, and tool conventions. L2 (task-level capability evolution)
evaluates whether the agent can improve reusable problem-solving behavior through execution and feedback. This
separation reveals whether a method primarily strengthens memory and retrieval, task-solving competence, or both.
Each level covers multiple task categories and stream structures. Tasks of the same category may occur in consecutive
turns or recur after several intervening turns, and later tasks may depend on information, artifacts, or experience
produced earlier in the stream. Together, these variations test immediate acquisition, long-term retention, transfer, and
dependency-aware reuse under realistic arrival patterns. The temporal controls also address a challenge identified in
long-context evaluation: information being present in the input does not ensure that it is used reliably. Controlled
retrieval and question-answering experiments found sensitivity to the position of relevant information (Liu et al., 2024).
We therefore vary spacing and intervening work explicitly; these are diagnostic conditions, rather than an assumption
that the same positional effect must occur in every evaluated agent.
4.1
L1 Language-Expressible Factual Evolution
Task construction.
L1 scenario tasks are drawn from the real-source-grounded release of ClawArena (Ji et al., 2026).
We retain the original task instructions, workspace evidence, conversation histories, staged updates, and executable
success criteria. Each scenario defines a user role and a persistent information environment containing documents and
structured records. Requests require evidence retrieval, conflict resolution, classification, or artifact production under
scenario facts, user preferences, and standing constraints. Authoritative records coexist with incomplete summaries and
obsolete statements, requiring the agent to identify relevant evidence and determine which statements remain valid. The
original executable checks assess the resulting artifacts, with separate checks for preference adherence where specified.
We add domain-specific knowledge threads centered on procedural rules, including evidence-grading standards and
reporting conventions. Each rule specifies conditions of use and is disclosed before its applications. Application
requests provide cases and required outputs without restating the rule. Case sets vary evidence types, numerical
thresholds, and exception conditions. For example, an evidence-grading request presents findings supported by a
reproducible demonstration, a vendor advisory, an unverified claim, or static analysis; the agent must assign grades
using the previously disclosed standard. Further applications vary requests or extend earlier outputs under the same rule.
Each task specifies accessible evidence, permitted actions, and criteria for success. We verify evidence availability, the
consistency of environmental updates, and the order of disclosure and application. Knowledge-thread tasks are evaluated
for rule application, incorporation of corrections, recall accuracy, and output completeness. Reference specifications and
evaluation criteria are withheld from the agent; feedback on unsuccessful attempts is limited to observable deficiencies.
The distinction between corrected rules, temporary exceptions, and unavailable information mirrors LongMemEval’s
separate evaluation of knowledge updates and abstention (Wu et al., 2025). Here, these checks are embedded in
executable scenario requests, so evaluation focuses on whether the agent applies the appropriate rule in its output.

L1 comprises 637 turns across 23 persistent streams in nine domains, with 23–32 turns per stream. Figures 4

requests, and new evidence can require earlier conclusions to be revised. Independent distractors add unrelated questions
and temporary exceptions whose effects must remain confined to the current request.

some rules. Dispersed and long-gap schedules are the most common, making reuse after intervening work a recurring
requirement. Pre-disclosure probes test recognition of missing rules; temporary overrides test whether exceptions
remain local. Figure 4 shows that first application generally follows disclosure after several intervening requests, while
recall occurs later. Among the 44 threads with recall, the median disclosure-to-recall interval is 19 intervening turns,
reaching 25 in the longest case; the median correction-to-recall interval is six turns. Recall must preserve unchanged
clauses from the initial rule and incorporate subsequent amendments.

4.2
L2 Task-Level Capability Evolution
L2 asks whether experience from earlier tasks improves the agent on a new task that requires the same capability.
Each capability is practiced on several tasks and then measured on a probe, a final unseen task of that capability. A
stream controls what lies between practice and probe along four axes: how many turns intervene, which work fills
them, whether the probe moves to another benchmark, and whether a later task must build on files that an earlier task
produced. Machine learning engineering (MLE) tasks are central to L2 because they require an experimental loop of
data diagnosis, model choice, and tuning under a compute budget. MLE-bench similarly evaluates dataset preparation,
model training, and experimentation as components of ML engineering (Chan et al., 2024); our MLE tasks are drawn
from AIRS-Bench and MLGym, as specified below.
Tasks and capabilities.
Every turn is a native task from SRA-Bench (Su et al., 2026), τ 2-Bench (Barres et al.,
2025), SkillLearnBench (Zhong et al., 2026), SkillsBench (Li et al., 2026b), AIRS-Bench (Lupidi et al., 2026), or
MLGym (Nathani et al., 2025), with its original instructions, inputs, and evaluation. Programming tasks are scored by
unit tests and document and data-analysis tasks by the upstream tests on the produced files. Service tasks run in the
original τ 2 environment: the agent calls its tools and converses with its user simulator, and the final database state and
dialogue are checked against the task’s assertions. A task is admitted only if its upstream reference solution passes
this evaluation and a corrupted submission fails in an offline container. Each task carries a capability label for the
solution procedure it shares with other tasks, derived from source annotations such as SkillLearnBench task families,
SkillsBench categories, τ 2 workflow types, the libraries exercised by SRA tasks, and the problem type of MLE tasks.
Tasks of one capability differ in inputs and required outputs, so a probe rewards the procedure rather than a remembered
answer. Reference solutions, tests, and hidden labels remain outside the agent’s environment.
MLE turns use distinct AIRS-Bench and MLGym tasks, each evaluated on its full hidden test set within the upstream
time budget. Their turn score is the AIRS-Bench normalized score, which maps the native metric to 0 at the task’s
worst or baseline anchor and to 1 at the best reported result, so partial progress below the state of the art remains
visible. Because CPU and GPU tasks demand different experimental practice, they form separate streams: one with
CPU-feasible tasks and one dominated by GPU-class tasks.

Stream construction.
Each stream combines three capabilities with n distinct tasks each (n = 5; n = 3 in MLE
streams). The first two tasks of a capability are learning turns (the first task when n = 3), the last is the probe, and the
rest are application turns; the roles share the same evaluation and differ only in position. All turns of a stream share one
persistent workspace in which each task adds its own directory and earlier files remain available.
For capability c whose tasks occupy turns σ(c, 1) < · · · < σ(c, n), the probe gap counts the turns between the probe
and the previous task of the same capability:
gc = σ(c, n) −σ(c, n −1) −1.
(7)
Blocked streams place each capability contiguously (gc = 0) and test immediate reuse. Interleaved streams rotate the
capabilities (gc = 2) and test reuse under interference. Delayed streams keep the stream length but defer the probes to
the end in reverse order (gc ∈{0, 5, 10}) and test retention over increasing intervals. The intervening work is either
the same benchmark’s other capabilities or, in cross-benchmark streams, tasks with different tools, environments, and
output formats. In two cross-benchmark streams, a probe comes from another benchmark whose capability shares the
procedure, for example a SkillsBench financial-data task after SRA dataframe-analysis tasks, which tests transfer.
The dependency stream makes later MLE work depend on earlier artifacts. It splits three MLE tasks into a preparation
stage and the original task: building feature tables before house-price regression, cleaning a time series before
forecasting, and implementing a payoff-estimation harness before designing a Colonel Blotto strategy. The original task
starts in an empty directory; its data and the prepared artifact exist only in the preparation stage’s directory, so it cannot
proceed without that earlier turn. Reference checks assess preparation; unseen probes end each line.
Statistics.
L2 comprises 252 turns in 18 streams, drawn from 181 distinct tasks of 6 benchmarks and spanning 9
domains and 20 capability groups (Figure 6a). Ten intra-benchmark and five cross-benchmark streams have 15 turns
each; the CPU, CPU+GPU, and dependency streams have nine turns each and contribute 27 MLE turns. Of the 54
probes, 18 directly follow the previous task of their capability, 24 follow two intervening tasks, and 12 follow five or
ten; four probes test cross-benchmark transfer. Figure 6b shows one stream of each kind, and Figure 6c shows that tasks
range from a single function checked by unit tests to hour-long model training scored against the best reported result.
5
Experiments
We conduct a systematic evaluation of five agent frameworks across three backbone configurations and three persistence
conditions, guided by the following five research questions:
❶RQ1: How do the evaluated frameworks perform across the two evolution levels, L1 and L2?
❷RQ2: How are retained state and post-turn updates related to task performance?
❸RQ3: How much can agents recover from an unsuccessful submission through within-task retries?
❹RQ4: Do recorded changes to persistent state correspond to better performance on later probe tasks?
❺RQ5: How do the observed results vary across task domains and independent streams?
5.1
Experimental Setup
Backbones and agent frameworks.
We evaluate three backbone models: DeepSeek V4 Flash (DeepSeek AI, 2026b),
GLM-5.3 (Z.ai, 2026a), and GPT-6.1 Terra (OpenAI, 2026b). DeepSeek V4 Flash and GLM-5.3 are deployed locally,
while GPT-6.1 Terra is accessed through its API. Four agent frameworks are evaluated across all three configurations:
Hermes (Nous Research, 2026), Prime Agent (Karten et al., 2026), Adaptive Auto-Harness (Liu et al., 2026), and
OpenCode (Anomaly, 2026). The fifth framework is configuration-specific: DeepSeek Harness (DeepSeek AI, 2026a)
for DeepSeek V4 Flash, ZCode (Z.ai, 2026b) for GLM-5.3, and Codex (OpenAI, 2026a) for GPT-6.1 Terra. These
frameworks are evaluated within their respective configurations and are not directly interchangeable. Among the
evaluated frameworks, Hermes reviews and updates memory and skills, Prime Agent refines a persistent harness,
and Adaptive Auto-Harness updates prompts, skills, and memory. In the audited DeepSeek V4 Flash and GLM-5.3
configurations, OpenCode, DeepSeek Harness, and ZCode do not have an enabled native post-turn learner.
Evaluation cohorts and coverage.
Due to limited model-call budgets and execution time, we evaluate selected task
cohorts rather than running every agent framework on all benchmark streams. The evaluated cohorts also differ across
backbone configurations. For DeepSeek V4 Flash and GLM-5.3, the selected L1 panel contains 181 turns across 10
streams, while the L2 panel contains 144 turns across 10 streams. The L2 panel consists of 135 turns from the primary
intra-benchmark panel and nine CPU AutoResearch turns. For GPT-6.1 Terra, each framework–condition pair covers

Persistence conditions.
Reset reinitializes the workspace and agent state before each turn and disables explicit
post-turn updates. Frozen retains the permitted workspace and agent state but disables those updates. Evolving retains
state and enables the post-turn update procedure when the evaluated framework implements one. Thus, Evolving
describes an execution condition; it does not guarantee that an update was attempted, committed, or useful.
For an aggregate score M, we report two descriptive contrasts:
∆persist = MFrozen −MReset,
∆E−F = MEvolving −MFrozen.
(8)
The first contrast captures the observed difference associated with retaining state. The second compares the Evolving
and Frozen conditions. Neither contrast is interpreted as a causal estimate of a learning algorithm, particularly when the
evaluated integration has no native learner or when runtime revisions and budgets differ.
Scoring and reporting.
A turn’s reward rs,t ∈[0, 1] differs from its pass/fail result. We use the last recorded
submission, keep partial rewards, and set rewards to zero only for recorded execution errors or timeouts. The main
score is the mean reward across evaluated turns, multiplied by 100. We also report submission pass rates, paired stream
differences, saved-state changes, and final probe scores. Appendix A gives scoring and state rules.
Runtime provenance.
The DeepSeek V4 Flash and GLM-5.3 selections include retained and replacement runs across
several runtime revisions. The replacement scope was fixed before inspecting replacement scores, and all displayed
results were recomputed from the selected terminal records. However, the historical and replacement runs do not form a
certified equal-budget cohort, and repeated-run uncertainty is not available. We therefore treat the results as descriptive
benchmark observations rather than statistically controlled estimates.
This reporting boundary follows the methodological concern that agent accuracy should be assessed together with
execution cost and standardized evaluation conditions (Kapoor et al., 2024). MLE-bench also studies resource scaling
explicitly (Chan et al., 2024). Accordingly, the condition contrasts below describe the selected runs and do not establish
which framework would lead under a common compute or model-call budget.

(62.87%) and gains the most from later submissions (11.49 percentage points). The first result describes solving tasks
immediately; the second describes additional success after feedback. A final acceptance rate combines these two
behaviors and can hide their difference. However, retry gains alone cannot rank the quality of feedback use: frameworks
enter retries on different failed tasks and may spend different amounts of work on each attempt. We therefore report
initial acceptance and later recovery separately without equating larger recovery gains with greater efficiency.
The failure categories also need to be read together. Prime Agent has relatively few executions with three failed
submissions (9.64% for DeepSeek V4 Flash and 9.13% for GLM-5.3), but another 28.92% and 24.62% end without a
pass after fewer submissions. Looking only at three-submission failures would therefore give an incomplete picture of
reliability. The shorter runs record neither successful recovery nor use of the full retry allowance. They can arise from
early termination or an exhausted work budget, so the available number of submissions should be distinguished from
the number the framework actually completes. Keeping these outcomes separate prevents incomplete attempts from
being mistaken for successful error correction.

5.4.1
Trace-level illustrations of recovery and execution quality
Two matched task comparisons under DeepSeek V4 Flash Reset show why extra attempts may fail to repair a solution.
On L1 Stream 1, Turn 3, Hermes and OpenCode receive the same request for a vulnerability reproducer and start
from 51 identical workspace files. Hermes sets up the source path inside the delivered script, which passes the normal
invocation and receives reward 1.00. OpenCode tests its script only with a temporary PYTHONPATH=src setting; the
normal invocation cannot import the local package, and all three submissions fail the task check (reward 0.00). Its local
test therefore checks a different execution environment from the one used to evaluate the delivered script. Repeating
this check leaves the mismatch unresolved.
On L2 SRA-blocked Turn 15, DeepSeek Harness uses os.path.join to construct a log-file path and receives reward
1.00. Hermes joins the directory and filename as strings. Its tests use a directory ending in a slash, which hides the
defect; when the directory is logs, the output becomes logstask_func.log rather than logs/task_func.log. All
three submissions retain this defect, and the task receives partial reward 0.15. Here, the failed recovery concerns the test
case as well as the code: the test does not expose the input on which path construction fails. Both comparisons show
why attempts alone are insufficient: recovery also depends on testing the failing input and repairing the relevant code.
These cases illustrate failure mechanisms; they do not estimate how often those mechanisms occur across the benchmark.
Three successful retries in Appendix C.6 provide complementary examples: reading earlier accepted artifacts, keeping
a correct partial code repair, and revising a recommendation after retrieving a missing fact. None invokes a post-turn
learner between submissions. These examples show how agents use available experience to repair the current solution.

6
Discussion
6.1
Self-evolution should be viewed as a continuous process
The results suggest that self-evolution involves several related but distinct processes, rather than a single update
operation. An agent first needs to solve the current task, then identify useful experience from the interaction, and finally
apply that experience to later tasks. Within-task retries provide an opportunity to correct the current solution using
immediate feedback, while post-turn updates aim to preserve experience beyond the current task. However, neither
process alone demonstrates that the agent has improved its future behavior. Later tasks must test this improvement.
The retry results illustrate the difference between local correction and long-term learning. Across DeepSeek V4 Flash
and GLM-5.3, 24.75% and 27.63% of executions that entered a retry eventually passed. Although later submissions
increased the overall acceptance rates, most executions that required retries still failed to pass. This suggests that simply
allowing more attempts is not enough. Agents also need to identify why an attempt failed and determine which part of
the solution should be changed. The trace examples in Section 5.4 further show that repeated attempts may preserve the
same error when the agent fails to test the actual execution conditions or identify the relevant defect.
A similar distinction applies to post-turn updates. Changing persistent memory or skills shows that an agent has
recorded new information, but does not establish that the information will improve a later decision. The probe results in
Section 5.5 provide a further check: an advantage in the overall L2 score does not always appear on the final unseen
tasks. These observations suggest that future self-evolving agents should be designed around a complete learning
process: collect experience, identify useful lessons, update persistent knowledge, and verify its use in subsequent tasks.
Each step should have its own evaluation criteria. For example, failure analysis should examine whether the agent
identifies the actual cause of an unsuccessful task, memory updates should be checked for correctness and relevance,
and later tasks should test whether the saved experience leads to better decisions or execution.
This view also helps clarify the difference between methods that improve the current response and those that support
future tasks. Self-Refine focuses on revising the current output through feedback, whereas CLIN studies adaptation and
generalization through persistent textual memory (Madaan et al., 2023; Majumder et al., 2024). A useful direction is
to connect these two processes more closely, so that feedback from current execution can be converted into reusable
experience rather than being discarded when the task ends.
6.2
Knowledge evolution and capability evolution require different learning mechanisms
The differences between L1 and L2 show that self-evolution cannot be measured or improved through a single type
of memory. L1 focuses on information that can be expressed in language, such as facts, preferences, constraints, and
procedural rules. L2, in contrast, requires agents to improve their ability to complete tasks through execution, including
writing code, processing data, using tools, and working with existing artifacts. Although both levels involve learning
from previous experience, the information that needs to be retained and the way it should be reused are different.
The experimental rankings support this distinction. Under DeepSeek V4 Flash Evolving, Adaptive Auto-Harness
achieves the highest L1 score (92.15), while DeepSeek Harness leads L2 (80.63). A similar difference appears under
GPT-6.1 Terra: Prime Agent reaches 85.91 on L1 but only 37.15 on L2. Moreover, the Evolving–Frozen differences
can change direction across the two levels. For example, Prime Agent shows a negative difference on L1 (-6.74 points)

but a positive difference on L2 (+14.77 points) under DeepSeek V4 Flash. These results do not imply that one level is
inherently more difficult than the other, since they contain different tasks. Instead, they show that an agent’s ability to
retain explicit knowledge does not necessarily reflect its ability to develop reusable task-solving skills.
This distinction offers a useful direction for future method design. For knowledge evolution, an agent should maintain
clear records of facts, user preferences, rules, and their corrections. Each record should include its source, applicable
conditions, and validity, so that outdated information can be revised without affecting unrelated knowledge. For
capability evolution, simply storing a description of a successful task may not be sufficient. The agent should preserve
the procedure that led to success, including the required inputs, execution steps, tool usage, and verification results.
New tasks can test this experience, beyond checking that the original artifact remains available.
More importantly, these two forms of learning should not be completely isolated. Explicit knowledge may provide the
conditions under which a skill should be used, while procedural experience can provide evidence for revising an existing
rule. A promising direction is therefore to maintain different representations for factual knowledge and task-solving
skills, while establishing links between them. This would allow an agent to select not only what it remembers, but also
which experience is useful for the current task.
6.3
The quality of an update matters more than its frequency
The state audit shows that different agent frameworks behave quite differently after task completion. Prime Agent and
Adaptive Auto-Harness frequently modify their declared learning states, whereas Hermes has no changed digest in the
available records. OpenCode, DeepSeek Harness, and ZCode do not have an enabled native post-turn learner in the
audited configurations. These results show that enabling an Evolving condition does not mean that every framework
performs the same update operation. Update frequency alone does not show useful learning.
The comparison between all-turn and probe performance provides further evidence. For DeepSeek V4 Flash Adaptive
Auto-Harness on L2, the Evolving–Frozen difference is only +1.23 points over all turns but reaches +13.27 points on the
final probes. In contrast, GLM-5.3 Prime Agent shows a positive difference of +0.88 points over all turns but a negative
difference of -0.95 points on the probes. These examples indicate that aggregate performance and later task performance
can reflect different aspects of adaptation. They also suggest that an agent may benefit from updates on some tasks
without achieving consistent gains on new tasks. Since the probe results cover only 30 tasks per framework–condition
pair and do not isolate individual updates, these differences provide initial, non-causal evidence.
For future self-evolving agents, this raises a more important question: what should be written into persistent state? A
useful update mechanism should not simply summarize every completed interaction. It should first distinguish general
lessons from task-specific details, identify whether a failure is caused by an incorrect decision, a missing tool, or an
execution problem, and retain only information that can help with future tasks. Repeated or verified experience may
deserve higher priority than a single observation. Meanwhile, outdated rules, duplicated entries, and lessons that conflict
with newer evidence should be revised rather than accumulated indefinitely.
The next step is to verify whether the saved experience is actually used. LongMemEval separates memory indexing,
retrieval, and reading, showing that retaining information and using it correctly are different challenges (Wu et al.,
2025). A similar design can be adopted for self-evolving agents: record what was saved, whether it was retrieved, how
it affected the agent’s decision, and whether the resulting task was successful. For procedural skills, the agent should
also test a saved procedure on new inputs before treating it as a reliable capability. These checks distinguish useful
learning from merely storing more state.
The current probe analysis does not yet establish which particular update leads to a performance change. A stronger
evaluation could remove a newly written memory before a probe, disable its retrieval while keeping the memory stored,
or compare execution with and without an earlier task artifact. These comparisons could separate gains from learned
state, retained workspace artifacts, and existing task-solving ability.
6.4
Self-evolution should account for task differences and interference
An agent operates on a variety of tasks over time, and experience that helps in one setting may not be useful in another.
The domain- and stream-level results show that an overall performance gain can be concentrated in a small part of
the task distribution. In the GPT-6.1 Terra L2 evaluation, Codex, Adaptive Auto-Harness, and OpenCode obtain their
largest positive differences on D3 application tasks (+28.10, +21.95, and +28.15 points, respectively). However, these
gains do not extend to all other domains. In particular, Codex has a -17.50-point difference on Titanic modeling. When
D3 tasks are excluded, its overall Evolving–Frozen difference changes from +3.46 to -1.68 points. This result shows
that an aggregate improvement may depend strongly on which tasks are included.

The stream-level results provide another view of this variation. Adaptive Auto-Harness performs better under Evolving
in 15 of 24 L2 streams, suggesting that its positive average is shared across a majority of the evaluated streams. Codex,
however, performs better in only 10 streams, is equal in six, and performs worse in eight. Prime Agent also shows a
clear difference between the two levels: it performs better in 19 of 20 L1 streams but in only eight of 24 L2 streams,
with lower scores in 13. Therefore, the size of an average gain and the number of tasks that benefit from it should be
considered together. Large gains on a few tasks need not extend across the stream.
These findings point to the importance of task-aware experience management. A self-evolving agent should not retrieve
every saved memory or skill whenever it encounters a new request. Instead, it should consider the current task, its
required capabilities, available tools, and the conditions under which previous experience was successful. A procedure
developed for data processing, for example, should not be applied to an unrelated task simply because both tasks involve
code execution. Recording the scope and conditions of a saved skill can help the agent avoid inappropriate reuse.
Task interference also raises the need for better memory maintenance. When a new task introduces a correction or
contradicts an earlier rule, the agent should determine whether the change applies globally or only to the current
situation. Similarly, frequently used experience should remain accessible, while outdated or rarely useful entries may
need to be revised or removed. Future methods could therefore combine task-aware retrieval, conflict checking, and
selective memory updates to reduce interference while preserving useful experience over long periods. The goal should
not be to accumulate as much information as possible, but to make the right experience available when it is needed.
6.5
Implications for future evaluation and limitations
Taken together, the findings suggest that evaluating self-evolving agents requires more than comparing their final
task scores. A useful evaluation should examine whether agents can solve tasks initially, recover from unsuccessful
attempts, identify and preserve useful experience, and apply that experience to later tasks. It should also report whether
improvements are shared across different domains and streams. These measurements provide a clearer view of where
an agent improves and where its learning process still fails.
Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the current results. First, the evaluated task cohorts differ
across backbone configurations. The DeepSeek V4 Flash and GLM-5.3 evaluations do not include the dedicated
artifact-dependency, GPU, and cross-benchmark L2 streams, while GPT-6.1 Terra covers a larger set of tasks and
streams. In addition, the fifth framework is configuration-specific: DeepSeek Harness, ZCode, or Codex. Therefore,
the results do not support controlled comparisons across backbone models or a universal ranking of all frameworks.
Second, the selected historical and replacement runs span different runtime revisions, and a uniform model-call or tool
budget has not been certified. Repeated-run confidence intervals are also unavailable, so the reported differences should
be interpreted as descriptive observations. Third, detailed trace and persistent-state audits are available for DeepSeek
V4 Flash and GLM-5.3, but not for GPT-6.1 Terra. Fourth, the domain and stream analyses are descriptive. Excluding
a domain changes the task distribution and should not be interpreted as a test of statistical significance. Finally, the
current evaluation does not include systematic removal of source-task artifacts or newly written learning states, so it
cannot establish the causal contribution of a particular update to later performance.
These limitations suggest several directions for further evaluation. First, future experiments should use matched
task cohorts, consistent execution budgets, and repeated runs to improve the reliability of comparisons. Second, the
remaining dependency-focused streams should be included across backbone configurations, allowing experience reuse
to be examined under more consistent conditions. Third, state-removal and artifact-removal tests should be introduced
to determine whether later success actually depends on previously acquired experience. It would also be useful to
extend trace collection to all evaluated frameworks and record memory retrieval, tool calls, model-call and token usage,
and execution time. Following the emphasis on cost-aware agent evaluation (Kapoor et al., 2024), these measurements
could distinguish better experience use from higher resource consumption.
Overall, the results suggest that the next stage of self-evolving agent research should move beyond simply enabling
persistent memory or automatic updates. More attention should be given to how agents identify useful experience,
represent it in a form that can be reused, select it for the right task, and verify whether it improves later behavior.
Developing this complete process is important for building agents that do not merely accumulate experience, but can
turn that experience into reliable improvements over long periods of use.
7
Conclusion
In this work, we introduce EvoLink-Bench, a benchmark for evaluating self-evolving agents in persistent, inter-
dependent task streams. Its two evaluation levels capture knowledge and capability evolution, while its protocol
distinguishes within-task recovery, persistent-state retention, and post-turn updates. Across 19,635 adjudicated turn
20


A Benchmark for Evaluating Self-Evolving Agents in Long-Horizon Interdependent Task Streams
slots, we find that agent performance varies across evolution levels, persistence conditions, backbone configurations,
and task compositions. Our results show that retries provide limited recovery, persistent-state updates do not necessarily
improve subsequent performance, and the benefits of self-evolution vary across domains and task streams. These
findings highlight the need to evaluate not only whether agents accumulate experience, but also whether they can
effectively reuse it in future tasks. Further causal analysis requires matched-budget repeated runs and controlled removal
of previously acquired states or artifacts.

Hermes reviews completed public trajectories to revise general memory, user memory, and a reusable skill directory.

A.2
Resource Limits
Public L1 runtime metadata declares ceilings of 12 model calls, 16 tool calls, and 600 seconds for task execution, with
a separate turn-boundary learning policy. L2 uses task-specific CPU profiles, with execution ceilings from 600 to 3,600
seconds and tighter model/tool caps where declared. Adapter limits and actual recorded usage must be distinguished
from these declarations; the selected historical and replacement runs do not form a certified uniform-budget cohort.
Explicit task failures and deadlines affect only the corresponding turn. Earlier committed rewards and later actually
executed turns keep their recorded values. The mix of runtime revisions, incomplete failed-request usage, and differing
adapter counters prevents a certified equal-budget or decoding-speed comparison.
A.3
Task Scoring and Reporting
A turn’s terminal reward rs,t ∈[0, 1] is distinct from its binary acceptance indicator bs,t. The runtime uses the final
scored submission rather than selecting the best score over retries. L1 applies package-owned executable checks,
including preference checks where specified. L2 uses native verification together with the prescribed task rubric. For
MLE tasks declaring continuous native scoring, the judge checks submission validity and a valid result receives the
upstream normalized metric, clipped to [0, 1]. This retains partial credit separately from binary acceptance.

C.3
Case 2: Path Handling and a Defect Left Unchanged Across Retries
The L2 pair uses SRA-blocked s01, Turn 15, under Reset, with the same public request, runtime-profile digest, and effec-
tive work limits. The function must count a requested element and write task_func.log inside the directory supplied
as log_path. DeepSeek Harness correctly builds this path with os.path.join(log_path, "task_func.log").
All three saved Hermes submissions use direct string concatenation, equivalent to log_path + "task_func.log".
With log_path="logs", this gives logstask_func.log, while the required destination is logs/task_func.log.
The agent’s own tests use ./, where the separator is already present. Its later edits focus on closing or clearing logging
handlers and leave the path expression unchanged. Hermes retains partial reward 0.15; DeepSeek Harness scores 1.0.
The existing offline artifact audit checks the saved DeepSeek Harness version and all three saved Hermes versions with
and without a trailing slash. It confirms the output-location difference while preserving the returned count. These checks
use temporary logging paths and do not create a new benchmark score. The evidence supports a concrete path-handling
and revision-strategy contrast; it does not establish that either framework is always better on unrelated tasks.
C.4
Recorded Learning-State Changes
The main-text Figure 9 reports the changed-digest counts and rates for the available Evolving snapshots. The native-
execution baselines have no enabled post-turn learner in these integrations, and their available declared-learning-state
snapshots remain unchanged. Hermes also has no changed digest in the selected DeepSeek V4 Flash and GLM-5.3
records, despite nonzero score differences between conditions. These observations reinforce that a condition label or
score difference cannot be used as a substitute for direct update evidence.
The Prime Agent GLM-5.3 records contain 172 state-changing updates whose reported learner-token count is zero.

Feedback within a task is not yet a learned rule.
After a normally evaluated submission, the runner saves the re-
sponse, tool calls, reward, acceptance flag, and public failure feedback. L1 records these in turns[].interactions[]
in the summary or retained completed-turn prefix; L2 records them in trace.jsonl. The feedback enters the follow-up
request or dialogue history for the next submission. Task files can also remain available for a local correction. This
preserves the evidence needed to retry the current task, but does not by itself create a reusable rule for future tasks.
The explicit learning call occurs after the turn.
The runner invokes the shared learning barrier once the task’s
submission loop has ended, passing the public trajectory and feedback from that turn. It does not invoke this cross-task
learner after every failed submission. Reset and Frozen disable this update path. Evolving enables supported learners,
but an enabled call can still fail, be rejected, or leave the state unchanged.

C.6.2
R2: Keeping a Correct Fix and Repairing the Remaining Requirement
GLM-5.3 Adaptive’s SRA-blocked s01, Turn 14 (bigcodebench_00956), asks for a string function. It must remove
punctuation, apply seeded random casing, and map spaces to one underscore, tabs to two, and newlines to three.
Saved trajectory, followed by its interpretation.
Submission 1 inserts underscores for whitespace and then removes
punctuation. Because an underscore is itself punctuation, the second operation deletes the separators that the first
operation just created. The native reward is 0.4. The first retry moves punctuation removal before whitespace conversion,
preserving the inserted separators. However, it uses a single regular-expression replacement for all whitespace, losing
the distinction between spaces, tabs, and newlines. Despite claiming success, it fails with reward 0.275.
The second retry receives an explicit public reminder of the three required replacement lengths. It keeps the corrected
operation order and replaces newline, tab, and space separately. The native task now passes with reward 1. A small
offline check of the three saved code blocks makes the difference visible: for input a b\tc\nd!, where \t and \n
denote tab and newline, the lowercase outputs are respectively abcd, a_b_c_d, and a_b__c___d. Lowercasing here
only makes the whitespace behavior easier to read. The check leaves the native score unchanged.
Why the retry helps.
The agent uses a useful part of its previous attempt: punctuation is still removed first. It then
changes the part that remains wrong, instead of discarding the whole solution. The detailed follow-up supplies the
remaining requirement directly, so this is evidence of using task feedback and retaining a successful local correction,
rather than unaided discovery of a new rule. The agent also spends calls browsing task/workspace files and initially
tests the wrong expectation that all whitespace becomes one underscore. Those actions do not explain the recovery; the
decisive visible change is the final replacement logic. The drop in intermediate reward also shows that a plausible edit
and a confident self-report do not guarantee an improvement.
