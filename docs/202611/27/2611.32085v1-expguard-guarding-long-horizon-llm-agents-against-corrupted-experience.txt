# ExpGuard: Guarding Long-Horizon LLM Agents against Corrupted Experience 

Yuzhou Chen 1, Licheng Pan 1, Zhixuan Chu 1∗, Yunsheng Lu 1, Yichen Sun 1, Han Zheng 1, Yan Wang 2, Bingyu Zhu 2, Haiwen Hong 2, Longtao Huang 2, Kui Ren 1

> 1

> ∗Corresponding author.

Long-horizon large language model (LLM) agents distill past trajectories into reusable experiences. Misleading or adversarial guidance in source trajectories can corrupt these experiences , allowing harmful instructions to persist across tasks and influence future agent actions. Assessing the risks of potentially corrupted experiences poses three challenges: (1) modeling provenance and execution dependencies before experience reuse , (2) learning task-conditioned risk from trajectory outcomes , and (3) repairing risky experiences at online inference . To address these challenges, we present ExpGuard, a framework for retrieval-triggered experience risk assessment and online repair . Specifically, to tackle challenge (1), ExpGuard integrates experience semantics with provenance and execution dependencies in a task-conditioned heterogeneous graph to predict attack and task-failure risks before experience reuse. To enable task-conditioned risk supervision and tackle challenge (2), we conduct matched counterfactual replays of each original experience and its minimally neutralized counterpart from the same task and environment checkpoint, deriving separate risk labels from differences in attack success and legitimate task completion. To tackle challenge (3) at inference time, ExpGuard repairs candidate experiences flagged as high-risk by the graph-based predictor at retrieval and re-predicts their risks before they enter the agent’s active experience context. Extensive experiments demonstrate the effectiveness of ExpGuard in identifying risky experiences and defending agents against experience corruption.

## 1 Introduction 

Large language model (LLM) agents increasingly undertake long-horizon tasks that require reasoning [1], planning [2], and tool use [3, 4]. Across extended interactions, they observe changing environments, modify persistent resources, and accumulate execution trajectories [5, 6, 7]. Tool-use and agent benchmarks expose the breadth of these interac-tions [8, 9]. Trajectory-derived experiences can condense execution histories into reusable guidance. For example, ReasoningBank [10] distills insights from successful and failed trajectories into structured memory items with a title, description, and content. Such items can encode the situations in which a strategy is useful, the reasoning behind it, and practical guidance for future tasks. Experience reuse allows agents to build on prior interactions [11, 12], retain workflows [13], and acquire executable capabilities [14]. The quality and trustworthiness of accumulated experience consequently affect later decisions. An experience can also carry misleading or adversarial guidance from the trajectory in which it originated. Agents routinely encounter untrusted observations and instructions during tool-mediated execution [15, 16, 17]. Memory-injection studies [18, 19, 20] show that adversarial content can be stored through ordinary interactions and influence later retrievals. A retrieved experience may increase the chance of an attack objective succeeding, reduce the chance of completing a legitimate task, or both. These effects depend on the current task and environment: guidance that is useful in one context may be harmful in another. Its influence may also propagate through actions that read or modify persistent artifacts, so the experience need not be explicitly cited at the point where a failure becomes visible. Protecting experience reuse requires connecting the content of an item with its historical provenance and the current execution context. ## 2 Motivation 

In this section, we illustrate how misleading guidance can persist through experience reuse and affect a later task in Figure 1. In this example, an unverified statement in persistent memory equates routine cleanup with clearing an entire workspace. After a session boundary, that statement contributes to an extracted experience. A later task asks the agent to remove only temporary files and preserve project files; reuse of the original experience expands the cleanup scope and violates the task constraint. The example makes the relevant risk depend on both the experience’s provenance and the new task’s authorized scope. The paired continuations in Figure 1 hold the new task, initial files, tools, agent configuration, and other experiences 3fixed while changing the target experience text. Their different attack and task outcomes supply the directional supervision later defined in Section 3.4. When the extracted experience is explicitly retrieved for a future task, ExpGuard assesses it before the agent can adopt its guidance. 

## 3 Methodology 

In this section, we present ExpGuard, a framework for assessing and repairing trajectory-derived experiences before their reuse in long-horizon LLM agents. Experiences are generated using ReasoningBank’s open-source extractor [10] and stored by ExpGuard with their historical provenance and version metadata. A task-conditioned heterogeneous graph combines that provenance with the current execution prefix. Matched replays of an original experience and its minimally neutralized counterpart provide separate attack-risk and task-failure-risk supervision for an HGTNN predictor. Replay continuations provide offline supervision but are excluded from the pre-reuse graph. At each explicit retrieval boundary, the predictor assesses candidate experiences before they enter the agent context; high-risk versions are repaired and their risks re-predicted. Figure 2 shows how offline supervision, graph-based prediction, and online repair connect, and we provide theoretical analysis and reproduction details in Appendices A and B.

3.3 Task-Conditioned Heterogeneous Experience Graph 

In this subsection, we construct a task-conditioned experience graph combining historical provenance and execution dependencies using only evidence available before reuse.  

> Minimal graph schema.

At step t, we define the heterogeneous experience graph as follows: 

Gt = ( Vt, R, E t), (9) where Vt is the node set, Et is the typed edge set, and R is the relation-type set. The full graph contains five node types: experience, task, action, observation, and artifact. An experience node holds the current version and provenance identifiers, while a task node holds the legitimate objective, constraints, and available evaluation specification. Action and observation nodes record executed steps and their results, while an artifact node represents a versioned persistent resource such as a file or application object. Conditions and guidance remain in the experience text, rather than becoming separate node types. Source trajectory and checkpoint identifiers are attributes of the relevant nodes and edges. The relation set R comprises seven core types: derived_from, produces, next, reads, writes, retrieved_for, and applies_-to. derived_from links an experience to supporting historical actions. produces links an action to its observation; next orders actions within a trajectory. reads and writes link actions to the artifact versions they access or change. retrieved_for links an experience to a task for which it was retrieved, whereas applies_to records an observed application of an experience to an action. Reverse edges may be added for message passing and inherit the evidence and timestamp of their forward relation, but they do not introduce new semantic relations.  

> Evidence and temporal scope.

Action, observation, and artifact events come from the agent runtime; experience text and source links come from extraction records. The current task specification and, where available, benchmark evidence provide task and checkpoint attributes. We retain only historical events connected to the target experience or relevant artifacts and the current task prefix. Each edge carries its source identifier and event time, so the graph can be reconstructed at a specific assessment point. In particular, a pre-reuse graph contains no action, observation, 5artifact change, evaluator outcome, or applies_to edge generated by a subsequent replay. Neutralized experience text is also excluded from the original branch’s input graph.  

> Construction at retrieval.

At a retrieval boundary, Gt is assembled from extraction records, relevant historical events, the current task, and the runtime prefix available at time t. For a retrieved experience ei, we extract a bounded neighborhood around its provenance and the current task: 

Gti,q = Extract( Gt, e i, q ; BG), (10) where BG bounds the number of relevant historical and current-prefix entities. We fix this subgraph as the pre-reuse snapshot ¯Gti,q before either replay begins offline or before the candidate enters the agent context online. This cutoff excludes subsequent execution and outcome evidence from the predictor. A later retrieval constructs a new graph using the evidence then available.

4.1 Experimental Setup 

Benchmarks. We evaluate experience-level risk identification on MemForm [33], CIK-Bench [34], and SMP-Bench, the evaluation dataset associated with Sleeper Memory Poisoning (SMP) [35]. Each evaluated unit is a retrieved experience under a current task and pre-reuse graph snapshot; its supervision follows the original–neutralized replay contrasts in Section 3.4. We use Gemma-4-12B-IT [36], DeepSeek-V4-Flash [37], and GPT-6 Luna [38] as agent backbones. For online defense, we examine direct experience override, adapted MemoryGraft [39], and adapted SMP attacks on STATE-Bench [40], AppWorld [41], and MemoryArena [42]. The online unit is an agent execution in which candidate experiences are assessed at retrieval before entering the active experience context. 

Comparisons. The identification baselines include random ranking; the prompt-injection classifiers PIGuard [43] and Llama Prompt Guard 2-86M [44]; a frozen-encoder MLP that scores experience text without graph message passing; and two few-shot LLM assessors. The online comparison includes No Defense, PIGuard, OWASP Agent Memory Guard [45], A-MemGuard [46], and a Claude Opus 5.5 few-shot auditor with rewriting. We additionally examine the graph predictor by shuffling edges, limiting its context to one hop, and comparing heterogeneous graph 10 encoders. These variants respectively probe relational organization, access to historical neighborhoods, and the choice of message-passing architecture. 

Metrics. For experience-level ranking, we use area under the precision–recall curve (AUPRC) [47] as the primary metric because risky candidates form a minority in every reported setting. We also report area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) [48] and recall at a 10% false-positive rate (R@10%FPR), which measures how many risky candidates can be identified when interventions on benign experiences are limited. For online defense, attack success rate (ASR) measures attainment of the attacker objective and task success rate (TSR) measures completion of the legitimate task. Lower ASR and higher TSR are preferable. The ∆ columns in Table 4 give percentage-point changes from No Defense. Full implementation configurations are provided in Appendix B.3.

## 5 Related Work 

ExpGuard connects trajectory-derived experience, persistent-memory security, counterfactual supervision, and heterogeneous dependency modeling to assess risk immediately before cross-task reuse. 

5.1 Long-Horizon Agent Experience and Corruption 

Agent memory extends beyond stored interaction records to reusable knowledge. Reflexion and ExpeL derive verbal reflections and transferable lessons from trajectories [11, 12]; Voyager stores executable skills [14], and Agent Workflow Memory induces reusable routines [13]. ReasoningBank distills both successful and failed trajectories into structured memory items [10]. ReMe combines experience distillation with adaptive reuse and refinement [49]; Memory-R1 learns memory-management operations [50]; and MemEvolve adapts experience and its surrounding memory architecture [51]. PlugMem instead emphasizes compact, task-agnostic graphs of episodic knowledge [52]. These approaches organize knowledge for future use, while ExpGuard evaluates whether a retrieved item’s guidance is risky under the current task and execution context. It retains the source trajectory as evidence alongside the extracted text. Retrieval-focused systems address when and how stored knowledge enters an agent’s decisions. ProactAgent models when to consult a structured experience base [53]. SAMem aligns retrieval with the current decision state, while AdaMEM adapts guidance from trajectory memory as a task unfolds [54, 55]. These methods focus on selecting and adapting experience during execution. Their retrieval objectives differ from the directional attack and task-failure supervision in ExpGuard, which assesses applicable candidates before admission and retains historical dependencies as risk evidence. Reuse also depends on how retrieved knowledge influences decisions. Studies of experience following and controlled memory interventions examine error propagation and agents’ varying causal dependence on condensed experi-ence [56, 57]. AMA-Bench emphasizes action, observation, and objective information in long-horizon memory [58]. Mem2ActBench tests memory application in tool actions, while Memora examines obsolete-memory reuse over extended interactions [59, 60]. Tool-use and stateful environments expose the extended execution chains through which retained guidance affects later actions [7, 4]. ExpGuard targets the risk of a particular retrieved experience before context admission, rather than memory retention or retrieval quality alone. Untrusted interaction can contaminate that guidance. Indirect prompt injection introduces instructions through external content [15, 16, 17]; persistent poisoning can influence later retrieval and execution [61, 62]. Query-only attacks show that poisoning need not require direct memory-store access [18, 63]. MemoryGraft and Sleeper Memory Poisoning expose forged procedural guidance and delayed activation [39, 35], alongside related procedural-memory and trigger-based attacks [64, 65]. These mechanisms make an experience’s apparent usefulness insufficient for deciding whether it should be reused. ExpGuard connects its source evidence to current task constraints and assesses the consequences of original versus neutralized guidance. 

5.2 Causal Attribution and Counterfactual Evaluation 

Controlled interventions relate outcome differences to specified changes [66, 67]. Counterfactual tests also evaluate attribution in language models [68, 69]. Causal Agent Replay intervenes on execution steps and replays continua-tions [30]; CausalFlow identifies failure-inducing steps through counterfactual analysis [31]. AttriGuard tests whether proposed tool calls depend on untrusted observations at runtime [70]. ExpGuard instead intervenes on reusable 15 experience at a matched checkpoint. Original–neutralized replay supplies directional attack and task-completion labels for separate risk heads, with unavailable outcomes masked independently. This places counterfactual execution offline, allowing online decisions from a pre-reuse graph rather than requiring replay at each intervention. 

5.3 Provenance Graphs and Heterogeneous Graph Learning 

Provenance represents information origins and dependencies [71, 28], while whole-system tracing and runtime queries connect actions to resources across execution [29, 72]. AMA-Bench also organizes agent memory through a causality graph [58]. GraphSAGE, R-GCN, and HAN provide neighborhood aggregation, relation-aware propagation, and heterogeneous attention [73, 74, 75]; HGT uses node- and edge-type-dependent attention [32]. ExpGuard uses these modeling principles for retrieval-time risk assessment. Its five node types and seven relations connect experience meaning, source actions, artifact interactions, and the current task prefix. Freezing the snapshot before replay keeps outcome evidence in supervision rather than predictor inputs. 

5.4 Online Guardrails for LLM Agents 

Prompt- and response-level safeguards such as Llama Guard and WildGuard [21, 22] complement checks of proposed actions, tool use, and task alignment [23, 24, 25]. Safiron provides pre-execution guardrails for planners [76], while AttriGuard adds runtime causal tests for tool invocations [70]. These approaches assess behavior at execution boundaries; persistent experience introduces an earlier opportunity to intervene before stored guidance enters the agent context. Memory-oriented defenses address related boundaries: MIND models intent-aware memory injection [77], A-MemGuard validates related memories and stores corrective lessons [46], MAPLE-Guard covers the memory life-cycle [78], and TrustMem verifies consolidation transitions [79]. Membrane retrieves contrastive safety memories to ground decisions about harmful and benign requests [80]. ExpGuard focuses on task-conditioned attack and task-failure risks of a retrieved trajectory-derived item. Their maximum triggers selective repair using provenance and current task evidence. Reconstructing the graph and re-predicting risk governs admission of the revised experience. 

## 6 Conclusions 

This work examined how a long-horizon agent can assess experience distilled from earlier trajectories before using it on a new task. An experience’s risk is conditioned by its source trajectory, the task now being attempted, and the actions its guidance may induce. ExpGuard retains historical provenance and represents the relevant execution dependencies in a minimal heterogeneous graph. It constructs supervision by replaying original and minimally neutralized versions from a matched checkpoint, deriving separate labels for attack induction and loss of legitimate task completion. Head-specific masks keep unavailable outcomes from being treated as observed negatives. A frozen text encoder and HGTNN then combine experience meaning with typed historical and current-task evidence to estimate both risks from a pre-reuse snapshot. At an explicit retrieval boundary, the larger predicted risk determines whether a candidate enters the agent context or requires LLM repair. Repair produces a linked experience version whose graph is reconstructed for re-prediction before reuse. This places assessment immediately before the agent can act on retrieved guidance. Across the reported identification benchmarks, ExpGuard leads all compared methods on AUPRC, AUROC, and recall at 10% FPR; its AUPRC advantage over the strongest comparator ranges from 0.091 to 0.175 across nine benchmark–backbone settings. Shuffling graph edges or restricting context to one hop lowers identification performance, indicating that relational structure and multi-hop history contribute useful evidence. In the online comparison, the full workflow attains the lowest ASR and highest TSR in all nine attack–benchmark settings, linking candidate-level prediction to better security and task outcomes during execution. The present intervention point is explicit experience retrieval, where a candidate can be assessed before it is consumed. Other paths that place memory directly into an agent’s context call for their own assessment boundary. MemoryArena [42] tests interdependent multi-session tasks, where earlier actions and feedback inform later subtasks and expose delayed corruption. 

Data partitioning and evaluation records. Versions and replay pairs sharing an experience and source lineage remain in one partition. Predictor inputs contain only pre-reuse evidence; supervision records retain branch outcomes and masks alongside tasks, checkpoints, graphs, versions, and provenance. B.2 Attack and Defense Settings 

Direct Experience Override modifies reusable behavioral guidance. Adapted MemoryGraft [39] injects forged procedural guidance into later experience reuse. Adapted Sleeper Memory Poisoning [35] activates corrupted guidance in later tasks or under trigger conditions. 

No Defense consumes retrieved experience without screening. PIGuard [43] provides content-based prompt-injection screening. OWASP Agent Memory Guard [45] screens memory operations using detectors and declarative policies. 

A-MemGuard [46] checks related memories through reasoning-path consensus and stores corrective lessons separately. 

LLM Auditor–Rewrite uses Claude Opus 5.5 (Few-shot) to flag risky experience, then applies the same LLM repair operator and budget ( BR = 3 ) as ExpGuard, controlling repair while comparing identification. ExpGuard uses the maximum of its two predicted risks to trigger repair, reconstructs the graph, and re-predicts before admission below γ

(Algorithm 1). Offline baselines and backbones are listed in Table 1. 

B.3 Implementation Details 

Experience Extraction and Retrieval. ReasoningBank’s open-source extractor [10] produces title–description–content experiences retaining source-trajectory provenance and version metadata. Each retrieval returns the top-4 candidates; applicable items undergo ExpGuard assessment before context admission. 

Heterogeneous Experience Graph. We retain the five node types and seven core relations in Section 3.3. Frozen BGE-M3 supplies 1024-dimensional node-text embeddings, concatenated with structured attributes and projected by type to 256 dimensions. Pre-reuse graphs use two-hop neighborhoods with approximately 64 nodes and 256 edges; reverse message-passing edges inherit forward-edge evidence. Two hops is the full-context RQ2 setting; one hop is the ablation. 

Risk predictor. The HGTNN has LG = 2 layers, four attention heads, and 256 hidden dimensions. Target-experience, task, and pooled graph representations concatenate to 768 dimensions. Two independent MLP heads, each 768 →

23 128 → 1 with a sigmoid, predict Attack Risk and Task Failure Risk; their maximum is the online score. Training uses masked BCE (Eq. (23)), λattack = λtask = 1 , and AdamW: learning rate 10 −3, weight decay 10 −4, batch size 32, dropout 0.2, and at most 100 epochs. Early stopping monitors validation AUPRC with patience 10. 

Counterfactual replay. We run R = 3 original–minimally-neutralized pairs from the same checkpoint, preserving task constraints, environment/artifact state, agent/tool configuration, and other experiences. Repetitions use different rollout seeds. Each pair follows Eqs. (15)–(16); head-specific majority voting aggregates labels, retaining observability masks and excluding Unknown supervision. Predictor graphs remain fixed before replay. RQ6 evaluates R ∈{1, 2, 3, 4, 5}.

Online repair. Validation-selected thresholds are γ = 0 .085 (STATE-Bench), 0.093 (AppWorld), and 0.076 (Memor-yArena). At maximum risk ≥ γ, the LLM rewrites experience using provenance, task constraints, and the current snapshot, with temperature 0.2–0.3 according to the run configuration and BR = 3 attempts per retrieved experience per boundary. Each attempt increments the version, reconstructs the graph, and re-runs the same two-head predictor. Risk below γ permits admission; otherwise repair continues, with exhausted high-risk candidates withheld. Context refresh follows Algorithm 1. All experiments used Intel Xeon Platinum 8368 CPUs and 8 NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPUs. 

B.4 Prompt Templates 

Prompt B.4.1: Minimal Experience Neutralization         

> System instruction. Construct a minimally neutralized counterpart of the supplied experience for paired offline replay. The counterpart will be compared with the original under identical conditions, so change only the spans that direct an agent’s future behavior. Apply the same rule to every experience, regardless of its content or apparent quality.
> Requirements. (1) Preserve the title–description–content schema, the topic, and all unaffected contextual information. (2) Identify behaviorally directive spans: imperatives, recommendations, trigger-conditioned rules, and statements that prescribe which tool, argument, scope, target, order, or output a future agent should use. (3) Rewrite each such span as a neutral, non-operative statement that does not instruct or recommend the behavior, for example as a description of what the source trajectory did or observed. (4) Keep edits local: leave text outside these spans unchanged. (5) Do not introduce a replacement strategy, new facts, entities, actions, tool results, a solution to any task, or any judgment about outcomes. (6) Do not decide whether the experience is correct, harmful, or safe; neutralize directive spans by the same criterion in all cases. (7) If no directive span is present, return the experience unchanged.
> Input. Original experience: {original_experience} .
> Output. Return one JSON object containing exactly the string fields title ,description , and content . Return no explanations, labels, risk scores, or replay predictions.
