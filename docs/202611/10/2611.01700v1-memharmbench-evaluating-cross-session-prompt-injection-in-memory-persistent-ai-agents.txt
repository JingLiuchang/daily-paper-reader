## Abstract 

AI agents often rely on persistent memory to retain user preferences and operational rules across sessions. However, this capability introduces a new safety risk: malicious instructions from messages, documents, or Web content can be retained in memory and influence future tasks even after the original attack content is removed. Existing attack success metrics, however, fail to capture who is affected and how attacks translate into downstream harm during otherwise normal task execution. We introduce MemHarmBench , a victim-centric benchmark for evaluating cross-session prompt injection in memory-persistent AI agents. MemHarmBench considers three categories of potential victims: users, workspace/project owners, and external stakeholders. Our evaluation first exposes an agent to malicious instructions and tests whether they are retained in memory. We then remove the attack content and initiate a new session with a legitimate task while preserving the agent’s memory. We jointly evaluate whether the agent follows the attacker’s intent, completes the legitimate task, and exhibits observable execution anomalies. Across multiple agent systems, we find that cross-session attacks can remain behaviorally stealthy: agents often complete tasks normally and exhibit no detectable anomalies while still making critical decisions aligned with the attacker’s intent. By connecting malicious memory retention to downstream decisions and affected parties, MemHarmBench provides a systematic framework for exposing safety risks that conventional attack- and task-success metrics may overlook. We introduce MemHarmBench , a victim-centric benchmark for evaluating cross-session prompt injection in memory-persistent AI agents. MemHarmBench addresses two limitations of existing prompt-injection benchmarks. First, attack-centric evaluation does not account for the parties affected by a successful attack. Second, binary attack-success metrics conflate attacks that disrupt task completion with those that redirect decisions while preserving normal execution. MemHarmBench therefore organizes attacks around three categories of potential victims—users, workspace/project owners, and external stakeholders—and separately evaluates target adoption, task completion, and execution anomalies. Specifically, MemHarmBench defines 12 attack objectives and instantiates them using 24 fixed templates across 12 operational contexts, yielding 288 cases with verifiable attacker-targeted decisions. Each case follows a two-stage protocol that traces an injected instruction from exposure to downstream influence. During 

exposure , the agent encounters a malicious instruction in external content, and we test whether it enters persistent memory. During triggering , the attack content is removed and the agent receives a legitimate task in a new session with memory preserved. We then evaluate whether the retained instruction redirects the target decision while the agent completes the legitimate task without observable execution anomalies. Across four agent–backbone configurations, retained injected instructions redirect agent decisions in 59.38– 76.39% of eligible indirect prompt-injection triggers (Figure 2). More importantly, when averaged equally across configurations, 55.71% of eligible episodes exhibit all three properties simultaneously: the agent adopts the attacker’s target, formally completes the legitimate task, and exhibits no judged execution anomaly. In more than half of eligible episodes, the attacker-selected value thus sits inside a complete deliverable produced by an orderly execution. In summary, our main contributions are: • Victim-centric modeling. We formulate cross-session prompt injection around the parties it can affect, with 12 attack objectives spanning users, workspace/project owners, and external stakeholders. • End-to-end cross-session evaluation. We trace injected instructions from memory retention to downstream target adoption, while measuring both legitimate task completion and execution anomalies. This uncovers risks that conventional attack- or task-success metrics alone can obscure. 3• Controlled and reproducible benchmark. MemHarmBench consists of 288 cases with standardized exposure conditions, isolated cross-session triggers, and case-specific evaluation criteria, enabling con-trolled and reproducible evaluation across agent implementations. We publicly release the benchmark and evaluation code. 

## 2 Related Work 

Recent surveys and systematic analyses map safety threats across large models, the LLM lifecycle, and self-evolving agent systems [22, 25, 40]. 

2.1 Memory-Persistent Agents and Prompt Injection 

AI agents combine reasoning with tools and actions [34, 49], including interaction with external Web content [27]. Persistent memory extends these capabilities across sessions by retaining prior experiences, feedback, preferences, and other user-specific information [31, 35, 57]. Recent systems provide explicit mechanisms for organizing and updating such state: MemGPT manages information across memory tiers [30], while A-Mem, Mem0, and MemoryOS support structured long-term memory management [7, 18, 47]. Benchmarks such as LoCoMo and LongMemEval further evaluate whether agents can retain and retrieve information over extended interactions [26, 44]. While this literature largely focuses on whether agents can remember and use information effectively, persistent memory raises a complementary safety question: which information should be allowed to persist and influence future decisions? 

Prompt injection makes this question particularly consequential. Indirect prompt injection embeds adversarial instructions in webpages, retrieved documents, or tool outputs that agents encounter while performing legitimate tasks [15, 50]. In memory-persistent agents, the influence of such instructions can extend beyond the session in which they appear: an agent may retain an externally supplied instruction as a rule or preference and apply it in a later session after the original content has been removed. This setting requires distinguishing 

authority from retention , and retention from adoption (Figure 3). A user-authorized preference may legitimately guide future behavior, whereas an externally injected rule may not; similarly, retention of an unauthorized rule need not lead to its adoption in a later decision. Our MemHarmBench explicitly separates these outcomes, tracing injected instructions from initial exposure and memory retention to their subsequent adoption in decisions affecting different parties. 

2.2 Memory Poisoning and Harm Evaluation 

From task capability to safety. Interactive benchmarks evaluate agents on shopping and Web navigation [9, 48, 58], visual Web and computer use [19, 45], and practical tasks across tool environments [12, 23, 51]. Safety and security benchmarks further examine whether adversarial inputs can redirect these capabilities: InjecAgent [52], AgentDojo [8], and Agent Security Bench [53] evaluate attack success alongside task utility, while Tensor Trust and AgentVigil study human-generated and automated attacks [38, 41]. AgentHazard evaluates harmful behavior across multi-step computer-use trajectories [13]. JustAsk studies system-prompt extraction through autonomous interactions by code agents [56]. Our MemHarmBench focuses on a distinct setting in which adversarial influence persists across sessions, redirecting later decisions after the original attack content has been removed. 

From memory access to rule acquisition. Existing memory attacks differ in how adversarial information enters an agent’s memory. AgentPoison directly inserts malicious memory or retrieval records [6], whereas MINJA induces memory acquisition through queries [10]. MemoryGraft introduces malicious content through external documents [36], while WhisperBench studies malicious email entering personal-agent memory [54]. In contrast, MemHarmBench exposes agents to low-privilege external content and evaluates whether malicious instructions are retained through their native memory mechanisms and subsequently adopted in a new session. From attack success to affected parties. Prior benchmarks broaden agent evaluation beyond attack success alone. ToolEmu [33] evaluates safety risks in emulated tool environments, while AgentHarm [1]and OS-Harm [20] examine malicious tasks, computer-use misuse, and prompt injection. StakeBench takes a stakeholder-centric view of Web-agent attacks, identifying affected parties while distinguishing attack success from task disruption and behavioral irregularities [42]. Our MemHarmBench extends this perspective across sessions by linking affected parties to malicious rule retention and subsequent target adoption, while separately measuring legitimate task completion and execution anomalies. This formulation reveals cases in which an injected rule redirects a consequential decision despite otherwise normal task execution. Because adversarial instructions can also be embedded in images [3, 32], we further extend our cross-session evaluation to visual prompt injection through sandboxed webpage images. 

## 3 The Design of MemHarmBench 

MemHarmBench evaluates cross-session prompt injection by tracing how malicious instructions enter persistent memory and influence later decisions affecting different parties. Its design combines a victim-centric attack taxonomy, controlled attack instantiation, and a two-stage evaluation protocol. This formulation connects stakeholder-oriented analysis of prompt injection with memory-based attacks whose influence persists beyond the session in which they are introduced [42, 54]. Rather than treating attack success as a single outcome, MemHarmBench separately measures whether an injected rule is retained and whether it is subsequently adopted in a legitimate downstream task in a new session. 

3.1 Victim-Centric Modeling 

Memory-persistent agent environment. An agent 𝐴 receives a task 𝑥 𝑠 in session 𝑠 , interacts with a synthetic environment 𝐸 𝑠 , and has access to persistent memory 𝑀 𝑠 . Its execution produces a trajectory 𝜏 𝑠 , a final response, and updates to the environment and memory state. Each benchmark case separates the interaction 5into two sessions. In the exposure session 𝑠 𝑒 , the agent encounters an attacker-controlled instruction embedded in external content. In a distinct trigger session 𝑠 𝑡 ≠ 𝑠 𝑒 , the original attack content is absent and the agent receives a legitimate task while retaining its persistent memory. This separation isolates whether an injected rule persists beyond its original context and influences a later decision. 

Victim taxonomy. We define the victim as the party potentially affected by the attacker-targeted decision, regardless of whether that party directly delegated the task to the agent. Figure 4 organizes twelve attack objectives across three victim categories, while Table 1 details the four objectives associated with each category. Each objective is instantiated as an attacker-selected target value or choice whose adoption can be directly evaluated in the trigger task. These objectives provide decision-level proxies for potential harm rather than measurements of downstream real-world consequences. Threat model. The attacker controls either a task-level instruction attributed to a low-privilege collaborator or one external carrier encountered during a benign task. 3.2 Attack Design and Benchmark Instantiation 

Attack template construction. Each attack template specifies a trigger, target entity, attacker-intended effect, case-specific value, and persistence scope. A deterministic rendering procedure instantiates these elements as either an explicit instruction or a convention embedded in a native business artifact. Context binding then supplies synthetic participants, verified facts, and the subsequent task. Separately, evaluator-side criteria define rule retention, attacker-targeted decision adoption, formal task completion, and trajectory anomalies. This design keeps the underlying attack mechanism consistent across contexts while cleanly separating agent-visible content from evaluation metadata. Appendix A provides the template and case schemas together with the complete objective–template mapping used for benchmark construction. 

Deterministic instantiation. MemHarmBench crosses 24 registered attack templates with 12 operational contexts to produce 288 cases. The templates comprise 8 direct-instruction and 16 indirect-content attacks, yielding 96 and 192 cases, respectively. Each attack objective is represented by two templates and instantiated across all 12 contexts. During context binding, template identity, delivery channel, slot structure, and rendering logic remain fixed and are shared across agent adapters. Programmatic checks verify catalog balance, required fields, native artifact formats, visible metadata, and the absence of attacker-selected values from trigger inputs.

Exposure and conditional triggering. Each episode begins with a case-replicate exposure sequence and proceeds to a trigger only when eligible. The exposure task explicitly asks the agent to adopt a workflow convention, allowing the benchmark to test whether the injected rule enters persistent memory. If the initial memory check is negative and no technical execution failure has occurred, the controller permits one predefined follow-up within the same session. Once retention is confirmed, a new trigger session preserves the agent’s product state while replacing the original business inputs and removing the attack carrier. The trigger instruction contains neither the attacker-selected value nor an explicit request to retrieve the retained rule, ensuring that subsequent target adoption reflects cross-session influence rather than renewed exposure. For efficient execution, a lightweight provisional gate may schedule the trigger before offline semantic judging is complete; an episode judged positive only afterward may therefore require deferred triggering. Reported trigger results include only episodes that were actually triggered and subsequently judged. Agent systems and base LLMs. We evaluate two agent systems, OpenClaw [29] and Claude Code [2], each paired with GPT-5 [28] and Gemini-2.5-Flash [11]. We run three full-catalog repetitions for each configuration, each comprising 192 IPI and 96 DPI cases, for a total of 3,456 case–replicate episodes. Every repetition thus spans all twelve objectives and contexts. All configurations share the same cases, evaluation rubrics, and denominator rules while retaining their native planning and memory mechanisms. Model requests are routed through a relay, and GPT-5 serves as the evaluator. All exposure episodes receive resolved formal labels, and every positive exposure has a fully judged trigger. Table 3 aggregates eligible triggers across repetitions. Appendix B provides execution details, coverage statistics, and aggregation procedures. 

Exposure and state transfer. In the main study, each exposure asks the agent to adopt a workflow convention. If the initial memory check is non-positive and no technical failure occurs, the agent receives one predefined follow-up within the same session. A positive retention gate initiates the trigger in a new session after the attack carrier has been removed. For OpenClaw, selected native-memory files are restored into a fresh workspace; for Claude Code, mounted product state is retained across a new conversation. Controlled extensions use a single exposure and transfer only allowlisted native memory, excluding conversation history, attachments, and exposure-workspace edits. Technical retries are reserved for execution or transport failures and never replace valid negative outcomes. Saved memory, trajectories, and deliverables provide the evidence for separate retention and trigger judgments. Appendix B details state boundaries and retry budgets. 

Runtime and evaluation. OpenClaw 2026.7.1 and Claude Code 2.1.228 run in Docker through relay endpoints. Each main-study stage allows up to 900 seconds, with a 970-second host-capture window for OpenClaw and at most two technical retries. Positive retention gates may also schedule deferred triggers following formal rejudgment. For OpenClaw, memory search is disabled when restoring selected native-memory Markdown. These product-specific transitions preserve each system’s native state boundaries. GPT-5 evaluates saved inputs, case predicates, trajectories, final responses, and state snapshots at temperature zero. For each judgment, it returns a label, confidence score, supporting evidence, rationale, and review flags. A second exposure judgment is invoked only after a resolved negative initial judgment. Conditional rates are computed over retained-rule episodes with fully judged triggers, while full-cohort adoption includes all assessable triggers. Recoverable tool errors alone do not constitute ATAR; trajectory anomalies are determined from the recorded execution behavior according to case-specific criteria in the evaluation rubric.

Semantic Alignment and Attacker-Goal Change. Following Wang et al. [42], High and Low conditions 11 varied candidate B’s relevance to the requested category in both exposure and trigger records, fixing the U3 promotion rule, evidence scores, and legitimate ranking criterion. The Other goal condition instead requested an unrelated summary, changing both the goal and success predicate. Two GPT-5 agents yielded 240 matched triplets across three conditions (720 chains), enabling within-case comparisons. High versus Low relevance accompanied retention increases of 30.00 and 25.00 points for OpenClaw and Claude Code, and adoption increases of 25.00 and 20.00 points (Figure 6a,d). The matched comparison contains 36 adoption gains versus 6 losses for OpenClaw, and 30 gains versus 6 losses for Claude Code: more decisions shift toward the attacker within the same cases. Other goal yields only 5.00% and 2.50% full-cohort adoption, yet conditional UTDR is 50.00% for both agents. Successful summary substitution necessarily omits the required ranking (Appendix C.1). 

Effect of Exposure-to-Trigger Distance. Whereas Wang et al. [42] varied progress before exposure, we varied normal sessions after one U3 exposure using two Claude Code backbones. Snapshots after zero, two, and four unrelated business sessions received identical trigger inputs, isolating the distance effect. This design directly measures how long injected rules persist. Retention and adoption declined but remained nonzero at distance four (Figure 6b,e). Full-cohort adoption fell by 5.00 points for each backbone. In the fixed initially positive cohorts, conditional ASR fell from 70.00% to 60.00% for GPT-5 and from 62.50% to 50.00% for Gemini-2.5-Flash. Rule loss is non-monotonic: for each backbone, nine adoption-positive outcomes at distance zero become negative at distance four, while three negative outcomes become positive, revealing case-specific rather than uniform decay. 

Cross-Field Cue Consistency. Following Wang et al. [42], we held the W1 malicious body fixed and changed only its applicability field to match or conflict with the target record. Two Claude Code backbones and two conditions yielded 480 chains in 240 matched pairs. Conflict accompanied retention decreases of 20.00 points for both backbones, and full-cohort adoption decreases of 20.00 points for GPT-5 and 15.00 for Gemini-2.5-Flash (Figure 6c,f). Conditional adoption under conflict nevertheless remained 62.50% and 66.67%. Conflict produces 30 adoption losses and 6 gains for GPT-5, and 24 losses and 6 gains for Gemini-2.5-Flash. Illustrative GPT-5 chains reconcile mismatched scope and retain rules adopted in formally complete maintenance plans (Appendix C.3). With the task and the malicious body held fixed, these matched cases trace the rule from a scope cue to retained memory and the final setting, making the decision process directly inspectable. This end-to-end traceability, from one controlled metadata field through retained memory to the final deliverable, distinguishes MemHarmBench’s factor studies from outcome-only attack evaluations. The same matched-pair protocol readily extends to other metadata fields, attack objectives, agent frameworks, and memory backends. Since each pair differs only in the manipulated field, every adoption change isolates that single cue, offering practitioners a precise, reusable diagnostic of which metadata cues an agent actually honors in later sessions.

## 5 Conclusion 

MemHarmBench connects persistent-memory prompt injection to the interests of users, workspace owners, and external stakeholders. Across four configurations and three catalog repetitions, target adoption co-occurs with formal task completion and no judged anomaly at a configuration-averaged frequency of 55.71% among eligible IPI triggers. Execution anomalies occur across all twelve attack objectives. Matched studies show greater retention and adoption for task-relevant targets, partial persistence across normal sessions, and reduced but nonzero adoption under conflicting scope cues. The visual extension also shows retention and later adoption after injected sandbox-page images, despite removal of the original page. Together, these findings motivate checking both the authority of stored rules and the decisions they influence after their original source disappears. This extends Web security assessment from the initial encounter to later decisions made under retained rules. 13 Limitations and Ethical Considerations 

Limitations. All outcomes are decision-level proxies for potential harm, not measurements of real-world consequences. Trigger-stage rates condition on retention, and IPI and DPI subsets differ in case composition, limiting direct comparison. Wilson intervals treat episodes as independent, omitting case and template dependence. Differences in native tools and retained state limit cross-system comparisons and prevent memory-only attribution. Factor contrasts are descriptive; the goal-change condition alters the success criterion itself. Visual tests use one configuration and controlled pages. Judgments use recorded execution evidence and the fixed rubric for each case, keeping every label auditable. 

Ethical Considerations. We use synthetic identities, records and destinations in isolated workspaces, with no real transactions or contact with represented parties. The authors are responsible for the claims, interpretations and evidence. All artifacts are public.
