Abstract
Self-improving embodied agents are increasingly being developed and compared on robot
simulation benchmarks. Several recent works expose the benchmark’s success criteria to the
learning agent, a practice we call Criteria Exposure (CE). These criteria, originally written
to verify policies trained by imitation learning, often demand more than the corresponding
language instructions, so a score from such a verifier may not faithfully reflect whether the
instructed task was completed, especially for robot-control programs written by an agent.
As embodied coding agents report ever higher benchmark scores, a natural question arises:
when a coding agent is exposed to the benchmark’s criteria, does it generate code that better
performs the instructed task, or code that simply overfits to the verification criteria? We conduct
controlled experiments with a self-improving agent with and without CE, and find that with
CE the agent quickly produces code with a higher success rate under the given criteria, but
this advantage disappears when the criteria are changed slightly without departing from the
original instructions. More importantly, the skills the agent develops under CE transfer poorly
across tasks. We further propose instruction-faithful criteria and show that they better reflect
the performance of agent-generated programs.

Introduction
Agents that write code to control robots with multimodal language models complete many manipulation
tasks zero-shot [12, 15, 19, 29], but preserve nothing from one episode to the next, which makes self-
improvement across episodes a natural next step [28, 31, 36]. Such agents are developed and compared
on embodied simulation benchmarks [9, 20, 22] originally built to evaluate end-to-end policies trained
by imitating demonstrations, such as vision-language-action (VLA) models [4, 6, 17, 25] and world
action models (WAMs) [26]. The actual success criteria of these benchmarks often ask for more than the
instruction; in the press_by_number task of RoboDojo, for instance, the criteria require the blue button to be
pressed exactly twice, between two groups of red presses, which the instruction never states (figure 1a).

the instruction, however, lacks this information. Existing self-improving methods therefore expose the
criteria to the agent to different degrees, from rule descriptions [10] and published scoring tables [13, 18]
to numeric thresholds [14], formal goal predicates [32], and the source code of the verifier [3]. A robot in
the real world, by contrast, receives only its instruction and, at most, a success judged by a human or a
VLM. Our research question is: when a self-improving embodied agent is exposed to the success criteria, does the
code it iteratively produces better perform the instructed task, or simply overfit to the verification criteria?
Studies of benchmark validity and of specification gaming show that criteria can demand more than the
instruction states and that agents exploit a verifier they can inspect [16, 37, 40], but we are not aware of
work that measures how much of a reported gain in embodied self-improvement comes from reading the
criteria, or whether it survives when the criteria change. We therefore compare two settings of one fixed
self-improvement loop, in which a frozen coding agent acts and a learner agent turns its episodes into a
library of skills and memory: with Criteria Exposure (w/ CE), the learner reads the criteria; without it
(w/o CE), the learner sees only whether each episode succeeded. We ask three questions: on which tasks
exposure raises the score; whether its lead survives a change of criteria, both of the criteria that judge
the episodes and of the criteria the learner reads; and whether what it adds carries over to new tasks
and to two further benchmarks, LIBERO-PRO and RoboCasa365. To change the criteria, we rewrite them
for the RoboDojo tasks whose criteria ask for more than the instruction, so that they verify only what
the instruction asks. On these tasks every episode is judged by both versions, and in a separate run the
learner reads the rewritten criteria instead of the original ones.
Our experiments reveal three findings. First, under the original criteria, exposure helps only on tasks
whose criteria ask for more than the instruction; when criteria and instruction agree, learning from
success alone does just as well. Second, the score of exposure follows the criteria it read: when the same
episodes are judged by instruction-faithful criteria, its advantage disappears, as the exposed libraries lose
successes while the success-only libraries gain them (figure 1b); conversely, a library learned from the
rewritten criteria scores far lower when judged by the original ones. Third, the lead of exposure does not
reappear on new RoboDojo tasks, and across benchmarks neither the exposed nor the success-only library
gives a clear gain over the empty library; only experience gathered within the target benchmark does.
Taken together, these results suggest a practical principle for evaluating embodied self-improvement: the
learner should receive only the feedback a robot would receive in the real world, and the benchmark’s
criteria should verify what the instruction says. A gain that holds when the criteria are changed but still
agree with the instruction reflects what the agent has learned about the task. Seen this way, whether the
learner reads the criteria is not an implementation detail of a self-improving agent but a choice about

Related Work
Code-writing embodied agents and their benchmarks. Language models plan and act for robots by
selecting skills [1], writing policy code [19], or composing value maps [15]; recent agents close the
loop with code replanning and thin tool layers [12, 13, 29], and frontier models now control robots
zero-shot. These agents are evaluated on benchmarks built for vision-language-action policies trained
on demonstrations [4, 6, 17, 25]: RoboDojo [9], LIBERO and LIBERO-PRO [20, 38], RoboCasa365 [22],
and RoboTwin 2.0 [8]. These works score agents with criteria written for demonstration-trained policies;
whether the criteria verify only what the instruction asks is rarely examined on its own, and we measure
how this gap enters the score of a self-improving agent.
Self-improving agents and the feedback their learners see. In digital domains, programs are evolved
against automatic evaluators [23, 34], agents reflect on textual feedback [28, 36], skills are verified in a
game world [31], and code is revised against tests [33]. Embodied systems differ in how much of the
success criteria their learners or agents see. Some give only success or keep the grader isolated [21, 27];
others add rule descriptions with coarse feedback [10], published scoring tables and partial numeric
thresholds [13, 14, 18], formal goal predicates [32], or the source code of the verifier [3]. In RATs, the
verifier reads goal predicates and ground truth while the policy writer’s side is filtered [35]. In these
systems the amount of criteria information is mostly fixed by design rather than varied as an experimental
factor. The closest comparison is an ablation of EmbodiedEvo between task descriptions alone and
descriptions with the wiki, but both arms are judged by the original criteria [18]. We treat exposure as the
variable and test what it gains under a second set of criteria.
Specification gaming and benchmark validity. Optimizing a proxy diverges from the intended objective [2,
30], with measurable effects of reward misspecification [24]. Language agents exploit tests and evaluators
they can inspect [5, 37], and agentic benchmarks need checks of their own validity [16, 40]. Where rule-
based criteria are missing, success is judged by VLMs [11] or by autonomous real-world evaluation [39].
Building on these studies, we further show that on embodied benchmarks the act of reading the criteria
turns the gap between criteria and instruction into score, and we measure this share with counterfactual
criteria that score the same instructions.
3
Preliminaries and Study Design
This section describes the controlled study we use to analyze what a self-improving embodied agent learns
from the success criteria. The study compares two settings of one loop, w/ CE and w/o CE, and answers
three questions: where exposure raises the score under the original criteria, whether the advantage holds
when the criteria change, and whether the library carries over to new tasks and benchmarks. We describe
in turn the loop and its two settings, criteria-limited tasks, instruction-faithful criteria, and the evaluation
protocol.
3.1
Self-Improvement Loop and Criteria Exposure

3.2
Criteria-limited Tasks
We call a task criteria-limited if 𝐶𝜏disagrees with 𝑆𝜏on some trajectories, either by rejecting a trajectory 𝑆𝜏
accepts or by accepting one 𝑆𝜏rejects (criteria looser than the instruction); all other tasks are faithful. Such
tasks appear in every benchmark we examined, RoboDojo, LIBERO [20] (whose instructions and goals
LIBERO-PRO keeps), RoboCasa365 and RoboTwin 2.0 [8], and their disagreements fall into a few kinds.
The criteria may ask for more than the instruction states, such as an order, a count or an end state: in
RoboDojo, press_by_number requires a button protocol its instruction never gives. They may be stricter
than the instruction needs, such as an unstated tolerance for an object resting on another in LIBERO.
They may contradict the instruction, as in a LIBERO task that asks to stack a bowl on the front bowl
while its criteria require the back one. Or they may verify less than the instruction asks: in RoboTwin 2.0,
shake_bottle_horizontally succeeds once the bottle is lifted.
4


We rewrite criteria and re-judge episodes on RoboDojo only. Its criteria report a score and stages besides
success, so an exposed learner reads the most complete information there, and we compared the criteria
of all of its tasks with their instructions; rewriting the criteria of other benchmarks and repeating these
experiments on them is left to future work. We distinguish seven types: hidden rules, hard thresholds,
timing and irreversible events, criteria defects, extra end states, loose criteria, and contradictions. In
RoboDojo, after a manual review of every task, the 20 criteria-limited tasks have 26 variants, and hidden
rules and hard thresholds are the most common types. Criteria-limited is defined with respect to the
instruction the agent receives at reset; the RoboDojo website publishes part of these rules in its task
descriptions and scoring tables.
3.3
Instruction-faithful Criteria
For each criteria-limited task we rewrite only the criteria code, never the instruction, and leave the original
repository untouched: hidden rules are removed or relaxed, defects fixed, hard thresholds relaxed to
what still clearly satisfies the instruction, loose criteria tightened where the simulator state can detect the
difference, and the return of both arms kept. The two criteria judged the same episodes independently,
and every episode whose verdict flipped was checked by hand without finding a misjudgment by the
new criteria. Each evaluation episode is then judged by both criteria, giving SRorig and SRfaith. Since both
criteria score the same instruction on the same episode, any difference between the two success rates
comes from what the criteria verify, not from behavior. 𝐶faith is ours and serves as a proxy for 𝑆𝜏; we
discuss this in section 5.
3.4
Experiment Settings and Evaluation Protocol
To isolate the effect of exposure, the two settings of each comparison use the same actor, primitives, round
budget and development layouts. The 25 learned tasks comprise 16 criteria-limited and 9 faithful tasks,
grouped in advance by whether their criteria were rewritten; Appendix A.4 lists the tasks. Development
uses layouts 0–4 of seed 1 and testing layouts 0–19 of seed 0. We report the success rate (SR) and compare
settings layout by layout. All experiments use the same actor and learner. To reuse libraries, we distill the
libraries of ten source tasks into one shared library and apply it to disjoint target tasks, in RoboDojo and
on two further benchmarks with aligned primitives.
We use three simulated benchmarks with different roles. RoboDojo is the main benchmark: its rule-based
criteria report a score and stages, and all three questions are answered on it. LIBERO-PRO is a single-arm
tabletop benchmark where zero-shot success is already near the ceiling; it tests whether transfer still has
room at high scores. RoboCasa365 has household kitchen scenes and the lowest zero-shot scores; it tests
cross-benchmark experience and also hosts in-benchmark learning.
4
Experiments and Analysis
We test three hypotheses:
• H1. Exposure raises the score only on tasks whose criteria ask for more than the instruction (section 4.1).
• H2. If exposure teaches the verifier rather than the task, its lead shrinks or reverses when the same
episodes are judged by instruction-faithful criteria (section 4.2.1), and each exposed library scores best
under whichever criteria its learner read (section 4.2.2).
• H3. What exposure adds does not carry over to new tasks or benchmarks (section 4.3).
4.1
Exposure under the Original Criteria
We first judge the benchmark’s way, by the original criteria, which are also the criteria the exposed learner
reads.

over five rounds on five development layouts, and test each library on layouts 0–19 of seed 0 against the
empty library. The two groups were fixed before any result was seen.

Table 1 and figure 3 show three patterns. First, learning from success alone doubles success,

4.1.2
Synthesis
• Exposure pays off only where the criteria ask for more than the instruction. Richer feedback is often
assumed to make self-improvement better. Our results show a condition: when the criteria verify what
the instruction says, success alone tells the learner what the task requires, and what remains, a reliable
procedure, is learned equally well in both settings. Exposure helps only with rules that the instruction
omits and that failures do not reveal.
• The advantage of exposure measures the benchmark as much as the agent. Because it comes from
what the instruction leaves out, the gain of exposure grows with the gap between a benchmark’s
instructions and its criteria. Some of what the criteria reveal is legitimate task knowledge, but it belongs
in the instruction: benchmarks that state in the instruction what their criteria verify (counts, order, end
states, the reset of the arms) and release versioned criteria with a comparison against the instructions
would let self-improvement be compared on the task alone.

If exposure teaches the task, its lead should survive under 𝐶faith; if it teaches the verifier, the lead should
shrink or reverse, and each library should score best under whichever criteria its learner read. We change
the criteria used for judging and the criteria that are read.

4.2.3
Synthesis and Implications
• Exposure makes self-improvement fit the criteria rather than learn the instructed task. A higher score
from reading the criteria is commonly taken as faster learning of the task. A learner that reads the
criteria, however, revises the library until it passes the verifier it reads, so the library learns the task
only as far as the criteria agree with the instruction. Under instruction-faithful criteria the exposed
library loses successes that the success-only library gains, and each exposed library scores best only
under the criteria it read: on press_by_number it solves all 20 layouts under 𝐶orig and none under 𝐶faith,
and the library that read 𝐶faith solves 65.0% of its test episodes under 𝐶faith but 13.5% under 𝐶orig.
7


Self-improvement should therefore be measured without exposure by default, with the success rate
under instruction-faithful criteria reported alongside: a robot in the real world has no criteria code, so
the part of a gain that survives a change of criteria is the part to expect there.
• Exposure changes what the learner writes down. A learner that reads the criteria can explain a failure
by the condition it violated, so its experience is phrased in the terms of one verifier; a learner that sees
only success has to find a physical cause, and its rules are closer to regularities of the robot. About a
third of the exposed memory entries cite the criteria code, and none of the success-only ones do.

4.3.3
Synthesis and Implications
• What exposure adds is specific to the task it was learned on. One might expect the setting that scores
higher on its own tasks to transfer better. It does not: the lead of exposure disappears on target tasks.
Exposed experience carries the conditions and precision needed to pass one task’s criteria, which
become noise or overhead elsewhere, whereas rules about the gripper hold across tasks.
• Experience transfers where the bottleneck is shared. Within a benchmark the robot, the scenes and
perception stay the same, and learned rules carry over. Across benchmarks the general part of the
experience is already covered by the target’s prompt, while what the target lacks has no counterpart in
the source, so the transferred skills go unused; experience gathered on the target benchmark, even on
other tasks, addresses its own bottleneck and helps.

5
Limitations and Future Work
The instruction-faithful criteria are ours, and the feedback is simulated. 𝐶faith serves as our proxy for
the success the instruction asks for, 𝑆𝜏. Neither setting’s libraries saw the rewritten criteria when they
were written; rules and types were fixed in advance; a second seed was judged independently and every
flipped episode checked. Still, only RoboDojo’s criteria were rewritten, and success-only feedback in
simulation is cleaner than the success a human or a VLM reports for a robot in the real world, which has
no criteria code. Next steps are an independent review, released criteria code, rewritten criteria for a
second benchmark, and VLM success detection in place of the simulator’s success [11].
Exposure is studied at two levels, as current systems implement it.
Most existing systems sit at
intermediate levels (rule descriptions, published scoring tables, partial thresholds), which we did not
study separately. A next step is to expose these levels one at a time and measure how the gain under
𝐶orig, and its loss under 𝐶faith, grow with what the learner reads.
Samples are small and the mechanisms are inferred. Each task has 20 test layouts, so quantitative claims
use pooled results and single tasks are described only qualitatively; and distillation variance was not
estimated. The accounts of what exposure writes into the library, of what carries over and of how the actor
uses transferred libraries rest on entry text, skill calls and action statistics, not on ablations. Next steps are
repeated distillations and ablations that remove or swap individual entries, such as the re-measuring
entries of the distilled w/ CE library or the memory entries of a translated library.
6
Conclusion
In this paper, we studied what a self-improving embodied agent learns when it reads the success criteria,
by comparing one self-improvement loop with and without Criteria Exposure, under the benchmark’s
own and instruction-faithful criteria, and on new tasks and benchmarks. Our results show that self-
improvement from success alone is real and survives a change of criteria, whereas the score that Criteria
Exposure adds comes mainly from satisfying the criteria that were read: it appears only where the
criteria and the instruction disagree, disappears under instruction-faithful criteria, and across benchmarks
neither the exposed nor the success-only library gives a clear gain, while only in-benchmark experience
helps. These findings provide a more careful reading of reported self-improvement gains on embodied
benchmarks and practical guidance for measuring them: use the feedback a robot receives in the real
world, and judge by criteria that verify what the instruction says. A robot in the real world never sees its
verifier; the agent we measure should not either.

Appendix
A
Implementation Details
A.1
Experimental protocol
Benchmark. RoboDojo’s simulation track uses a dual-arm ARX-X5 with RGB observations only; its 42
tasks span five capability dimensions. The 20 tasks on which the zero-shot actor solves fewer than half of
the episodes of the official evaluation seed, with five stronger ones, make the 25 compared tasks (table 4).
Every run uses one version of RoboDojo (Table 3); task source, criteria, leaderboard script and evaluation
layouts are identical file by file across the checkouts we compare.
Conditions. The actor’s model, primitives and prompt are the same in every condition. The learner with
Criteria Exposure reads the criteria code, each episode’s score and its passed stages; the one without sees
full trajectories and success only. All conditions are compared over five rounds of improvement (rounds
0–5). The groups (Appendix B) rest on reading the code, the learners’ diagnoses and the failed rollouts.
Layouts. Development reruns layouts 0–4 of official evaluation seed 1 every round; the test layouts are
layouts 0–19 of seed 0 (500 episodes per condition), which no learner sees.
Statistics. Development batches are noisy: rerunning one library on one layout changes the verdict in
4% of cases, and the two round-0 batches of the same empty library differ in at least 13% of episodes. We
therefore draw conclusions from the test layouts and pooled curves, and read single-task development
curves only qualitatively.
A.2
Implementation and settings
Actor. GPT-6 Astra writes one code cell per MLLM call against PyRUA-Lean’s frozen robo API [29]: RGB
perception (SAM 3 [7] and multi-view measurement) and motion primitives, with no VLA policy, depth or
simulator state. The library is installed as a package library/: cells import its skills, and its live memory
entries are appended to the prompt.
Library. A skill is a function of robo that imports only standard modules, numpy and other library modules,
does no I/O and returns {"ok": ..., "why": ..., ...}. A memory entry is ## {task}-<stage>-<nnn>
followed by stage, symptom, applies, do, status and evidence. A task’s library holds at most 40,000
characters, all of which the actor reads.
Learner. Claude Code reads the session, the task’s dev runs, the API source and, with Criteria Exposure,
the task’s criteria; it writes only the candidate library, its verification rollouts and its notes. Without
Criteria Exposure, criteria, scores and stages are removed from the evidence.
Acceptance and rollback. A candidate may change only the task’s own directory, must keep every
memory entry identifier, must cite a judged rollout or dev batch for each entry newly marked dev_verified,
and must pass the static skill rules and the size limit; none of this looks at success, and no candidate was
refused. The active revision rolls back to the best earlier complete batch when a complete batch has two
or more fewer successes; ties go to the batch that passed more stages, and a batch with an episode lost to
infrastructure neither triggers a rollback nor becomes the best.
A.3
Evaluation details
The 25 learned tasks are grouped by whether their criteria were rewritten. Layouts that the benchmark
marks unstable are replaced by the next layout in the official order. Under dual judging an episode ends
early only when both criteria have reached a verdict, so neither verdict is truncated.

B
Comparing Criteria with Instructions in RoboDojo
Criteria mechanism.
RoboDojo criteria are sequences of stages: check stages in order, final_check at
termination, and trigger_check, evaluated only at the step its condition becomes true; query requires
exact counts and fails the episode for good once a count is exceeded; conditions nest with alternating
AND and OR. Of the 42 tasks, 33 require both arms to return within 15 cm and 20° of home, while only 5
instructions ask for a reset.
Method.
For each task we compare gen_instruction, run_reward, get_score and the configuration, grade
the severity as high, medium or low, and confirm by hand; the review is mainly a static reading of the
code.
Types.
The type names are ours.
• Hidden rule: the criteria verify conditions the instruction does not state (order, counts, relative position),
e.g. the button protocol of press_by_number or the tile to draw in make_kong.
• Hard threshold: the tolerance is stricter than clearly satisfying the instruction requires, e.g. 97% of the
liquid in pour_liquid_into_cup.
• Timing, event triggers and irreversibility: verification at one step only, or permanent failure once an event
happens, e.g. pouring judged only when the bottle returns upright; a third press in press_by_number.
• Criteria defect: the criteria code or an asset annotation is wrong, e.g. mug 14 in hang_mugs_random, the
bug in solve_equation.
• Extra end state: an end state the instruction does not mention, e.g. in make_toast the two remaining slices
must stay on the bread shelf.
• Loose criteria: trajectories that skip the action the instruction asks for are accepted, e.g. sweep_blocks
accepts blocks picked up without the broom.
• Contradiction: the criteria require the opposite of the instruction, e.g. in classify_objects_by_language
the categories are named after asset folders, so a chocolate bunny counts as a wooden toy.

What Exposure Writes into the Library
Setup.
We classify the memory entries of the ten source libraries of the RoboDojo transfer (section G),
for both settings, by whether they cite the criteria code, and compare entries that the two settings wrote
from the same source failures.
Results.
Two patterns emerge. First, an exposed library writes its experience in the language of the
criteria: 19 of the 55 exposed entries cite the criteria code (47% of the characters), and none of the 53
success-only entries does. Figure 6 shows three exposed entries: the grip-and-pull of the button cap in
swap_blocks and the ban on sweeping in sweep_blocks_random, which cite the criteria code, and the hidden
protocol of press_by_number, which restates the order of the checks in words.
Such entries fail under 𝐶faith and have to be removed for transfer: distilling on-10 dropped 9 entries
on “what the benchmark checks”. Second, a learner that cannot see the criteria has to find a physical
cause. From the same source failures, in which the gripper closes on nothing, the success-only learner
traced the cause to a half-open gripper whose fingers KEEP moves close again, and wrote “pass OPEN on
every move of an empty gripper”; the exposed learner recorded the symptom and kept a formula that in
effect half-opens the gripper (figure 7). Not all exposed entries concern the verifier, though: some carry
information the instruction should state, such as the pen orientation in fill_pen_holder.

G
Library Transfer
To reuse libraries across tasks, we distill the libraries of ten source tasks into one shared library and
apply it to disjoint target tasks; entries of exposed libraries that are tied to the criteria are removed
during distillation. Across benchmarks, the libraries are applied on LIBERO-PRO and RoboCasa365 with
primitives aligned to those of RoboDojo (Appendix J); within RoboCasa365 we also run both settings of
the loop and distill one library from each, as a reference for in-benchmark experience. In RoboDojo, the
success-only and exposed libraries off-10 and on-10 take ten learned tasks as sources and are applied to
ten target tasks that no source covers (table 2); off-rc10 and on-rc10 are distilled within RoboCasa365 from
both settings of the loop. The case study is in Appendix H.
Brief.
The distiller receives, for each source task, the library as the actor read it in the last evaluated
revision (retired entries removed), the learner’s last diagnosis and the development successes per round,
and the robot API source; it does not see the target tasks and is told not to look for them. It writes
memory entries of at most 12,000 characters in the source format (stage, symptom, applies, do, evidence)
and at most two skill modules of 15,000 characters. Every entry must state a general rule, name no task,
object, dimension or goal configuration in do, and cite evidence from at least two source tasks, or from
one task where it decided between failing and succeeding rounds, flagged as a single-task rule; a separate
notes file, not given to the actor, records the source of every entry and what was left out. The two briefs
differ in one passage on the learners: “they never saw the benchmark’s judging code” (w/o CE) against
“A rule that only describes how one task is judged (its thresholds, its check order) does not transfer; keep
what is about perception and manipulation” (w/ CE).
Process.
Each library is distilled in one session by Claude Code with Claude Opus 5.5 (effort high).
Before evaluation, every library is checked for the character limits, the entry fields, source task names,
and that each library function it names exists and parses; skills are exercised against a stub robot with the
real API signatures, not in simulation.
Results.

sits in insert_tubes and solve_equation (Appendix H).

Why Cross-benchmark Libraries Help Little
Setup.
The libraries off-10 and on-10 of Appendix H are translated to the prompt and primitives of
LIBERO-PRO and RoboCasa365; off-rc10 and on-rc10 are distilled from RoboCasa365 tasks disjoint from
its ten target tasks. At the start of an episode the library is placed in the actor’s working directory: a skill
runs only if the actor’s code imports it, while the memory entries are appended to the prompt. We count
the episodes whose code imports or calls a skill.
Results.
Three reasons explain why neither setting’s library gives a clear gain across benchmarks.
First, the translated skills are almost never called. Even within RoboDojo only 5% (off-10) and 20.5%
(on-10) of the episodes call a skill. None of the 200 RoboCasa365 episodes with off-10 or on-10 calls one,
and on LIBERO-PRO only 5 and 12 of 60 episodes do. The libraries distilled within RoboCasa365 are
21


called in 158 of 200 episodes, mostly their perception functions for two-view localization and joint margins.
Across benchmarks, the memory entries appended to the prompt are the only part of a translated library
the actor uses.
Second, what the translated libraries hold is not what the targets need. They cover grasping from above,

Third, LIBERO-PRO leaves little room. With the aligned primitives, the empty library already solves 188 of
200 episodes on the four swap suites, and only six of the 40 tasks are not solved on every seed. On four
of these six every library solves 8–10 of 10 seeds; the differences come from two tasks, putting a book
into the back compartment of a caddy (empty, off-10, on-10: 6 / 8 / 5 of 10) and putting a mug into the
microwave and closing it (2 / 3 / 3). The same library solves different numbers on the two halves of the
seeds (empty 21 / 24, off-10 22 / 26, on-10 25 / 21, of 30 each), a spread larger than the gain of any library.
Interpretation.
Transfer helps where source and target share the bottleneck. Within RoboDojo the robot,
the table and perception stay the same, and off-10 gains on target tasks. Across benchmarks, the general
part of the experience (grasping, placing, finishing) is what the target’s prompt and the actor already
handle, and what the target lacks, moving the base and operating articulated parts, has no counterpart in
the source. A library distilled on the target benchmark, even from other tasks, covers that bottleneck,
is called in most episodes, and helps. Two caveats apply: a skill that is never called does not mean the
library has no effect, since its memory entries are always in the prompt; and we evaluated one way of
translating a library, not variants such as skills without memory.
