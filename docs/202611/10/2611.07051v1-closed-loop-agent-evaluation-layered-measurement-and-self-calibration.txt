Closed-Loop Agent Evaluation:
Layered Measurement and Self-Calibration
Ruifeng Ren*, Gun Ahn
Massachusetts Institute of Technology, Cambridge, MA, USA
rayren@alum.mit.edu, guna23@mit.edu
Abstract
Once an agent is live, teams want to know whether it is doing
its job, not just whether it throws errors. Yet most evaluation is
one-off: a benchmark run, a look at traces, a single score. Its
conclusions neither check themselves nor reach the agent. We
propose closed-loop evaluation, an evaluation method for de-
ployed agents. It models evaluation as performance estimation
under biased raters and censored outcomes, and it requires ev-
ery level’s conclusion to trace back to atomic data and to be
computed by versioned rules. The method covers four levels
of analysis (span, trace, conversation and engagement), mea-
sures along a spine of Execution, Substance, Trajectory and Re-
sults, treats Conduct as a constraint throughout, and uses Prove-
nance and Adjudication components to certify the evidence and
the evaluators. Evaluator agents generated by an Agent Factory
carry these components, each with a domain knowledge pack
and an adapter harness. Two loops close the evaluation: an inner
loop audits the evidence, rules and metrics themselves, and an
outer loop uses per-component regression checks to drive agent
improvement. We also give a pipeline that runs this process au-
tomatically. Using the business-analysis agent SharpInsight as
a case and porting the method to public conversation data, we
show how problems differ across levels and how evidence-and-
rule judgment is more reliable than holistic scoring (ICC 0.86
vs. 0.81; κ 0.81 vs. 0.63).

Introduction
Enterprises increasingly deploy LLM agents the way they
staff a role: the agent works independently, faces customers
directly and operates under a specification (its system
prompt). Yet it is still evaluated like software, with offline
benchmarks, trace inspection or an LLM judge deciding
whether an answer is good. These practices share two flaws.
First, evaluation is open-loop: once a score is computed the
process ends, nobody checks whether it can be trusted, and
nothing ensures it changes the agent. Second, judgment is a
black box: an LLM returns a score without showing what it
consists of, and another model may conclude differently.
Our approach is to review an agent the way an organiza-
tion reviews an employee. Nobody reviews an employee with
one person’s overall score. A review examines attendance
records, work product, client feedback and business results
against explicit standards, and it calibrates the raters. Person-
nel research has long found that a rater’s own tendencies can
explain more of the variance in ratings than the performance
being rated (Scullen, Mount, and Goff 2000), and that reward-
ing proxies distorts behavior (Kerr 1975; Holmström and Mil-
grom 1991). Agent evaluation faces the same problems, at a

scale that demands automation.
We make four contributions: (1) a closed-loop evaluation
method comprising a measurement model, four levels of
analysis, seven components and two loops; (2) an architec-
ture that separates atomic data acquisition from determin-
istic analysis, with data-level audits; (3) an Agent Factory
pipeline that makes the loop run automatically; and (4) em-
pirical runs on a real agent and a public dataset showing how
the method operates and ports.

The Closed-Loop Evaluation Method
Measurement model and levels. Analysis runs at four lev-
els: a span is one model or tool call, a trace is the span tree
of one turn, a conversation is a session, and an engagement
is a user’s conversations and outcome events within horizon
T. Higher levels sit closer to user value, but their evidence
is sparser and later. For unit x, the output of evaluator e is
modeled as

bias, and the output is visible only when an observation mask
Me(x) = 1. The model yields three requirements. A single
rater confounds be with θ, so multiple calibrated raters are
needed. Pooling segments with different ge estimates a popu-
lation that does not exist, so evidence must be certified before
scoring. And M is not missing at random, since users who fail
silently leave first, so unobserved outcomes cannot count as
successes.
Seven components. The method sets four spine stages
along the path of value creation (Figure 1): Execution, Sub-
stance, Trajectory and Results. Conduct runs throughout,
since an agent can succeed at every stage and still overstep.
Provenance certifies the evidence and Adjudication certifies
the evaluators; neither sits on the spine, which would confuse
the instrument with the object measured. Table 1 gives each
component’s atomic data and analysis, independent of any
business domain.

Clauses, censoring and admission. A specification com-
piles into clauses r = (φt, ψ) of a trigger and an obligation.
Rules detect triggers and LLMs only acquire the atomic da-
tum of whether the obligation is met, with evidence, yielding
applicability A(r) = P(φt) and conditional compliance C(r)
= P(ψ | φt). For unseen outcomes we do not impute satisfac-
tion: the Results stage supplies delayed labels (return, gap,
and redo, defined by character-bigram Jaccard ≥0.6 between
successive requests), and sessions with no return within the
horizon are marked censored. Audits work at the data level:
replacing ye in the measurement model with data item aj,e,
each data item must pass cross-model agreement to enter f,
and metrics must also pass criterion validity, discriminant
validity and a Goodhart test (Goodhart 1975), under which
a metric must not improve when a lower stage degrades.
Two loops. The inner loop acts on evaluation itself: when
a test fails, the data definition, rule, evidence scope or metric
changes, the version changes, results are recomputed, and fail-
ing data items leave the analysis. The outer loop acts on the
agent: findings become fixes, and a new version must pass re-
gression checks on every component, with zero tolerance for
invariants and no significant decline elsewhere, before ramp-
ing up, and new traﬀic enters the next round. Components are
reported side by side, not as a weighted composite, to avoid
multitask distortion.

Span level. Table 2 summarizes the four levels. Syn-
chronous turns contain 22,884 spans, 3.31 per turn. Logs
record step type and order but not per-step time, so the ba-
sic data item is step-chain completeness. In normal mode,
turns without the thinking step fail 15.7% of the time against
0.0% with it; without retrieval, synchronous turns fail 21.6%
against 1.0%. Of abnormal deep-task stops, 85% occur at or
before 20% progress, at a median of 60 s against 15 min
for completed tasks, so trouble concentrates in task start-up.
These chain anomalies accompany failure and are not neces-
sarily its cause. Context reconstruction runs on 91% of syn-
chronous follow-up turns; among normal-mode turns with re-
trieval, median latency is 58 s with it against 48 s without.
Trace level. Delivery fails on 3.6% of turns, highest in
deep mode (8.0%). Latency baselines must be per mode (nor-

dicator is at the other end: normal answers within 5 s fail
38.7% of the time, against 0.0–0.2% between 10 and 120 s.

Conversation level. Sessions with a failure are 5.6%, ris-

Engagement level. 10.3% of users met a failure. When a
session ends in failure, the next session is a redo in 38.6%

The levels differ clearly. The same failures affect 3.6%
of turns, 5.6% of sessions and 10.3% of users: small low-
level rates accumulate upward. Each level also sees only its
own problems: spans locate which step broke but not whether
users suffered, while engagement shows redo but not which
step caused it.

4 How the Loop Runs
These results are not the product of one pass but of the loop’s
successive corrections.
Inner loop: evaluation corrects itself. The audit applies
the measurement model to data. Treating Opus and Sonnet as
two raters on the 259 delivered turns, 85.8% of the variance
in S comes from the responses, systematic rater bias is about
zero and the residual is 14.2%, giving a single-rater ICC of
0.86 and a two-rater jury of 0.92. By contrast, direct 1–5 LLM
scoring on the same turns has a residual of 19.0% and an ICC
of 0.81. Trajectory outcomes tell the same story: rule-derived
from atomic data, cross-model κ = 0.81; as direct LLM labels,
only 0.63. This is the value of separating acquisition from
analysis.

and conclusions. Evidence scope is the clearest. To control
cost, the first pass kept only the head and tail of long an-

Outer loop: per-component regression. The outer loop
requires a new version to pass regression checks on every
component. Before the gate meets a real release, two ques-
tions need answers: does it raise false alarms when nothing
changed, and how large a regression can it catch? Two offline
experiments test the gate itself. Both randomize by user and
avoid any time split (Table 4).
A/A test. We split all users at random into two halves 4,000
times (Kohavi, Tang, and Xu 2020). The halves have no real
difference, so any verdict is a false alarm. A per-turn test (|z|
≥2) raises at least one false alarm in 98% of splits, because
turns from one user are not independent and a few heavy users
can move a rate. The gate therefore uses a user-clustered test,
with per-metric thresholds calibrated on half the splits. On the
held-out half, the seven metrics together false-alarm 4.5% of
the time, within the 5% target.
Seeded defects. On one half of the users we rewrite the
logged turns (blanking answers, resending the previous re-
quest, inserting a disclosure sentence or template) and recom-
pute with the same rules; latency is scaled up. Each defect
runs 300 times at each strength. At 80% detection, the gate
catches a rise of 3.2 points in failure, 2.1 in disclosure and
1.5 in template misfires, and a 46% latency increase, but it
is insensitive on replay (18.1 points) and replay after failure
(35.5 points). Defects rarely spill across components: Trajec-
tory and Conduct defects flag other components at most 2%
of the time. The exception is that injected failures change the
denominator of replay after failure, which is flagged in about

From findings to fixes. Each finding becomes a fix bound
to an acceptance metric. The case yields three: clarify very
short inputs before launching a report, accepted on the failure
rate of 1–5 character prompts (7.3%); tighten template trig-

5 Porting to Other Agents
The method’s generality comes from its layering. The mea-
surement model, levels, components, loops and admission
rules stay fixed, and a port replaces three things: the specifica-
tion sets clauses, the knowledge pack sets baselines, lexicons
and data definitions, and the harness sets which logs and
outcome events connect. The deterministic analysis f mostly
carries over. Take Substance and Results: a customer-service
agent would check whether replies cite policy and issues get
resolved; a coding agent, whether tests pass and code is not
reverted; a data-analysis agent, whether definitions are stated
and reports are adopted. These mappings are reasoned, not
yet tested one by one.
We also ran a test on public data, applying the Trajec-
tory acquisition prompt unchanged to 60 Chinese WildChat
conversations (164 turns; Zhao et al. 2024) from a general-
purpose assistant, a different domain and user base. Acqui-
sition ran without modification, and the same rules gave 17
smooth, 17 struggled, 3 blocked and 23 censored, whereas
direct LLM labels left 50 unknown. Keyword rules did not
transfer: replay rule precision fell from 0.94 to 0.75 and re-
call from 0.46 to 0.21. This marks the boundary of porting:
atomic data acquisition and deterministic analysis trans-
fer, keyword rules must pass the audit again, and the Re-
sults stage must connect to new event sources.
6 Making the Loop Run Automatically
Closed-loop evaluation only makes sense when automated.
People can run one audit; they cannot do it continuously for
every turn and version. The key is an orchestrator agent that
organizes the Agent Factory’s whole pipeline (Figure 6).
Five steps. (1) Trigger: a new trace starts incremental eval-
uation, a specification or version change starts recomputation,
and a schedule starts the audit. (2) Ingest and clean: the har-
ness normalizes logs, and Provenance segments traﬀic and
checks evidence coverage. (3) Acquire and analyze: on a
cache miss, rules or LLMs acquire data through the cascade
in parallel, and deterministic rules compute each level’s re-
sults. (4) Automatic audit: a sample goes to a second model
for re-acquisition, agreement and validity are computed, and
failing data items leave the analysis and trigger a definition
rewrite. (5) Automatic action: new versions are compared
component by component, invariant breaches open incidents,
and frequent defects become proposed fixes.
How the factory builds evaluators. The orchestrator
reads the role specification and system prompt, generates data
definitions, evidence scopes and output schemas, writes de-
terministic rules as versioned code, and rewrites them when
the audit fails. People define the component split and invari-

Where people stay. People keep three decisions: defining
invariants, approving specification changes and reviewing au-
dit samples. The current implementation is still offline batch
processing. Online triggers, gold sets and the release gate are
not yet connected to production, and outer-loop fixes have not
been validated through a real release.
7 Discussion
What the method changes. Closed-loop evaluation turns
evaluation from a one-off score into a running control system,
and separating acquisition from analysis keeps that system
explainable and auditable. The case’s most serious problems
sit not in infrastructure but in job design: one or two charac-
ters can launch a report of over ten thousand characters, and
deep mode rewards length over consistency. Such problems
surface only through layered measurement and are fixed only
through the outer loop. Just as important, the method can over-
rule itself: metrics were retired and conclusions withdrawn,
which open-loop evaluation cannot do.
Limitations. The case covers one agent and one language,
and the judged sample is small. Checklists mostly used
clipped text, and full-text checks cover only 20 deep reports.
Form items in S nearly saturate, limiting its discrimination,
and some data items still need better agreement. Agreement
between models does not mean correctness, so a human gold
set is the next step, and the κ line is an empirical choice. Re-
tention net of redo removes exactly the returns more com-
mon after failure, so part of the reversal comes from the def-
inition, and the two intervals overlap. Execution rules have
recall of only 0.33, so rule-based failure rates may be un-
derstated. Seeded defects assume random occurrence across
users, while real defects may cluster in some scenarios. Our
contrasts are observational, and causality needs controlled ex-
periments (Kohavi, Tang, and Xu 2020; Bickel, Hammel, and
O’Connell 1975).
Relation to prior work. This method complements capa-
bility benchmarks (Yao et al. 2025; Jimenez et al. 2024; Xu
et al. 2025) and their methodological critiques (Kapoor et al.
2025, 2026; Yehudai et al. 2026). It carries LLM and agent
judges (Zheng et al. 2023; Wang et al. 2024; Zhuge et al.
2025; Shankar et al. 2024) and failure diagnosis (Cemri et
al. 2025; Zhang et al. 2025) into production, and it draws on
implicit-satisfaction research (Hassan et al. 2013; Lin et al.
2024; Rodden, Hutchinson, and Fu 2010), atomic fact check-
ing (Min et al. 2023) and ML-ops practice (Sculley et al.
2015). Conduct draws on alignment research (Bai et al. 2022;
Sharma et al. 2024) and the distinction between task and con-
textual performance (Borman and Motowidlo 1993).
8
Conclusion
Evaluating an agent, like reviewing an employee, takes more
than one judge and one score. We proposed closed-loop eval-
uation: every level’s conclusion is computed by rules from
traceable evidence, an inner loop calibrates evaluation itself,
an outer loop drives agent improvement, and an orchestra-
tor agent links these steps into an automated pipeline. The
method’s levels, components and loops are not tied to a spe-
cific domain. We tested its core components on SharpInsight,
checked the portability of some on public conversation data,
and tested the outer-loop gate offline; the real effect of the
fixes and the method’s performance on other agent types
await real releases and further study. Evaluation is finished


only when it can correct itself and change the agent. That is
what closing the loop means.
