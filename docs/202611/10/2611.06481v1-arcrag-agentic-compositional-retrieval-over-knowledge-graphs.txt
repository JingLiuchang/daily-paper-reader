Abstract
Graph-based retrieval-augmented generation
(GraphRAG) answers questions by retrieving
over a knowledge graph built from a corpus.
Yet retrieval strategies are designed around the
structure their own construction pipeline pro-
duces, and whether they survive a change of
construction is rarely tested. We study this
cross-construction portability: whether one re-
trieval procedure, with no construction-specific
schema, relation names, or retuning, stays effec-
tive when the graph is built differently. We in-
troduce ArcRAG, which induces a query-local
graph interface at inference time (the node la-
bels, relation descriptors, and property keys
reachable from the entities a question names),
plans multi-hop traversals against it, and exe-
cutes and repairs them as Cypher. We build
each of the MuSiQue, HotpotQA, and Trivi-
aQA corpora as a schema-guided graph (typed
nodes, a closed relation set, source prove-
nance) and as an open-information-extraction
graph (untyped nodes, free-text relations), with
the retriever’s configuration fixed across them.
We compare against a construction-agnostic
Direct-Cypher agent, ablate each stage across
retry and pass budgets on both graph families,
and compare with results reported for prior
GraphRAG systems, most of which design re-
trieval together with their own graph construc-
tion. Across the two constructions, we find
that retrieval need not be co-designed with the
graph: query-time interface induction keeps a
construction-agnostic retriever effective when
the construction changes, although construc-
tion still shapes absolute accuracy.

Introduction
Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) grounds a
language model in an external corpus by retriev-
ing information pieces (passages) most similar to a
query and conditioning generation on them. Simi-
larity ranking is sufficient when the answer is con-
*Corresponding author.
tained in a single passage, and insufficient when the
answer must be composed across several. Consider
a question of the form which member of a given
film’s cast has two children who attended a partic-
ular university: answering it requires enumerating
a set, testing a property of each of its elements,
and verifying a constraint that no individual pas-
sage asserts. Top-k alone cannot perform these
operations. Graph-based RAG addresses this by re-
trieving over a knowledge graph (KG) rather than a
flat collection of passages, so that the relations con-
necting evidence are part of what retrieval returns.
Composition, though, is only half of what these
settings demand: in regulated domains — finance,
law, healthcare, compliance — questions are sel-
dom one-hop, and an answer must also be traceable
to the specific disclosure, clause, or record it rests
on. Success in these settings is evidence returned
together with the path that connects it, rather than
the k passages nearest a query.
Dense embedding retrieval, the workhorse of
naive RAG, cannot supply that, and the reason is
structural rather than a matter of scale. Each pas-
sage is represented independently, while a multi-
hop question depends on the relations among pas-
sages.
As corpora grow and reasoning chains
lengthen, the relevant facts remain individually
retrievable yet become relationally invisible: a
vector index surfaces the constituent pieces with-
out representing the path that joins them, leav-
ing the generator to reconstruct those connections
from semantically similar text amid substantial sur-
rounding noise. FinReflectKG–MultiHop demon-
strates that this is not a deficiency stronger models
will absorb: even reasoning-oriented models per-
form better given precisely KG-linked evidence
than given text-only page windows that contain the
same facts in less structured context (Arun et al.,
2025b). Knowledge graphs close this representa-
tional gap directly. Typed edges turn “which entity
connects to which” into a traversable structure, and

the traversal followed during retrieval can itself
serve as provenance. This is the insight behind
GraphRAG (Edge et al., 2025) and the class of
graph-based retrieval systems it inspired, and it re-
duces the open problem to a single question: given
a knowledge graph, how should one retrieve over
it?
The difficulty in answering that question is that a
knowledge graph is not one kind of object. Graphs
differ in schema commitment, from open informa-
tion extraction with free-text relation strings to
schema-guided extraction against a closed, typed
relation set, and in whether edges carry provenance
back to a source chunk. Most GraphRAG systems
fix one point in this space and design retrieval for it,
and cross-construction portability is rarely tested
directly.
Instead, each fixes a point in that space and
builds a strategy that presupposes it. Personalized
PageRank presupposes a dense synonymy-linked
entity graph, and returns a ranked passage list rather
than a traversal a user can cite (Gutiérrez et al.,
2025a); GraphRAG’s global search presupposes a
hierarchical Leiden partition and the community
summaries precomputed over it (Edge et al., 2025);
LightRAG’s dual-level retrieval presupposes an
entity-and-relation keyword index and remains a
one-hop lookup (Guo et al., 2025); and PathRAG
selects paths by a heuristic computed once over a
precomputed path index, with no mechanism for
noticing a wrong turn (Chen et al., 2025). Agen-
tic methods loosen the fixed strategy but not the
coupling: LLM-guided traversal over graphs (Sun
et al., 2024; Luo et al., 2024; Jiang et al., 2023;
Jin et al., 2024) proceeds against node and edge
semantics known in advance, and the BYOKG line
(Mavromatis et al., 2025; Agarwal et al., 2024a)
queries unfamiliar graphs but pairs exploration with
a per-graph preparation or linking stage rather than
deriving the semantics at query time (§2).
What these strategies share is not a common
weakness but a common coupling: because each
encodes assumptions about the graph it runs on,
retrieval and construction cannot be varied inde-
pendently. Agentic methods that let a language
model walk a graph directly (Sun et al., 2024; Luo
et al., 2024; Jiang et al., 2023; Jin et al., 2024)
loosen the fixed-strategy assumption but not the
coupling: they traverse using node and edge se-
mantics supplied in advance, most often those of
a curated graph with a fixed ontology (§2). The
strategy we describe is specified independently of
construction: one retrieval procedure, applied un-
changed to graphs that differ in edge semantics and
connectivity.
ArcRAG separates graph retrieval into explo-
ration and exploitation.
It reads each graph’s
schema by introspection, which says how the graph
is stored, and an Explorer queries the graph at in-
ference time for a query-local graph interface: the
relation descriptors and paths reachable from the
entities the question names. Exploration is also
prompted to surface aliases and paraphrased rela-
tion descriptors near those entities, so later stages
work over the variants a noisy graph contains with-
out an offline canonicalization pass. A Planner and
a Generator then exploit what was found, choosing
paths and executing them as Cypher, and the search
returns to exploration when the evidence falls short.
No step requires graph-specific prompts, relation
names, or retuning (§4).
Two questions organize the paper, each paired
with the contribution that addresses it.
• RQ1, mechanism. Does inducing a query-
local graph interface and planning executable
traversals against it improve compositional
retrieval over direct Cypher generation, and
how do exploration, planning, and retry and
pass budgets account for the difference?
• RQ2, portability. Can the same retrieval pro-
cedure, with one configuration per dataset and
no graph-specific prompts or relation names,
operate across knowledge graphs produced
by structurally different construction meth-
ods, and what accounts for the accuracy that
changes between them?
Our contributions are the following.
• A formulation of cross-construction portabil-
ity for graph retrieval: whether one retrieval
procedure can operate when relation seman-
tics, typing, and provenance are not fixed by
its construction pipeline (§4).
• ARCRAG, which induces a query-local graph
interface from the target graph at inference
time and plans, executes, and repairs compo-
sitional traversals against it (§4).
• A controlled evaluation: a comparison with
a Direct-Cypher agent, component ablations
across retry and pass budgets on both graph
families, and a paired transfer design over

object of study and the retriever an instrument;
whether a system transfers across constructions

Knowledge Graph Construction
We construct graphs over the same corpora us-
ing two extraction methodologies, chosen because
they differ along three of the axes identified in §1:
schema commitment, relation vocabulary, and con-
nectivity. Both are existing published pipelines
rather than contributions of this work, applied un-
modified except where a corpus requires it, and
neither is presupposed by the retrieval harness. The
order below is expositional and implies no prece-
dence.
Schema-guided construction.
We apply FinRe-
flectKG’s pipeline (Arun et al., 2025a), in which an
extraction agent proposes triples chunk by chunk
and a reflection pass checks each proposal for faith-
fulness to its source chunk before committing it,
to MuSiQue, HotpotQA, and TriviaQA. Two adap-
tations are inherently corpus-specific. In place of
table-aware chunking we chunk at the passage level,
following each corpus’s existing paragraph segmen-
tation so that chunk boundaries align with the units
the hybrid dense and BM25 retriever already in-
dexes. In place of the business-defined disclosure
schema — segments, risk factors, related parties
— we extract against an LLM-constructed schema
suited to encyclopedic text. The extraction-and-
commit reflection loop is otherwise the same loop
used for the financial 10-K graph. The resulting
graphs have typed nodes and a closed relation set.

Methodology
Real-world knowledge graphs are incomplete and
noisy (Paulheim, 2017): one entity is stored un-
der several names, one relation as several predicate
strings, and edges a question needs may be missing;
open information extraction leaves entity and rela-
tion phrases uncanonicalized altogether (Vashishth
et al., 2018). A retriever written against one con-
5


struction’s schema inherits its assumptions about
all three. ArcRAG’s stages can instead be read
through the exploration exploitation trade-off famil-
iar from search (Kocsis and Szepesvári, 2006), al-
though no stage implements a bandit or tree-search
policy. The Explorer explores: it establishes what
the graph contains around the entities a question
names, including their variants, without commit-
ting to an answer. The Planner and Generator ex-
ploit: they commit to the most promising explored
paths and execute them as queries. When the ev-
idence does not support an answer, the search re-
turns to exploration. The budgets reflect the same
trade-off: retries extend exploitation and passes
renew exploration, and how much the Planner’s
guidance adds depends on both the budget and the
graph (Appendix D).
4.1
Setup
The target is a labeled property graph G
=
(V, E, ℓV , ℓE, pV , pE) with node and edge labels
and key–value properties. Its schema SG, the node
labels, relationship types, and property keys, is read
by introspection before any question (line 1); none
is written by hand. SG shows where relations are
stored: in relationship types for a schema-guided
graph, in an edge property for an open-vocabulary
one. We write ρ(e) for the resulting relation de-
scriptor of edge e. For a question q with anchors
A ⊆V , the entities it names, exploration yields the
query-local interface ˆSq: the descriptors and sig-
natures (ti, ρ, tj) observed near A, with the paths
Iq that confirm them. Executed queries return the
witness W, the subgraph connecting the anchors to
an answer. Only provenance depends on the graph
family: whether edges point to source chunks se-
lects the store that seeds retrieval and the evidence
the answer reads (lines 6–10 and 20). Beyond intro-
spection, ArcRAG needs only a hybrid text index
over that store. Algorithm 1 and Table 3 specify
the procedure.
4.2
Explore
Without exploration, a query generator guesses
which entities and relations exist: SG says how the
graph is stored, not what lies near a question. Ex-
ploration supplies this, and canonicalizes at query
time: explorers are prompted to surface candidate
aliases and paraphrased relation descriptors near
the anchors, so later stages reason over the ob-
served variants instead of relying on an offline
canonicalization pass. Phrases from the question
are retrieved by reciprocal rank fusion (Cormack
et al., 2009) of dense and sparse search (line 4) to
obtain anchors (line 5); chunks bound later queries
to the subgraph they induce, while retrieved triples
only hint where to look (lines 6–10). Two explorers
each issue one batch of read-only Cypher queries
(line 11), locating relations through SG. Their re-
port, ˆSq, scales with the exploration budget, not the
graph, and is a per-question view, not an ontology.
Exploration gives the largest gain in our ablations
(Appendix D).
4.3
Plan
The Planner is the exploitation heuristic: it decides
which explored paths are worth executing, so the
Generator need not find them by trial and error.
From SG, ˆSq, and Iq it fixes the answer type and
emits a bounded set of subplans, each a traversal
from anchors it attaches (line 12); a bridge ques-
tion needs one, a comparison or aggregation sev-
eral. One subplan is always an open neighborhood
scan, so some evidence is gathered even if targeted
subplans fail. The plan is fixed for its pass. Its
value depends on the graph and the budget: on the
typed graph it helps most when retries are scarce,
while on the open-vocabulary graph it is neutral
at small budgets and slightly ahead at large ones
(Appendix D).
4.4
Generate
The Generator executes rather than proposes, so
every result can be checked before it reaches the
answer. It compiles each subplan into one read-
only Cypher query over the descriptors and variants
surfaced in ˆSq (line 14). One query chains the
subplan’s hops: an open far end enumerates a set,
the next hop steps across the rows returned, and a
filter applies a constraint no single passage states.
On chunk-backed graphs, queries are scoped to
the retrieved chunks. The first attempt is the least
constrained; an unacceptable result (Table 3) is
refined by revising predicates and bindings while
the traversal shape stays fixed (lines 15–17). This
limits uncontrolled rewrites but does not guarantee
that a correct earlier candidate survives. A subplan
still unacceptable when its budget runs out is left to
the outer loop; acceptable results join the witness
(line 18).
4.5
Answer Synthesis
The witness determines what the answer model
reads (lines 20–21). Where its edges point to source
6


chunks, the answer is composed from those pas-
sages, which keep qualifications a triple drops; oth-
erwise it is composed from the returned rows, at the
coarser grain of an edge. The graph’s provenance,
not a per-experiment choice, decides which applies.
Each answer can be traced to the evidence it was
given and the witness re-executed, though we do
not verify claim-level attribution.
4.6
Retrieve–Answer Loop
The loop is where the search returns to exploration.
Within a pass, an unacceptable result triggers an-
other attempt; across passes, an answer the evi-
dence does not support yields a follow-up question
q′ that seeds a fresh pass (lines 22–23), which can
reach relations or paths the previous one missed.
The witness accumulates across passes, and the
loop stops when the answer is judged sufficient,
no follow-up can be formed, or the cap is reached.
More passes raise accuracy in every ablated config-
uration (Appendix D).
5
Experimental Setup
5.1
Datasets and Samples
We evaluate on three QA benchmarks that dif-
fer in how much composition an answer requires.
MuSiQue (Trivedi et al., 2022) chains up to four
single-hop questions, with the bridging entities ab-
sent from the question text. HotpotQA (Yang et al.,
2018) is predominantly two-hop. TriviaQA (Joshi
et al., 2017) is predominantly single-hop and serves
as a control for whether the harness degrades per-
formance when little traversal is needed. For each
dataset, both graph families are built and frozen
before evaluation (Table 2), and questions are an-
swered end to end, with no gold passages, entities,
or hop counts supplied. Main results follow the
validation-sample convention of HippoRAG and
IRCoT (Gutiérrez et al., 2025a). The component
ablations use a stratified sample of 100 questions
drawn from all three datasets and run on both graph
families.
5.2
Baselines
We compare against two kinds of reference. The
first is results reported for prior GraphRAG systems
(Table 4), most of which design retrieval together
with their own graph construction (§2). The sec-
ond is a Direct-Cypher agent: the Generator alone
with a single attempt and a single pass, compil-
ing Cypher from the question and the introspected
schema with no exploration, planning, refinement,
or follow-up pass. Given more retries and passes,
the Generator alone becomes an ablation of Ar-
cRAG, which we compare with Explorer + Gener-
ator and the full pipeline at each budget.
5.3
Protocol
Within each dataset, the configuration is identical
for both graph families; only the graph and its ac-
companying store change, as determined by prove-

Models, budgets, caps, and retrieval settings are

6.2
Cross-Construction Transfer
The open-vocabulary graphs carry one node la-
bel, one relationship type, and no chunk prove-
nance (§3). With the same configuration, ArcRAG
obtains 38.0 EM on MuSiQue, 65.5 EM on Hot-
potQA, and 71.6 F1 on TriviaQA over them (Ta-
ble 5). Their introspected schema is nearly empty,
so the relation semantics the system uses come
from the query-local interface alone. The schema-
guided graphs score higher by 6.0 EM, 9.0 EM,
and 16.3 F1: portability is not invariance, and be-
cause the two families differ in both structure and
provenance, these gaps reflect their combined ef-
fect. Against HippoRAG and HippoRAG 2 as re-
ported by Bai et al. (2025), the open-vocabulary
row is well above HippoRAG and within 4 points of
HippoRAG 2 on MuSiQue and HotpotQA, higher
on EM and lower on F1. For RQ2, one configura-
tion operates across both constructions and remains
competitive on the one with no relation schema.
6.3
Position Against Reported Results
Table 4 lists, for each prior system, the best figure
its authors report on each dataset, taken across all
models and configurations they evaluated. With the
schema-guided graph, ArcRAG obtains 44.0 EM
on MuSiQue, 74.5 EM on HotpotQA, and 87.9 F1
on TriviaQA, above every figure in the table on ev-
ery metric; the narrowest margin is HotpotQA F1,
80.8 against 79.5 for StepChain GraphRAG. Be-
cause protocols, answer models, and scoring code
differ across sources, this places ArcRAG at the
top of the reported range rather than establishing a
controlled improvement over any one system.
Limitations
This is an early study of cross-construction porta-
bility, and its scope reflects that. We evaluate two
graph families over the same three corpora, chosen
to differ in kind rather than degree: schema-guided
8


extraction with typed nodes, a closed relation set,
and chunk provenance, against open information
extraction with untyped nodes, free-text relations,
and no provenance. Two maximally different con-
structions test portability more severely than sev-
eral similar ones would, but they do not establish
transfer to curated ontologies or to graphs with
different node granularities.
Because the two families differ in both struc-
ture and provenance, the accuracy gap between
them reflects both, and we do not separate the two.
Canonicalization at query time is a design property
of the harness; we do not measure how much noise
or variation it absorbs. Comparisons with prior
GraphRAG systems use their best reported figures,
whose protocols and answer models differ from
ours, rather than reruns under a shared setup. Each
configuration is run once, and the answer model
samples at temperature 1.0 and also judges whether
its own evidence is sufficient, so the results carry
run-to-run variance that we do not quantify.
The benchmarks are public and the answer
model is a large pretrained model, so parametric
knowledge may contribute to absolute scores; com-
parisons that share the answer model limit but do
not remove this effect. Our experiments use ency-
clopedic QA and do not establish reliability for the
regulated domains that motivate traceable retrieval:
an executable witness does not guarantee factual
correctness, and deployment there would require
domain-specific validation and human oversight.
Finally, graph construction still affects accuracy,
so we do not claim that retrieval and construction
never benefit from co-design.
Ethical Considerations
Data.
All experiments use public benchmarks
built from Wikipedia and web text: MuSiQue (CC
BY 4.0), HotpotQA (CC BY-SA 4.0), and Triv-
iaQA (CC BY 4.0). We use them for research
evaluation, consistent with their licenses, and the
knowledge graphs we build from them are derived
artifacts of the same text. These corpora concern
public figures and encyclopedic facts; we collect
no new data from people and process no private
information.
Models.
Agents use gpt-oss-120b (Apache 2.0)
and answers use Nemotron-3-Super-120B-A12B
(NVIDIA Nemotron Open Model License); dense
retrieval uses Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B (Apache
2.0). All are openly released models, used within
the terms of their licenses. Graphs extracted by
language models inherit their errors and biases, as
well as those of the source text, and retrieval over
such a graph can surface them.
Intended use and risks.
Our motivation includes
regulated domains where answers must be trace-
able, but our experiments use encyclopedic QA and
do not establish reliability there. An executable wit-
ness shows how an answer was reached, not that it
is correct, and we do not verify claim-level attribu-
tion. Any deployment in finance, law, healthcare,
or compliance would require domain-specific vali-
dation and human oversight.

B
Additional Implementation Details
All values in this section reflect the pipeline’s
source and configuration as run. The three cor-
pora (MuSiQue, HotpotQA, TriviaQA) are served
by structurally identical pipelines, each built on a
single shared processing loop. The four agents —
Exploration, RootPlanner, Planner, and Generator
— contain no construction-specific names in their
prompts or code. A corpus differs only in its per-
corpus constants (Memgraph connection, Qdrant
collection, retrieval depth), its schema, which is
read from the graph by introspection rather than
written by hand, and its per-corpus context prompt.
B.1
System Overview
Per query, each endpoint runs an iterative retrieve–
answer loop (Algorithm 1), capped at 6 passes:
1. Introspect the graph’s schema (node labels,
relationship types, and property keys) once
per graph, and cache it.
2. Rewrite the query once, before entering the
loop.
3. Loop, each pass running the shared pipeline:
• Exploration — extract search phrases
→hybrid retrieve →deterministic gap
analysis →2 parallel graph explorers.
• Planning — the RootPlanner decom-
poses the query into at most 4 ordered
subplans; the PlannerAgent attaches
each subplan’s binding anchors.
• Generation — the GeneratorAgent
emits one read-only Cypher query per
subplan.
• Execute every subplan’s final Cypher,
render deduplicated chunk context, an-
swer the original query, and self-judge
sufficiency.
• Stop once the answer is judged suffi-
cient, or the proposed follow-up query
is empty; otherwise re-run on the follow-
up query, accumulating context across
passes.
Terminology note. RootPlanner and PlannerA-
gent together implement the Planner stage (PLAN
in Algorithm 1); GeneratorAgent implements the
Generator (COMPILE and REFINE); the two explor-
ers implement EXPLORE; and the answerer imple-
ments COMPOSE and SUFFICIENT. A pass is one
iteration of the outer loop; hops refer only to traver-
sal depth within a subplan’s query. Node-creator
and visualize/report/extractor calls (Table 10) are
internal sub-steps of Generation, not separately
named stages.

Footprint by Graph Family
This comparison regroups Table 12 by graph con-
struction: schema-guided FinReflectKG versus

It describes cost, not accuracy; accuracy by graph

Which family retrieves more context?
The di-
rection is not consistent across corpora:
• MuSiQue — OpenIE retrieves more context
than FinReflectKG: 5,753 vs. 3,282 mean
tokens (∼1.75×).
One possible explana-
tion is that, without typed anchors to narrow
the search, retrieval over the open-vocabulary
graph returns more candidate rows; we have
not tested this.
• HotpotQA — the direction reverses: FinRe-
flectKG retrieves more context than OpenIE:
3,637 vs. 2,449 mean tokens (∼1.49×).
• TriviaQA — FinReflectKG is the outlier by
a wide margin: 32,632 vs. 4,873 mean tokens
(∼6.7×), with an even wider p90 gap (78,394
vs. 12,650). The mean/p90 ratio within Fin-
ReflectKG itself (∼2.4×) is consistent with
a heavy right tail, in which a subset of queries
run toward the iteration cap and accumulate
large context.

D
Component Ablations
Table 13 and Figure 2 vary which stages run and
how much budget they receive, on both graph fam-
ilies. All runs use the same stratified sample of
100 questions: 33 from MuSiQue, 34 from Hot-
potQA, and 33 from TriviaQA. Two budgets are
varied: retries, the number of attempts the Gener-
ator is allowed per query, and passes, the number
of full iterations of the retrieve–answer loop. The
figure plots accuracy against their product to show
the overall trend; the table separates the two. We
read the results as a search problem. The Explorer
expands the frontier, establishing which relations
and paths actually exist around the anchors; the
Planner acts as a heuristic, ranking candidate paths
and committing to some of them; each Generator
retry tests a candidate by trial and error; and each
pass restarts the search from a follow-up question.
Direct-Cypher baseline and Generator alone.
With one retry and one pass, the Generator alone
is a Direct-Cypher agent: it compiles Cypher from
the question and the introspected schema in a sin-
gle attempt, with no exploration or plan, and an-
swers 12.0% of questions on FinReflectKG and
6.0% on OpenIE. With more retries and passes it
is no longer a plain text-to-Cypher baseline but Ar-
cRAG’s Generator without exploration or planning:
its refinement loop loosens or tightens a query in
response to the result, and further passes restart
retrieval from a follow-up question. This raises
accuracy to 50.0% and 59.0% at 5 retries and 5
passes. That it does better on OpenIE at high bud-
gets suggests that, with a single relationship type,
almost any query pattern is valid, so refinement
becomes a search over predicate strings; we have
not tested this explanation.
Exploration.
Adding the Explorer improves on
the Generator alone at every budget on both graph
families: by 15–39 points on FinReflectKG and
by 12–32 on OpenIE. The relative gain is largest
where the schema says least: at one retry and one
pass on OpenIE, exploration raises accuracy from
6.0% to 32.0%. As budgets grow on OpenIE, the
Direct-Cypher agent closes part of the gap, and
exploration’s advantage narrows to 12 points at 5
retries and 5 passes.
Planning.
The Planner’s contribution depends
on the graph. On FinReflectKG it improves on
Explorer + Generator when the Generator has a
single retry (by 3–11 points) and gives the best
accuracy at every retry–pass budget up to 9; at 5 re-
tries Explorer + Generator is ahead by 21–22 points,
which suggests that committing to a plan narrows
the search the Generator would otherwise perform.
On OpenIE the pattern reverses: the Planner is no
better than Explorer + Generator at budgets up to
9, and 11 points behind at one retry and one pass,
while at larger budgets it is ahead by 3–5 points,
within sampling noise at this sample size. One pos-
sible reason is that on an open-vocabulary graph
a plan commits to specific predicate strings, and a
single retry cannot repair a wrong commitment; we
have not tested this explanation either.
Summary.
Query-local exploration is the com-
ponent that transfers: it improves on direct Cypher
generation at every budget on both constructions.

E.1
Example: Multi-Hop Self-Correction
(MuSiQue, 4-hop)
Question.
Find the date when the city that is the
location of Greenwood Laboratory School became
the capital of the state in which the screenwriter of
the film “The Poor Boob” was born.
16


Reasoning trace.
Figure 3 visualizes the six
passes. In Pass 1, the system resolves Greenwood
Laboratory School to Springfield, Missouri, suc-
cessfully identifying the city but leaving the film’s
screenwriter unknown. Since only part of the rea-
soning chain is resolved, answer synthesis marks
the pass as insufficient.
In Pass 2, the planner broadens the search from
a precise entity lookup to a wider search over film
credits, retrieving six candidate screenwriters. Al-
though additional evidence is obtained, none can
yet be confirmed as the correct writer.
In Pass 3, the search narrows again by investigat-
ing the birthplaces of the candidate set. This elim-
inates several false leads and identifies the actual
credited writers—Margaret Mayo, Z. Wall Coving-
ton, and Gardner Hunting—but their birthplaces
remain unresolved.
In Pass 4, the planner further narrows the search
to the birth states of these three writers. Margaret
Mayo is found to have been born in Illinois, while
the remaining candidates provide no useful evi-
dence. This partially resolves the reasoning chain
but creates a conflict with the earlier Missouri an-
chor.
In Pass 5, the system performs its most targeted
search, attempting to resolve only Covington’s and
Hunting’s birthplaces. Neither search succeeds,
producing no additional evidence.
Finally, in Pass 6, the planner broadens the
search once more, abandoning the exhausted
candidate-specific searches and instead querying
whether Springfield served as the capital of any
state. Combining this with the Illinois evidence
from Margaret Mayo, the system retrieves that
Springfield, Illinois became the state capital in
1839, completing the reasoning chain and produc-
ing a sufficient answer.
Final answer.
1839 (correct).
Discussion.
This example demonstrates the itera-
tive search strategy illustrated in Figure 3. Rather
than repeatedly attempting the same retrievals, each
pass contributes partial progress by either discover-
ing new entities, eliminating incorrect hypotheses,
or adapting the search scope from precise entity-
level retrieval to broader graph exploration. Evi-
dence accumulates across passes and incrementally
refines the active reasoning chain until the sixth
pass has sufficient evidence to answer the question.

Compute and Reproducibility
All language models and the embedder were ac-
cessed through an OpenAI-compatible inference
API; no models were trained or fine-tuned, and
no local GPUs were used. The pipeline is im-
plemented in Python using the OpenAI client li-
brary for model calls, the Neo4j Python driver for
Cypher queries over Memgraph via the Bolt pro-
tocol, and the Qdrant client for hybrid dense and
BM25 retrieval ([versions]). The six main runs
(three datasets, two graph families, 1,000 questions
each) persist 85.78M tokens (Appendix C), and
the component ablations comprise 54 runs over the
same 100-question sample. All settings are listed
in Appendix B.2; agents run near-greedy (tempera-
ture 0.0–0.2), the answer model runs at temperature
1.0, and each configuration was run once.
