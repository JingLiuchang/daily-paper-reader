## Abstract 

Shared history in human dialogue is not merely a repository of facts, but the foundation of evolving relationships and mutual understand-ing. However, mainstream conversational mem-ory systems rely on passive semantic similarity over external fact stores, frequently retrieving topically similar records while missing rela-tionally pivotal experiences. Inspired by cogni-tive psychology’s Self-Memory System (SMS), we propose MindMem , a mental-state-driven memory framework that asynchronously con-solidates shared experiences into source-linked mental states of the character, the user, and their relationship. At each turn, a specialized mental-state reasoner ( Mind ) contextualizes these per-sistent states to anticipate latent user needs, form proactive intentions, and recover support-ing episodic evidence that surface-level match-ing overlooks. To evaluate history-sensitive adaptation beyond factual recall, we introduce 

MemReal-Bench , a longitudinal benchmark grounded in month-long real-user interactions across dyadic and group scenes. By pairing Di-rect Question Answering (DQA) with Situated Memory Realization (SMR), it tests whether agents bridge the gap between knowing past facts and naturally using shared history, world rules, and relational trajectories. On MemReal-Bench , MindMem reaches 59.03% SMR and 82.58% DQA—exceeding the strongest mem-ory baseline by 24.19 and 9.19 percentage points, respectively, and outperforming oracle target-memory injection on SMR. Code and dataset are available at § MindMem. 

## 1 Introduction 

“We are who we are in good measure be-cause of what we have learned and what we remember.” 

> — Eric Kandel, Nobel Lecture (Kandel, 2000)

Consider a scenario where a user initially ex-pressed a preference for solitude when upset, but recently shared anxiety about an upcoming inter-view (Figure 1). When the user reaches out feeling low today, a system relying on passive similarity matching surfaces the earlier solitude preference and inappropriately withdraws. In contrast, draw-ing on recent shared preparation and growing mu-tual trust, an ideal companion infers the underlying cause, anticipates a present need for support, and gently checks in on the interview. This contrast highlights a crucial gap in current conversational agents: the value of long-term memory lies not merely in retaining an external store of past facts, but in reasoning over shared experiences to antici-pate what the partner needs in the present interac-tion. Achieving such relational sensitivity remains challenging for existing architectures. Prior sys-tems support long-term interaction through dia-logue memories (Xu et al., 2022), recursive sum-maries and user profiles (Wang et al., 2025; Zhong et al., 2024), reflection and planning (Park et al., 2023), or model-controlled access to memory tiers (Packer et al., 2023). These approaches already extend beyond passive similarity matching. Our focus is on how a persistent understanding of the user, the character, and their relationship can guide turn-level need anticipation and source-linked re-call: why a past experience matters now and what conversational direction it should support. To address this, we draw inspiration from the Self-Memory System (SMS) in cognitive psychol-ogy (Conway and Pleydell-Pearce, 2000), where a dynamic working self maintains active goals to modulate access to an autobiographical knowledge base . Translating this mechanism to conversa-tional agents, we propose MindMem , a mental-state-driven memory framework. In our approach, shared experiences are asynchronously consoli-dated into source-linked mental states of the char-acter, the user, and their relationship. ## 2 Related Work 

2.1 Long-Term Dialogue Agents 

Long-term dialogue agents leverage accumulated interactions to maintain continuity and personal-ize responses, early on through recursive sum-maries (Wang et al., 2025; Zhong et al., 2024) or reflective abstractions that support planning (Park et al., 2023). Recent work advances this paradigm along two complementary axes: memory organiza-tion and retrieval control. To enrich organization, A-Mem links atomic notes into evolving knowl-edge networks (Xu et al., 2025); to refine control, Self-RAG integrates adaptive retrieval with self-reflection (Asai et al., 2024). In multi-session di-alogue, MemGuide aligns memory selection with task intent and missing information (Du et al., 2026), while MapDia couples historical-topic re-trieval with policies for reintroducing past topics (Wu et al., 2025a). Together, these methods con-nect memory structure with deliberate decisions about when and why to access past experiences. In character-based interaction, early frameworks such as Character-LLM (Shao et al., 2023) and RoleLLM (Wang et al., 2024) focus on role pro-files and role-consistent generation. Long-term character dialogue additionally requires interpret-ing shared history through an evolving understand-ing of the partner. DualMem separates factual records from persona-conditioned insights (Zhang et al., 2026). ToMAgent trains explicit Theory-of-Mind reasoning with dialogue lookahead to sup-port goal-directed social interaction (Hwang et al., 2026), while PsyProbe combines structured user-state modeling, memory construction, and strategy planning for exploratory counseling (Park et al., 2026). These systems provide complementary mechanisms for persona interpretation, social rea-soning, and proactive dialogue. In this context, 

MindMem studies their integration with a persis-tent, source-linked store of character, user, and relationship states: background consolidation re-vises these states across sessions, and a specialized turn-level reasoner uses them to anticipate needs and select supporting episodes. 

2.2 Memory and Dialogue Evaluation 

Long-term memory benchmarks evaluate conver-sational agents at progressively deeper levels of granularity. Foundational benchmarks focus on factual access and cross-session reasoning, such as multimodal QA and event summarization in Lo-CoMo (Maharana et al., 2024), or temporal updates and abstention in LongMemEval (Wu et al., 2025b). Moving beyond passive factual extraction, recent benchmarks examine how memory shapes dialogue generation and constraint adherence. RoleMemo (Zhang et al., 2026) and LoCoMo-Plus (Li et al., 2026) evaluate persona-conditioned interpretation of historical evidence and adherence to implicit user-state, goal, and value constraints. At the response level, MemUse (Sumida et al., 2026), MADial-Bench (He et al., 2025), and StratMem-Bench (Wu et al., 2026) assess naturally cued mem-ory integration, emotion-cued activation and inti-macy, and strategic memory selection in character dialogue, respectively. Advancing this shift from knowing to situated using, MemReal-Bench pairs explicit factual veri-fication (DQA) with Situated Memory Realization (SMR) across longitudinal dyadic and group inter-actions. Crucially, it evaluates how shared experi-ence informs subtle, proactive response choices— such as conversational initiative, world-rule adher-ence, and interpersonal distance—under natural triggers that do not require artificial or explicit his-torical recounting. 

## 3 Mental-State-Driven Memory Framework 

Inspired by goal-directed memory access in the Self-Memory System (Conway and Pleydell-Pearce, 2000), MindMem decouples background state consolidation from online mental-state rea-soning (Figure 2). Across long-term interactions, the framework maintains raw dialogue spans in an episodic store Et alongside source-linked mental states in Mt. Given a user utterance ut, local scene context ct, and character specification p at turn t,the turn-level reasoner Mind contextualizes Mt

to guide evidence retrieval and generate a history-consistent response yt.

3.1 Asynchronous State Consolidation 

An LLM consolidator C asynchronously revises 

Mt from completed interactions, retaining prove-nance pointers to the underlying dialogue spans in Et (Park et al., 2023). Online reasoning reads the latest committed snapshot alongside recent di-alogue, which may contain observations not yet consolidated. Immediate needs and response inten-tions are inferred at query time rather than written back as new historical evidence. The schema contains 40 dimensions across seven families, grouped into participant states, dyadic dy-namics, and shared context (Appendix A). Dimen-sions use either narrative text or structured records to represent character and user dispositions, rela-tionship states, and shared experiences. Consoli-dation revises prior interpretations while retaining their evidence and situational scope: a desire for company during interview preparation can coexist with a general preference for solitude. 3.2 Mental-State-Guided Recall and Generation 

Dense retrieval first uses (ut, c t) to obtain top-K

episodic candidates Ct from Et (Karpukhin et al., 2020). In a single model call, Mind , parameterized by πθ, reads (ut, c t, p ), the accessible contents of all 40 state dimensions, and Ct, and produces 

zt = ( τt, St, Vt).

The Thinking trace τt interprets current needs, char-acter constraints, and relational context to form a tentative response intention. It guides selection of state entries St ⊆ Slots( Mt) and episodic can-didates Vt ⊆ C t; either selection may be empty (Appendix A). State selection provides an access path beyond the initial vector candidates. For an indirect utter-ance such as “I feel low today,” Mind may select an ongoing interview goal whose source episodes have little lexical similarity to the utterance. The re-trieval pipeline resolves the selected entries’ prove-nance links and assembles the evidence: 

Lt = LinkedEvidence( St; Et),

Dt = Assemble( Vt ∪ L t).

Link resolution applies temporal and visibility cutoffs; assembly deduplicates and orders the episodes within the context budget. Thus, sup-porting episodes can enter Dt even when they are absent from Ct.The response generator Gϕ conditions on 

(ut, c t, p ), τt, selected states St, and assembled episodes Dt to produce yt (Lewis et al., 2020). Provenance and temporal tags connect state inter-pretations to their supporting episodes. The genera-tor is instructed to check tentative intentions against this evidence and explicit user requests, revising unsupported assumptions when needed (Gao et al., 2023). In Figure 2, the interview state exposes anxiety ( e3) and timing ( e4), supporting a gentle check-in. 

3.3 Training the Mental-State Reasoner 

Training adapts only the compact reasoner πθ while keeping consolidation, dense retrieval, and the gen-erator Gϕ frozen. Dropping turn indices, let x =(u, c, p, M, C). We first initialize πθ via supervised fine-tuning (SFT), minimizing the negative log-likelihood of reference decisions z∗ = ( τ ∗, S∗, V∗)

curated over training histories (including empty se-lections when history is unneeded; Ouyang et al., 2022). To align selection with downstream memory use, we further refine πθ via Group Relative Policy Opti-mization (GRPO; Shao et al., 2024). For each input 

x, we sample a group of G decisions {z(i)}Gi=1 , as-semble their source-linked evidence, and generate corresponding replies {y(i)}Gi=1 using the frozen generator Gϕ. Each pair (z, y ) receives a compos-ite reward r(x, z, y ) ∈ [0 , 1] :

r(x, z, y ) = g(z)λfmt + λacc a(x, z )+ λqual q(x, y ),

where the nonnegative weights sum to one. The format gate g(z) ∈ { 0, 1} equals 1 only if z is struc-turally valid and references admissible IDs in M

and C. Selection accuracy a(x, z ) averages the set-level F1 scores of S and V against reference targets, while an LLM judge scores downstream response quality q(x, y ) ∈ [0 , 1] for historical grounding, character continuity, relational consistency, and cal-ibrated uncertainty (Appendix B.2). GRPO updates 

πθ using group-normalized advantages, clipped policy ratios, and a KL penalty toward the SFT reference, optimizing memory selection through downstream reply quality without backpropagating through retrieval or generation. 

## 4 Experiments 

4.1 MemReal-Bench Construction. MemReal-Bench jointly evalu-ates factual recall and situated memory realization across long-term dyadic and group character dia-logue. Its seeds are distilled from a month-long source collection of real-user and character inter-actions; for user privacy, raw chat logs are never passed to the generator or included in the bench-mark. A three-stage pipeline (Appendix C.2) (1) distills user personas, interaction patterns, and rela-tionship trajectories; (2) synthesizes multi-session dialogue mixing dyadic and group scenes, anno-tated with participants, session/turn coordinates, and visibility scopes; and (3) extracts atomic mem-ory records from the realized transcripts to build natural-response triggers, target-memory rubrics, and Direct Question Answering (DQA) items. The evaluated benchmark comprises 310 Situated Mem-ory Realization (SMR) probes and 511 scored DQA questions over 4,617 dialogue turns. As summa-rized in Table 1, it evaluates not only factual re-call but whether responses naturally respect world rules, relational trajectories, character principles, and user preferences. 

Evaluation Protocol. For probe i with histori-cal cutoff bi and responding character ri, the sys-tem receives xi = ( H≤bi,r i

Instances enforce temporal validity over su-perseded states (Wu et al., 2025b), visibility bound-aries across group and dyadic scenes (Kim et al., 2023), and separation between the real user and the fictional avatar; natural triggers cue the situation without leaking the target (Li et al., 2026; Sumida et al., 2026). 

Metrics: DQA and SMR. DQA tests explicit factual access via binary-scored questions judged against annotated answer points; abstentions count as incorrect. SMR tests whether a response realizes the target memory through history-specific details or actions not inferable from ui alone. Recount-ing the past is unnecessary: keeping a requested distance or honoring an established principle can re-alize memory, whereas trigger echoing, generic em-pathy, or state contradictions cannot. SMR probes span five dimensions (Figure 3; Table A6): Factual Realization (FR), World Consistency (WC), Rela-tional Realization (RR), Role Realization (RoR), and Personalization (PI). An identity-blind LLM judge assigns binary verdicts, and overall SMR is their micro-average (Appendix C.1). 

4.2 Experimental Setup Implementation Details. Following Section 3.3, we instantiate Mind with Qwen3.5 backbones at 0.8B and 2B scales. Training uses an internal cor-pus of de-identified, real-world user–agent inter-actions and comprises supervised initialization on 2,000 examples followed by GRPO, with a fixed Response Generator and reward judge; configu-rations are given in Appendix B.3. Mind reads the full contents of all 40 slot dimensions. Train-ing and benchmark test histories are partitioned by source user with no overlap, and benchmark annotations are excluded from optimization. The main results on MemReal-Bench , LoCoMo, and LongMemEval-S, as well as the diagnostic analy-ses, use the SFT+GRPO-2B checkpoint (Tables 2 and 3). At test time, histories are ingested chrono-logically up to bi under the responding character’s visibility boundaries. All MemReal-Bench condi-tions share evaluation instances, historical cutoffs, prompt templates, decoding settings, a fixed com-mercial generator and an independent LLM judge. Following top-k memory selection in prior work (Xu et al., 2025), we control retrieval by record count: both MindMem and the baselines supply 16 recalled evidence records to the generator. 4.3 Main Results Comparison with Memory Systems. Without access to long-term memory or recent dialogue context, NoMem shows limited situated realiza-tion, whereas MindMem outperforms all mem-ory baselines on DQA and every SMR dimen-sion (Table 2). Gains over the strongest baseline per dimension are largest for relational realiza-tion (RR, +27.66 points) and personalization (PI, +25.72). Using the same SFT+GRPO-2B check-point, MindMem scores 91.95% on LoCoMo, be-hind SimpleMem and A-Mem, and reports the high-est LongMemEval-S score (87.20%). 

Comparison with Gold Memory. MindMem 

surpasses oracle GroundTruth on overall SMR (59.03% vs. 50.65%) despite lower DQA accuracy, matching the oracle on RoR (61.54%) and exceed-ing it on the remaining four dimensions. Supply-ing target memories therefore does not guarantee situated realization, and the gap is visible in the or-acle itself: GroundTruth answers 96.67% of DQA questions yet realizes only 50.65% of SMR probes, and only 20.00% on PI, where realization depends on recognizing which established preference ap-plies now. This suggests that the bottleneck lies less in access than in connecting a known fact to the present situation, which MindMem addresses through an explicit response intention ( τt) and se-lected user and relational states ( St; Section 4.5). 

Performance Across Interaction Stages. Ta-ble 3 (right) reports SMR across three cutoff-session buckets. MindMem achieves 58.3%, 53.6%, and 64.9% in the early, middle, and late buckets, respectively, with higher observed scores than the memory baselines in each bucket. Its mar-gin over GroundTruth varies across buckets, from 0.9 to 16.7 percentage points. These results de-scribe performance across interaction stages; differ-ences in probe content and SMR dimension com-position preclude attributing the variation solely to history length.

4.5 Ablation Study 

Using SFT+GRPO-2B as Full , we evaluate three variants (Table 4). (1) w/o GRPO uses the SFT-2B checkpoint to assess the alignment gain of GRPO beyond supervised initialization. (2) w/o special-ized Mind replaces the trained reasoner with a general-purpose LLM under the identical active-recall interface. (3) w/o active recall ablates the 

Mind -driven active-recall pathway by removing the single-pass decision tuple (τt, St) and its resolved source links Lt, while retaining 16 vector-retrieved evidence records. All conditions share test histo-ries, historical cutoffs, the response generator, and judge settings (Appendix B.3). 

Full exceeds w/o GRPO by 4.84 percentage points in SMR and 0.58 points in DQA. Replac-ing the specialized Mind with the general-purpose model reduces these scores by 17.09 and 2.15 points, respectively, while ablating the active-recall pathway reduces them by 29.35 and 16.24 points. These results support the benefit of the active-recall pathway, with additional gains from the special-ized reasoner and GRPO. Further backbone and stage comparisons are provided in Appendix D (Ta-ble A7).

## 5 Conclusion 

We introduced MindMem , which consolidates shared experiences into persistent character, user, and relationship states and uses turn-level rea-soning to guide source-linked episodic recall and conversational responses. We also introduced 

MemReal-Bench , a benchmark seeded by real interactions that evaluates explicit factual recall (DQA) and situated memory realization (SMR). On this benchmark, MindMem improves SMR by 24.19 percentage points over the strongest mem-ory baseline. Its advantage remains 19.4 points on shared probes where all compared memory systems correctly answer every linked DQA question, re-vealing substantial differences in situated use even when explicit recall succeeds. Failure analysis fur-ther identifies response realization as a remaining bottleneck even when target memories reach the generator. Together, these findings support cou-pling memory access with reasoning about how shared history should shape the current response, and evaluating both factual recall and situated use in long-term dialogue. 

## Limitations 

State size and update delay. Mind reads the full visible contents of all 40 state dimensions. Ac-cumulated entries can increase prompt length and inference cost. Asynchronous consolidation can also leave committed snapshots behind recent ob-servations. Local context may contain corrections absent from the snapshot, and the reliability of re-solving such conflicts requires measurement. 

Interpretation and generator dependence. 

Consolidation can overgeneralize temporary preferences, and Mind can infer unsupported in-tentions or relationship changes. LLM judges can exhibit position, verbosity, and self-enhancement biases (Zheng et al., 2023). Training against a fixed generator and reward judge may favor their response styles or selection preferences; transfer across generators has not been established. 

Empirical scope. While our evaluation covers month-long dyadic and group interactions, extend-ing validation to broader domains and multi-month lifelong settings remains an open direction for fu-ture work. Relational appropriateness depends on annotation criteria and judge calibration, and se-lection agreement with one reference may under-represent valid alternatives. Larger-scale human assessment and broader longitudinal comparisons remain important directions for establishing these capabilities at scale.

## A State Slot Schema 

Schema organization and slot types. The schema organizes Mt into 40 core slot dimensions across seven semantic families (Tables A1–A2): (1) Participant states (user , avatar , and role fami-lies) distinguish the real user from their role-play persona while tracking the character’s dispositions and goals; (2) Dyadic dynamics (relationship fam-ily) capture evolving trust, boundaries, milestones, and unresolved open loops; and (3) Shared con-text (emotional-event , collective , and storyworld 

families) maintain pivotal shared memories, group alliances, and world rules. Each dimension is in-stantiated as either a text slot (cohesive narrative syntheses, e.g., how joint interview preparation fos-tered mutual trust) or a structured slot (discrete keyed records, e.g., specific interview anxieties and dates). A slot identifier combines a group prefix and a field, such as user. and preference.life 

in user.preference.life . Each entry retains at-tribution, its temporal scope, and provenance point-ers to Et.Epistemic grounding and information bound-aries. To ensure realistic multi-session and multi-party interactions, all stages—consolidation, re-trieval, source-link resolution, and generation— enforce strict epistemic constraints: (1) causality ,restricting access to records strictly prior to turn 

t; (2) visibility , restricting reads from Et and Mt

to records accessible to the responding character; (3) attribution , separating the real-world user from their assumed fictional avatar; and (4) epistemic calibration , preserving temporal scopes and dis-tinguishing recorded observations from tentative interpretations. Current explicit requests constrain the response. Turn-level reasoning guides the cur-rent reply without being written back as factual evidence. 

Reasoner input scope. At query time, Mind 

reads the accessible contents of all 40 core slot dimensions alongside the current dialogue and ini-tial vector candidates. The schema fixes the num-ber of dimensions, not a token budget; input length depends on stored content and retrieved candidates. 

Reasoner interface and action space. The input is (ut, c t, p, Mt, Ct): the current utterance, local dialogue and scene context, character specification, accessible state entries with provenance, and initial episodic candidates. The output is zt = ( τt, St, Vt).The interpretation τt distinguishes evidence from tentative needs and response intentions; St selects supplied state-entry IDs, and Vt selects supplied candidate IDs. Either list may be empty. The re-trieval pipeline, rather than the reasoner, resolves selected provenance links under the same temporal and visibility constraints. For illustration, suppose the accessible states in-clude s1 (an active interview goal, linked to e3, e 4)and s2 (a general preference for solitude), while vector candidate c1 contains an earlier solitude-related episode. Given a current expression of low mood, the following JSON schematically repre-sents the decision tuple: 

> {"tau": "Tentatively ask about interview.", "S": ["s1"], "V": [] }

The retrieved evidence is then Lt = {e3, e 4} de-spite the empty vector selection. These episodes support a tentative check-in.

## B Implementation and Training Details 

B.1 Training Procedure 

Training inputs include current context, available state slots, and pre-retrieved candidates. SFT ini-tializes Mind to produce Thinking and selected memories. GRPO then refines the SFT policy us-ing downstream response rewards. We evaluate SFT and SFT+GRPO checkpoints separately to measure the additional benefit of GRPO. A fixed generator produces replies, which an LLM judge evaluates for the response-quality reward. Format and selection-accuracy rewards provide additional GRPO feedback, with a KL penalty to the SFT ref-erence. Consolidation and vector retrieval remain fixed; selected state entries supply source-linked evidence before generation. No test annotations enter training. 

B.2 Reward Specification 

This subsection details the composite reward for-mulation introduced in Section 3.3, including ID-matching conventions and the response-quality judge rubric. 

Format validity. Let g(z) ∈ {0, 1} indicate whether Mind output is parseable, contains the re-quired fields, and uses admissible references: state-entry IDs must belong to the input slot store, and episodic IDs must belong to the vector candidates. Invalid outputs receive zero total reward. 

Selection accuracy. For annotated training tar-gets (S∗, V∗), we define 

a(x, z ) = 12 F1(S, S∗) + 12 F1(V, V∗),where F1(A, B ) = 2 |A ∩ B|/(|A| + |B|) for a nonzero denominator. We set F1(∅, ∅) = 1 ; if exactly one set is empty, its score is zero. Matching operates on exact entry IDs at the annotated state-entry and candidate-evidence granularity. Source-linked episodes resolved deterministically after slot selection are excluded from the candidate-selection target V∗.

Response quality. The reward judge assigns an integer score s(x, y ) ∈ { 0, 1, 2, 3, 4}, normalized as q(x, y ) = s(x, y )/4 according to the rubric in Table A3. Its evaluation context comprises the char-acter specification, current conversation, and his-torical evidence available at the sample cutoff (ex-cluding future turns). The response-quality judge uses the following instruction: Evaluate the reply using historical grounding, character and relationship consistency, and appropriateness to the current interaction. Distinguish supported statements from uncertain interpretations. Do not reward length, explicit memory mentions, or greater intimacy by themselves. Apply the 0–4 anchors in Table A3. Return an integer score and a short justification tied to the supplied context. Do not use future dialogue or benchmark test annotations.

C.2 Construction Pipeline and Quality Control MemReal-Bench is constructed via a three-stage construction pipeline: 1. Seed Distillation: We distill user personas, interaction habits, and relational progression trajectories from a month-long source collec-tion of real-user and character interactions. Raw chat logs are excluded from downstream dialogue-generation inputs and benchmark histories. 2. Longitudinal History Synthesis: Distilled seeds are paired with character and storyworld specifications to synthesize multi-session di-alogue mixing dyadic and group interactions. Every turn is indexed with participant identi-ties, session/turn coordinates, and character-specific visibility scopes. 3. Evidence-First Probe Construction: We ex-tract atomic memory records (propositions, verbatim quotes, source turns, and tempo-ral/visibility scopes) directly from the realized transcripts. Each probe pairs a natural conver-sational trigger with a target-memory rubric (for SMR) and explicit verification questions (for DQA). 

Temporal and visibility verification. The split unit is the source-user seed: histories sharing a seed belong to the same partition. For each probe i with cutoff bi and responding character ri, the verifica-tion protocol checks that: (1) all target evidence occurs at or before bi within scenes visible to ri; (2) superseded states are marked with validity intervals so models are evaluated on the latest valid state; and (3) natural triggers cue the situational context without lexically leaking the target memory.

D.2 Diagnostic Taxonomy for Failure Localization 

The audit in Section 4.4 uses two categories, de-fined by whether the stored target memory reaches the final generator input. 1. Recall Miss: The target memory is stored in the memory system but absent from the final generator input. 2. Realization Failure: The target memory is present in the final generator input, but the response fails to realize it appropriately. These categories localize failures at the recall– realization boundary. They do not distinguish up-stream causes of recall misses or separate failures in Thinking guidance from failures in response gen-eration.
