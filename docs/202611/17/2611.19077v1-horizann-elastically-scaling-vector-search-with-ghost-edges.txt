## ABSTRACT 

Approximate nearest neighbor search (ANNS) is fundamental to modern data-intensive AI applications such as retrieval-augmented generation, recommendation systems, and semantic search. Among existing indexing methods, graph-based indexes emerge as the state of the art due to their superior recall-throughput tradeoffs. Scal-ing these indexes to multiple machines requires partitioning the data and building indexes on each. However, when new machines are added or removed, the data must be repartitioned and the in-dexes reconstructed from scratch, making horizontal scaling costly at billion scale. In this work, we present HorizANN , a distributed graph-based ANNS system that scales horizontally without requir-ing full index reconstruction. To achieve this, we introduce ghost edges, a mechanism for preserving cross-partition connectivity as partition boundaries change during repartitioning. By reusing the structural information that would otherwise be discarded during repartitioning, HorizANN avoids costly full index reconstruction. As the index grows from 100M vectors on one worker to 1B vectors on ten workers, HorizANN inserts each 100M vectors within 2.5 hours and sustains 3.4–3.5K queries per second at 0.998 Recall@10.

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Modern AI applications—ranging from large-scale recommenda-tion engines [11, 24] to retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) pipelines [8, 23, 31]—rely fundamentally on Approximate Nearest Neighbor Search (ANNS) to navigate billions of high-dimensional vectors through various types of indexes such as KD-trees [7] and random projection trees [12], hash-based methods such as locality-sensitive hashing [2, 25], cluster-based methods such as inverted file indexes (IVF) [28], and graph-based methods (e.g., HNSW [32], NSG [18]). To handle these immense workloads, state-of-the-art production systems such as Milvus [40] and Weaviate [38] partition datasets into subindexes and distribute them across clusters on each worker node. When a system indexes millions of new vectors daily or faces highly variable workloads, expanding or shrinking cluster capacity without service interruption is critical. 

> ∗Both authors contributed equally to this work.

However, these existing systems are horizontally inelastic : they cannot dynamically add or remove worker nodes. The core problem with existing distributed graph-based systems is that they statically assign vectors to worker nodes , partitioning data permanently at con-struction time. Whenever worker nodes are added or removed, the new vector-worker assignment shifts partition boundaries, invali-dating the local graph structures and forcing subindexes to undergo full reconstruction from scratch [9, 40]. At billion-scale, this full index rebuilding takes hours and frequently requires partitions to be taken offline [36, 46]. 1 Because current architectures lack a mech-anism to dynamically redistribute data across an evolving topology, distributed graph indexes remain bottlenecked by a costly choice between resource underutilization and severe service downtime. To eliminate this inelasticity, our goal is to design a distributed graph-based ANNS system that supports dynamic repartitioning —the ability to seamlessly redistribute vector data across an altering number of worker nodes without full index reconstruction. Real-izing such an elastic, horizontally scalable architecture, however, requires overcoming two fundamental challenges: (1) Structural Dependency of Graph-based Index. Unlike cluster-based indexes (such as IVF) [10, 28, 33, 46] where independent vectors are mapped strictly to independent centroids and can be predictably moved to-gether, vectors in a graph index are bound by structural proximity edges established through expensive beam search [14, 32]. Naively repartitioning across a new topology discards these cross-boundary edges, and re-establishing them later requires repeating the costly search phase entirely. (2) Multi-Worker Query Routing Bottle-necks. To maintain high throughput during cluster resizing, queries must be routed selectively. While broadcasting to all workers elimi-nates the performance benefits of partitioning [19], under-routing or inaccurate partition selection risks missing relevant local struc-tures and severely degrading recall [6]. To address these challenges, this work introduces HorizANN , a distributed graph-based ANNS system that scales horizontally with-out index reconstruction (Table 1). (1) HorizANN tackles the first challenge—structural dependency—through a structure-preserving repartitioning mechanism that maintains both intra- and inter-partition edge information. In summary, we make the following contributions: 1. Horizontally Scalable Graph Index. We present HorizANN ,an incremental distributed graph-based ANNS system that scales horizontally. As the index grows from 100M vectors on one worker to 1B vectors on ten workers, HorizANN sustains 3.4–3.5K QPS at Recall@10 of 0.998. 2. Horizontal Index Repartitioning. Based on the concept of ghost edges, we introduce a split-and-merge algorithm that repartitions graph indexes without reconstruction by preserv-ing cross-partition connectivity. As the index grows to 500M vectors on 6 workers, repartitioning takes only 15% of the total build time. 3. Implementation and Evaluation. ## 2 MOTIVATION 

We motivate the design of HorizANN by demonstrating the impor-tance of graph-based indexes (§2.1) and highlighting the fundamen-tal limitations of conventional index repartitioning (§2.2). 

## 2.1 The Necessity of Distributed Graphs 

Graph-based vector indexes are one of ANNS, critical for the re-trieval stage of many applications like recommendation systems, semantic search, cybersecurity, and bioinformatics [5]. In particular, retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) has become the standard ar-chitecture for grounding 2 large language models in external knowl-edge [31]. We hence quantify how downstream task quality relies on retrieval recall by evaluating 1,000 queries from HotpotQA [48]—a multi-hop benchmark requiring two supporting documents—using Gemini 2.5 Flash. We simulate varying retrieval qualities by ran-domly introducing irrelevant documents to question answering. As shown in Figure 2, downstream QA accuracy (EM, i.e., exact match) scales quadratically (𝑟 2) with retrieval recall 𝑟 due to the multi-hop dependency. At 𝑟 = 0.90, EM drops from a baseline of 23.2% down to 17.7% (approximating 0 .90 2 = 81% of maximum effectiveness). More generally, tasks requiring 𝑘 independent pieces of evidence exhibit an 𝑟 𝑘 performance decay, making ultra-high recall non-negotiable as application reasoning complexity grows. Therefore, the strict recall-accuracy relationship calls for the adop-tion of graph-based indexes like HNSW, which can offer higher accuracy than other methods (e.g., IVF) [3], when all graph data can fit in memory. Albeit the necessity, dynamically scaling graph-based indexes into multiple worker nodes remains under-explored. In distributed environments, horizontally scaling cluster-based indexes such as IVF variants is well-understood [33, 46] because vectors are mapped independently to disconnected centroids. On the other hand, han-dling inter-partition edges for graph-based indexes is non-trivial: vectors are explicitly bound by proximity edges. Filling the archi-tectural gap between graph-level retrieval performance and cluster-level horizontal elasticity remains an open and critical problem.

Under a centralized setting, a graph-based index operates by navigating a network of proximity edges . Queries traverse from an entry point through the graph, moving progressively closer to the target vector by following these edges to nearer neighbors. While the specific algorithms used to construct these edges vary across different graph indexes, they all share this fundamental reliance on continuous edge connectivity for successful routing. However, when graphs are repartitioned across a distributed cluster, this core routing mechanism is directly disrupted. 

Problem 1: Fractured Routing and Dropped Recall. As conceptu-alized in Figure 1, proximity edges frequently cross the boundaries of the newly assigned partitions. When data points migrate, these cross-boundary edges are severed. For instance, if vectors move to a new worker node, their existing incoming and outgoing edges to vectors remaining on the original worker are discarded. Because graph-based ANNS’s greedy routing relies entirely on continuous edge connectivity to navigate closer to a query’s neighborhood, these severed boundaries form routing dead ends. When a query searches these fractured, disjoint subgraphs, it cannot traverse the missing links, causing retrieval recall to collapse toward zero.

Problem 2: Catastrophic Reconstruction Costs. Re-establishing the missing graph connectivity through standard means requires re-inserting the migrated vectors, incurring unnecessary recom-putation costs. This requires re-running expensive beam searches for every vector in the altered partitions to locate valid proximity neighbors. At billion-scale, this index reconstruction takes hours which may degrade overall system availability or double the re-source allocation. An elastic ANNS system must therefore find a way to preserve cross-partition proximity edges across dynamic cluster shifts with-out executing recurrent, full-scale graph builds. HorizANN achieves this balance by natively retaining these cross-partition boundaries during repartitioning.

## 3 SYSTEM OVERVIEW 

This section outlines the design of HorizANN , defining the external user interface (§3.1) and the internal architecture (§3.2). We then discuss the logical data model that HorizANN represents (§3.3) and the design goals it achieves (§3.4). 

## 3.1 User Interface 

HorizANN provides a high-level abstraction that consists of five operations. They allow users to interact with a horizontally scal-able vector index as if it were a single, local data structure. From the user’s perspective, the system maintains a global collection of vectors V ⊂ R𝑑 ; we denote a vector by 𝑣 ∈ R𝑑 and a query by 𝑞 ∈ R𝑑 . This design effectively hides the complexities of the distributed architecture like query routing, data partitioning, and result aggregation. 

Data Maintenance. Data maintenance is performed through in-cremental operations that keep the index synchronized with the application’s evolving state. A user invokes Insert (𝑣 ) to add a new vector 𝑣 ∈ R𝑑 to the global collection V. This is typically called when new searchable entities, such as user profiles, docu-ment embeddings, or product features, are generated. Conversely, Delete (𝑣 ) is used to remove a specific vector from V. This ensures the vector is immediately pruned from the search space and will no longer appear in later searches. 

Search. The primary mechanism for retrieval is the Search (𝑞, 𝐾 )

operation, which enables similarity-based discovery. By providing a query vector 𝑞 ∈ R𝑑 and a parameter 𝐾 , the user requests the 𝐾 

most similar vectors in V relative to 𝑞 . The system identifies the global top-𝐾 approximate nearest neighbors and returns them as a single ranked list. 

Resource Management. HorizANN also exposes administrative operations for elastic scaling. A user can call AddWorker (𝑤 ) to integrate a new compute and storage resource into the cluster. This is typically prompted by the growth of V toward memory limits or a need to increase search throughput under high query loads. Similarly, RemoveWorker (𝑤 ) allows for the decommissioning of a resource for cost optimization during periods of lower demand.

## 3.3 Logical Data Model 

HorizANN logically represents a Hierarchical Navigable Small World (HNSW) graph [32], albeit a physically distributed one across work-ers. Note that HorizANN ’s techniques also apply to other graph-based indexes such as NSG [18] and Vamana [27]. 

Data Structure. HNSW is a graph based index that combines small-world graphs [30] and skip-lists [35] to create a multi-layer structure for efficient similarity search. The HNSW index 𝐻 over vectors V consists of layers 𝐿 0, 𝐿 1, . . . , with each layer being a graph 𝐺 = (𝑉 , 𝐸 ) whose vertices are vectors, i.e., 𝑉 ⊆ V . Each edge 

(𝑢, 𝑣 ) ∈ 𝐸 connects vectors that are likely near each other. Each 𝑣 is assigned a level ℓ (𝑣 ) at insertion and appears in layers 𝐿 0, . . . , 𝐿 ℓ (𝑣 ) ,so 𝑉 = V on 𝐿 0 and 𝑉 shrinks on upper layers. A vertex keeps at most 2 𝑀 out-edges in 𝐿 0 and 𝑀 in each upper layer, and search starts from the entry point , a vector with the highest level. Unless stated otherwise, the techniques in this paper treat every layer in the same way, so we describe them on a single layer. 

Construction. HNSW is built incrementally. Each inserted vector 

𝑣 is assigned a random maximum layer ℓ (𝑣 ). The algorithm searches for nearest neighbors at each layer from 𝐿 ℓ (𝑣 ) down to 𝐿 0, and adds edges connecting the new node to up to 𝑀 neighbors per layer. 

Search. Given a query vector 𝑞 , search traverses layers top-to-bottom. At each layer, greedy traversal moves to the neighbor closest to 𝑞 until no improvement is found; this node becomes the entry point for the next layer. Upper layers locate the neighborhood, and the bottom layer returns the final top-𝐾 candidates. 

KNN Property. Each vertex in HNSW is connected to a subset of its approximate nearest neighbors found by beam search; we refer to this as the KNN property . Greedy search relies on this property to move progressively closer to the query.

## 3.4 Design Goals 

HorizANN is designed to achieve four design goals. (i) Consistent Accuracy : HorizANN as a distributed system maintains nearly con-sistent recall as index and cluster size grow. (ii) Efficient Reparti-tioning : HorizANN quickly adapts itself with changing workers and workload, avoiding a full index reconstruction. (iii) Throughput Scale-up : HorizANN ’s query throughput scales with the number of workers, enabling the system to handle increased load by adding machines. (iv) Online Repartitioning : HorizANN continues to serve queries during repartitioning, sustaining throughput comparable to that before repartitioning. 

## 4 HORIZONTAL INDEXING

We first define the distributed index structure, whose ghost edges preserve the KNN property (§3.3) across partition boundaries (§4.1). We then describe how vectors are partitioned, inserted, and deleted while preserving this property (§4.2), and how a query is routed to only the relevant partitions and aggregated into a global result (§4.3). How the index adapts when workers join or leave the cluster is deferred to §5.

## 4.3 Distributed Query 

With the index partitioned across several partitions, effective query-ing requires finding the smallest number of partitions which contains the true nearest neighbors for a given query. 

In a small scale experiment, we partitioned the SIFT1M dataset (1 million embeddings, 128 dimensional) into 10 and 5 partitions and measured, for each query, how many partitions contain at least one true top-10 neighbor. Figure 4 shows the results. On average, true neighbors reside in only a few partitions, indicating that a small number of probes 𝑛 probe suffices for high recall. A constant 𝑛 probe 

is also wasteful, since it varies across queries. This motivates the need for a query-aware router which routes a query 𝑞 to only the most relevant partitions, to avoid under or over routing.

## 5 HORIZONTAL INDEX SCALING 

While §4 assumes a given set of workers, HorizANN must also adapt as workers join or leave the cluster. This capability serves two of our design goals (§3.4): Throughput Scale-up , since newly added workers absorb a share of the data and query load, and Efficient Repartitioning , since the cluster must reach its new configuration without a full index reconstruction. Both hinge on repartitioning ,the process of redistributing data across a potentially changing number of workers. This section formalizes the repartitioning prob-lem on a single layer (§5.1), presents HorizANN ’s split-and-merge mechanism, which extends naive repartitioning with ghost edges, first on a single layer and then on all layers of HNSW (§5.2), de-scribes when repartitioning is triggered and how data moves when workers are added or removed (§5.3), and explains how HorizANN 

keeps serving queries while repartitioning is in progress (§5.4).

## 5.3 Dynamic Repartition Actuators 

HorizANN repartitions the index in two cases: the cluster automat-ically adds a worker when a partition exceeds the size threshold 𝜏 ,and an operator can also add or remove a worker manually. 

Worker Addition. Adding a worker proceeds in three steps. Firstly, the main node maintains a sample of vectors via reservoir sampling [39] from the input stream and runs balanced 𝑘 -means on it to compute 𝑘 +1 centers and broadcasts to all workers. Secondly, each worker derives the new assignment 𝜋 ′ from these centers and repar-titions its index 𝐻 𝑖 under 𝜋 ′ (§5.2); only vectors whose assignment changes leave their worker. Lastly, workers update vector counts. The main node updates the routing index to include the new worker. 

Worker Removal. Removing a worker follows the similar proce-dure as adding with reversed data flow. The main node computes 

𝑘 −1 centers. The departing worker splits its index and distributes subindexes to remaining workers. After remaining workers merges received subindexes, the departing worker deregisters. 

## 5.4 Online Repartition 

To keep serving queries during repartitioning, we extend the design in §5.3 into an online protocol, under which each query is answered entirely by the indexes either before or after repartitioning. Taking worker addition as an example, we describe the protocol below. 

Repartitioning Protocol Design. The key idea is to assign each query an epoch , the version of the indexes that serves it. The main node tags each query on arrival with the epoch of its current routing index, and each worker answers the query with its index of that epoch. While the cluster moves from epoch 𝑒 to 𝑒 +1, the main node and every worker keep their indexes of both epochs, so queries tagged with 𝑒 keep using the old indexes while the new ones are built. The protocol performs this move in three stages, starting once the workers receive the new centers (§5.3): 

• Prepare. Each worker loads its ghost edges and runs Split, which only reads its current index 𝐻 𝑖 , so 𝐻 𝑖 keeps serving epoch 

𝑒 ; the worker writes the outgoing subindexes to disk and starts its staged index 𝐻 ′

## 6 EVALUATION 

We evaluate HorizANN on billion-scale datasets, comparing against state-of-the-art distributed vector search systems. Our experiments reveal the following findings: 

• Incremental Insertion. During incremental index construc-tion from 0 to 1B vectors, HorizANN consistently inserts each successive 100M vectors in 49–148 minutes while baselines fail to insert all vectors. (§6.2) 

• Higher Search Performance. HorizANN achieves the highest QPS at every Recall@10 up to 0.99 on all three datasets, with 2.1–3.9 × the QPS of SPTAG and 9–21 × that of Harmony at Recall@10 = 0.95. (§6.3) 

• Horizontal Scalability. As the cluster grows from 1 to 10 work-ers and the index grows from 100M to 1B vectors, HorizANN 

achieves a 2.4–3 × increase in query throughput while maintain-ing consistent recall across cluster sizes. (§6.4) 

• Repartitioning Efficiency. Growing the index from 0 to 500M vectors and from 1 to 6 workers, repartitioning takes 15% of total build time, and at most 14 minutes in one pass. (§6.5) 

• Online Repartitioning. HorizANN keeps serving queries dur-ing repartitioning: when 500M vectors are repartitioned from 5 to 6 workers, throughput averages 84% of its pre-repartitioning level and Recall@10 stays at 0.98. (§6.6) 

• Bounded Per-Worker Memory. As the index grows to 1B vectors, each worker’s peak memory stays below 214 GiB, and ghost edges add at most 16% to the index memory. (§6.7) 

## 6.1 Experimental Setup 

Hardware. All experiments run on CloudLab [17] using c6320 nodes at the Clemson cluster, each with two 14-core Intel Xeon E5-2683 v3 CPUs (2.0 GHz, 28 cores total), 256 GiB RAM, and two 1 TB SATA HDDs, connected via 10 Gbps Ethernet. We use 12 machines for billion-scale datasets. 

Datasets. Table 2 presents the datasets. SIFT [29] and SPACEV [36] are standard billion-scale benchmarks. We further generate IBM1B, a synthetic dataset of 1 billion 128-dimensional vectors sampled from a Gaussian Mixture Model with 300 components which learnt from real world embeddings. 

Baselines. We compare against two distributed vector search sys-tems. SPTAG [9] is Microsoft’s ANN library; we use its SPFresh [46] index, which organizes vectors into posting lists via SPANN-style [10] hierarchical balanced clustering and supports in-place updates. For a fair comparison, we place SPTAG ’s on-disk index on a 180–200 GiB RAM disk (sized per dataset) on each worker, so that its entire index resides in memory. Harmony [45] partitions data using a hy-brid of dimension-based and vector-based strategies with adaptive query routing. 

Hyperparameters. All workers build HNSW indexes with 𝑀 =32 and ef con =200. We vary ef search (default 500) and routing fan-out 

𝑘 seed (default 10) to trace QPS–recall curves. We report Recall@10 and throughput in queries per second (QPS), measuring over 10K queries with brute-force ground truth.

## 6.3 Search Performance 

This experiment aims to show that HorizANN achieves higher search performance than existing distributed systems, despite its support for elasticity. 

Setup. Because Harmony fails to build an index on any of the three 1B datasets, we evaluate search performance on the first 100M vectors of each dataset with 10 workers. For HorizANN , we perform queries in batches of 5K on the incrementally built index, and each worker searches in parallel on all its cores. We vary ef search and 

𝑘 seed . For SPTAG , we bulk-build each shard’s index over its 10M vectors. Its client sends queries in 64 threads, and the aggregator forwards each query to every shard which serves requests with 28 threads. We vary SearchInternalResultNum , the number of postings each query searches on each shard. Harmony builds an IVF index with √𝑁 = 10 ,000 lists, takes the whole query set as one batch, and pipelines it in groups of 2,000 queries through 5 teams of 2 workers, each using all its cores. We vary 𝑛 probe only. Figure 7 presents the Recall@10–QPS curves. 

Results. Up to a Recall@10 of 0.99, HorizANN achieves the highest Recall@10 at a given QPS, or equivalently, the highest QPS at a given Recall@10. Across the three datasets, at Recall@10 = 0.95, 

HorizANN delivers 2.1–3.9 × the QPS of SPTAG and 9–21 × that of 

Harmony . At 0.99, its advantage is 1.4–3.9 × over SPTAG and 5–27 ×

over Harmony on SIFT100M and IBM100M, while Harmony cannot reach 0.98 on SPACEV100M before running out of memory. 

Analysis. First, as demonstrated in §4.3, HorizANN partitions data with balanced k-means, so each worker holds a cluster of nearby vectors, and the main node routes each query only to the few partitions that hold its nearest routing samples. In con-trast, SPTAG broadcasts every query to all workers, therefore every query consumes work on every machine. Second, within a parti-tion HorizANN runs an in-memory graph search whose cost grows slowly with the search budget, whereas SPTAG and Harmony scan entire posting lists or IVF lists, whose cost grows linearly with 

SearchInternalResultNum or 𝑛 probe . Across the three datasets, raising 𝑛 probe from 10 to 100 cuts Harmony ’s QPS by 5.1–6.1 ×,and raising SearchInternalResultNum from 8 to 96 cuts SPTAG ’s QPS by 2.4–3.1 ×, while HorizANN already exceeds 0.95 recall at its smallest ef search of 500 on all three datasets.

HorizANN inserts each vector into its resident worker, and uses the routing index to find other workers that may hold its nearest neigh-bors. It searches these workers for the vector’s nearest neighbors and builds ghost edges, which are stored on its resident worker. Therefore, the number of vectors with ghost edges on each worker keeps increasing. On the first worker, the share of vectors with ghost edges grows from 14% after the repartitioning at 100M to 59% after the one at 500M, and its ghost store grows from 0.5 to 17 GiB. As a result, each worker spends more time loading ghost edges from disk ( Prepare ), activating the ghost edges of each vector while merging indexes (line 15 in Algorithm 1, Transfer & commit ), and persisting them back to disk ( Retire & persist ).

## 6.6 Online Repartitioning 

This experiment aims to show that HorizANN sustains high query throughput and recall while repartitioning. 

Setup. We apply the same setup as in §6.5. During the repartition-ing phase at 500M vectors, we continuously issue queries from a client and record the QPS prior to repartitioning for 6 minutes. Next, we initiate the repartitioning process to add the sixth worker, while maintaining the ongoing query operations from the client. Once repartitioning is complete, we conduct another 6-minute period to measure the QPS following the repartitioning (all measured under default parameters). 

Results. Figure 10 shows the query throughput before, during, and after repartitioning on 500M vectors. HorizANN answer queries during repartitioning with 11.7K QPS on average (84% of the 14.0K QPS before repartitioning), while Recall@10 stays at 0.98. And there are several QPS drops (marked as 1 – 6 ). 

Analysis. Search and repartitioning share the CPU cores of each worker, so throughput drops whenever workers run a compute-heavy repartitioning step. In 1 , all five workers split their index at the same time. In 2 and 3 , workers copy the vectors they keep into a new index, four workers at once in 2 , and the last one in 3 . In 4 , transfer starts and every worker merges the vectors it receives at the same time. In 5 , two workers merge the largest pieces they receive. Outside these drops, throughput stays at 93– 94% of its pre-repartitioning level: loading and saving ghost edges are primarily disk-bound rather than CPU-bound, and after 5 , only the new worker continues merging and does not yet serve queries. Finally, 6 corresponds to the main node switching to the new routing index, so throughput falls below 1K QPS for a moment right after the switch. Subsequently, as worker nodes release the old index, throughput drops again.

## 6.7 Memory Usage 

This experiment aims to show that HorizANN keeps per-worker memory bounded as the index grows, with a low memory overhead from ghost edges. 

Setup. We apply the same incremental index building setup as §6.2. For each worker, we track the peak memory usage reached since its startup; this value is read at the end of repartitioning in each phase. We also measure the ghost edges memory overhead, by recording the memory consumption right before and after loading ghost edges for each worker. So the ghost edges overhead is the total extra memory that loading adds on all workers, divided by their total memory before loading. 

Results. Figure 11a–Figure 11c show the trends of per-worker peak memory as the index grows on three datasets. The highest per-worker peak across all phases is 207 GiB on SIFT1B, 213 GiB on SPACEV1B and 204 GiB on IBM1B, at most 83% of the 256 GiB node RAM; this peak is reached in the repartitioning after 800M vectors (600M on IBM1B). From 100M to 1B vectors, the mean per-worker peak grows about 1.8 × from 84–90 GiB to 153–166 GiB, while the dataset grows by 10 ×. Figure 11d shows the memory overhead of ghost edges. From 400M vectors onward, ghost edges add 11–16% to the workers’ memory, and this does not grow as workers join. 

Analysis. A worker usually reaches its peak memory while merg-ing the index pieces it receives, at a median of 50–60 GiB above its

memory at the end of insertion. As described in §5.4, while merging, the worker still keeps its old index to serve queries, in addition to the staged index and the index piece it is merging. This extra memory is released once the old index is retired. The share of ghost edges in memory stays about the same: although ghost edges keep growing (§6.5), the index grows at the same pace. 

## 7 RELATED WORK 

We organize related work into single-node vector indexes whose graph structures, streaming update mechanisms, and clustering designs inform our local worker architecture (§7.1), and distributed vector search systems spanning sharded, global-graph, and hybrid partitioning paradigms (§7.2). 

## 7.1 Single-Node Systems 

Graph-Based Indexes. Graph-based indexes answer queries by greedy traversal over neighbor links and lead in-memory ANNS in recall and throughput [3, 5, 42]. In-memory designs include NSG [18] and HNSW [32], while disk-resident designs such as DiskANN [27] and Starling [41] store the graph on SSD to reach bil-lion scale on one machine. These systems provide the foundational index structure that HorizANN distributes across workers. 

Incremental Indexing. Streaming graph indexes either buffer in-sertions and periodically merge them into the on-disk graph [37] or update the graph in place [20, 44]. Deletions and updates further require repairing neighbor links, since frequent updates otherwise leave vertices unreachable [43, 44]. These repairs are confined to a single graph on one machine. HorizANN ’s ghost edges instead preserve cross partition links as vectors migrate between workers. 

Cluster-Based Indexes. IVF-style indexes such as SPANN [10] keep centroids in memory and posting lists on disk. Because vectors in different clusters share no edges, these indexes can cheaply split and merge clusters online to handle updates and query skew [33, 34, 46]. HorizANN adopts a similar split/merge pattern, inspired by SPFresh’s LIRE [46], but must additionally preserve graph edges that cross partition boundaries. 

## 7.2 Distributed Systems 

Sharded Approaches. Sharded approaches partition the dataset and build an independent index per partition [4, 13, 16]. Their recall depends on partitioning and routing, which prior work improves by co-locating near neighbors [15, 19, 21] and by routing with centroid indexes [13] or error bounds [50]. However, they assume static partitions, whereas HorizANN repartitions dynamically and keeps its router up-to-date via reservoir sampling [39]. 

Global Graph Approaches. Global graph approaches build one graph over all vectors and spread it across machines [1, 49, 51] or a disaggregated memory pool [26]. Search hops therefore cross ma-chines. Prior work reduces the communication cost with RDMA [51], asynchronous I/O [49], or near-data processing [26]. However, re-mote hops remain on the search path. HorizANN instead keeps each local search within one worker and retains cross-worker edges as ghost edges without traversing them. 

Hybrid Approaches. SPIRE [47] introduces a hierarchical IVF structure with accuracy-preserving recursive construction. It iden-tifies a balanced partition granularity to avoid read-cost explosion. However, SPIRE uses a proximity graph only at the root level to route queries, while its leaf partitions are IVF-style posting lists on SSD that are scanned rather than traversed. Harmony [45] takes a different approach, combining dimension-based and vector-based partitioning to balance workloads under skewed query distribu-tions. Its cost model adaptively selects partitioning strategies per query, achieving high throughput across diverse workloads. How-ever, Harmony uses static partitioning without support for dynamic repartitioning or incremental updates. 

## 8 CONCLUSION 

We presented HorizANN , a distributed graph-based ANNS system that achieves horizontal elasticity without full index reconstruc-tion. By introducing ghost edges to retain cross-partition proximity relationships on disk with zero search-time overhead, HorizANN 

enables an online split-and-merge repartitioning protocol that reac-tivates dormant edges as partition boundaries shift, paired with a lightweight reservoir-sampled seed index for selective query rout-ing. Across three billion-scale datasets, HorizANN scales incremen-tally from 1 to 10 workers with stable per-phase insertion times while existing systems time out or exhaust memory, delivers up to 3.9 × and 27 × higher query throughput than SPTAG and Har-mony at Recall@10 of 0.99, achieves 2.4–3.0 × throughput scale-up with consistent accuracy, and sustains query throughput across repartitioning with at most 16% ghost-edge memory overhead.
