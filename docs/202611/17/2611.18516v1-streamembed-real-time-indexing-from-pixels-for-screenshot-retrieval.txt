## ABSTRACT 

Screen history can serve as searchable visual memory for people and agents, but unlike an offline corpus, it must be encoded and indexed as each screenshot arrives. OCR-based indexing is fast but loses visual information; visual encoding takes over two seconds. Yet screens change incrementally: most pixels repeat within and across screenshots. This raises a question: do visual encoders need to recompute their representations from scratch for every new screenshot? We present S TREAM EMBED , a system for incremental visual embedding that exploits this redundancy. Guided solely by pixel differences, it combines spatial token pruning , which skips blocks that repeat a neighbor, with temporal KV reuse , which reuses cached keys and values for blocks unchanged across screenshots. A small learned adapter compensates for pruning and reuse to preserve fidelity to full-screenshot embeddings. On S CREEN MEM EVAL , our benchmark of 10,426 questions over 184 hours of screen histories from two humans and a computer-use agent, S TREAM EMBED sets a new accuracy–latency Pareto frontier on an M2 Pro GPU: it encodes up to 3.7 × faster than full-screenshot encoding without sacrificing accuracy and improves accuracy over OCR-based indexing by up to 6.3 Recall@10 points at matched latency. A memory-efficient vector index stores a simulated year of screenshots in 0.13 GB of RAM. Together, these results make real-time indexing of screen history in its native visual form practical, laying a foundation for fully local visual assistants. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Much of daily computer work passes through laptop screens: reading papers, browsing products, and messaging col-leagues. Yet people remember few of the details and may later ask: “Which paper had the pink and blue Pareto fron-tier curves I saw last week?” A searchable screen history could help people recover these details, and also provide persistent memory for locally running agents (Packer et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2025). Because screenshots capture ev-ery application without specialized integration, they offer a unified interface across text and visual contents (Hu & Lee, 2020). Answering questions about past activity then becomes a retrieval problem over a continuously growing screenshot history. The key challenge is maintaining this visual memory contin-uously without exhausting system resources. Unlike a fixed corpus that is indexed offline (Karpukhin et al., 2020), this setting requires real-time indexing : encoding and indexing each screenshot as it arrives, ideally locally to keep private content on device, while consuming only a small fraction of the laptop’s capacity.

Converting each screenshot into OCR text, as existing open-source tools do (OpenRecall, 2024; McGhee, 2023), is fast but loses what only pixels capture (Wang et al., 2026c). Di-rect visual embedding preserves this information but takes 2.3 s per screenshot on the GPU. At one screenshot every 5 s, this uses the GPU for 28 minutes per hour of the user’s work, and at one every 2 s, it cannot keep pace. Even commercial systems, such as Microsoft Recall (Microsoft), require dedi-cated hardware like NPUs and at least 16 GB of RAM. Thus, the central question is: can we retain visual retrieval quality while keeping continuous encoding lightweight enough to run silently in the background? Screen histories, however, offer a particular opportunity: they change incrementally and are largely redundant (Fig-ure 2). Background elements often repeat within and across screenshots, and typing or moving the cursor changes only a few regions, leaving the rest untouched. This raises a ques-tion: do we need to compute representations from scratch for every screenshot? 

We propose S TREAM EMBED , which encodes screenshots as a stream rather than as isolated images, exploiting this redundancy. Within a screenshot, it drops pixel blocks that merely duplicate a neighboring block (spatial pruning), cut-ting the computation by 33–41%. ## 2 PROBLEM SETUP 

Real-time indexing. While a human or an agent uses a com-puter, its screen history arrives as a stream of screenshots 

S = ( s1, s 2, . . . ), one every ∆ seconds. Each screenshot 

st is indexed as it arrives, building a memory for future questions. To keep private content local, indexing runs on the user’s device. Here, indexing st means encoding it into a vector vt with an embedding model E and adding vt to a growing index. Indexing is real-time if it keeps pace with the stream, so that screenshots never accumulate faster than they are in-dexed. Let the indexing latency L be the mean time to index one screenshot, including any preprocessing such as OCR. Keeping pace thus requires, at minimum, L ≤ ∆; practical use further calls for a small L, since the device remains in use for other work during indexing. Retrieval runs only when a question q arrives: the same model E embeds q, and Tk(q) = ( s(1) , . . . , s (k)) returns the k indexed screenshots with the highest cosine similarity to q, as in dense retrieval (Karpukhin et al., 2020). This takes about 0.4 s (Table 4) and runs once per question rather than every ∆ seconds, so only indexing is time-constrained. The challenge thus lies in indexing, which has two compet-ing goals: preserving in each vt whatever a future question may need, and keeping L small. Accordingly, §6.2 reports retrieval accuracy against L for each method.

## 3 VISUAL ENCODING 

A screenshot index encodes each arriving screenshot with a vision-language embedding model. This section describes the challenge, encoding as the main cost of indexing (§3.1), and the opportunity, redundant blocks that account for most of it (§3.2). 

3.1 Challenges 

Indexing a screenshot has two steps: encoding and insertion. Insertion takes under a millisecond, so nearly all of the cost lies in encoding. As Table 1 shows, encoding a screenshot takes 2.33 s on an M2 Pro GPU: 7.5 × as long as encoding its OCR text, and 3.6 × the total latency of a text index even with OCR included. At one screenshot every ∆ = 5 s, encoding thus occupies the GPU nearly half of the time, on a lap-top that remains in use for other work. This cost likely explains why existing tools index OCR text, despite a 10.1-point drop in R@10 (screenpipe, 2024; OpenRecall, 2024; McGhee, 2023). Moreover, it recurs for every screenshot, since screenshot indexes encode each one from scratch. 

3.2 Opportunities 

Encoding each screenshot from scratch ignores how much of a screen repeats, both within a screenshot and across screen-shots. (i) Spatial redundancy arises within a screenshot, where backgrounds, margins, and blank panels span large areas (Figure 2(a)). (ii) Temporal redundancy arises across screenshots, where typing or moving the cursor changes a few regions and leaves the rest of the screen as it was (Figure 2(b)). Natural video has little of either: texture varies within a frame, and camera motion alters most pixels between frames. Figure 2(d) quantifies this. In screen recordings, 67–77% of the pixel blocks that become visual tokens repeat a neigh-bor exactly or are unchanged from the previous screenshot, against 6–19% in natural video. An encoder that treats every screenshot as new thus spends most of its work on redundant tokens. It could instead skip blocks that repeat a neighbor, reuse the computation of blocks unchanged since the previous screenshot, and recompute only the rest. STREAM EMBED builds on this idea (§4). 

## 4 STREAM EMBED 

STREAM EMBED exploits this redundancy (§3.2) by encod-ing screenshots as a stream, computing only what has not been shown before. Spatial pruning removes redundancy within a screenshot (§4.1), temporal reuse removes redun-dancy across screenshots by recomputing only changed blocks (§4.2), and a consistency adapter keeps the embed-ding of the reduced input close to that of the full screenshot (§4.3). Figure 3 shows how a screenshot is indexed. 

Embedding a screenshot. A vision-language embedding model (Ma et al., 2024), such as Qwen3-VL-2B (Li et al., 2026), encodes a screenshot in three steps. First, a vision encoder (ViT) (Dosovitskiy et al., 2021) turns each 16 ×16 

patch into a vector. Second, each 2×2 group of patches, a 32 ×32 pixel block, forms one visual token. Third, a language model (LM) reads these tokens between a fixed text prefix and suffix, and its normalized last-layer hidden state at the last token is the embedding E(s). The ViT and the LM stack self-attention layers, in which each token forms a query, key, and value and attends to the keys and values of all tokens, bidirectionally in the ViT and causally in the LM. A 1280 ×720 screenshot, resized to 1280 ×704 ,becomes a 22 ×40 grid of 880 blocks and tokens.

We fine-tune the embedding model with LoRA (Hu et al., 2022) on the training split of S CREEN MEM EVAL (§5.3) and use it to embed questions. 5.2 Questions 

SME has single- and multi-recording questions q, answered by one ( n = 1 ) or several ( n ≥ 2) recordings (Table 3). 

Single-recording questions. A single-recording question asks where, when, what, or how a task was done, or which value was entered, and is phrased in the first person, as the worker or the persona would ask it. Opus 5.5 (Anthropic, 2026) writes one of each type for each recording, based on its annotations, which describe the task, application, and time. The generator never sees screenshots or OCR text, so it favors neither index type. Most screenshots in every history show a clock, so times can be matched on screen. To keep each answer unique, the same model, as a judge, compares each question with similar recordings, discards it if another recording answers differently, and labels record-ings with the same answer as relevant. For SME-Agent, GPT-6-astra (OpenAI, 2026b) also checks that the record-ing’s screens and actions support the answer.

## 6 EXPERIMENTS 

We first compare S TREAM EMBED with text and screenshot indexes (§6.2). We then index a long stream, simulating up to five years of screenshots to test how different index types scale (§6.3). Finally, we apply S TREAM EMBED to other encoders (§6.4) and ablate its components (§6.5). 

6.1 Setup Datasets. We evaluate on the three histories of SME (§5). Fine-tuned models are trained on 1,000 questions about the earliest recordings of SME-H1, and all checkpoints and set-tings are selected on 315 questions about the next 79 record-ings; neither set overlaps with the test questions. SME-H1 is thus in-domain for every fine-tuned model, SME-H2 tests transfer to another worker, and SME-Agent is out-of-domain, recorded from a computer-use agent on another operating system (§5.1). 

Baselines. We compare S TREAM EMBED with text and screenshot indexes. A text index extracts the OCR text of each screenshot with Apple Vision OCR (Apple Inc.) for GPU latency and with RapidOCR (RapidAI, 2021) for CPU latency, since Apple Vision OCR runs on the Neural Engine. It then ranks the text with BM25 (Robertson & Zaragoza, 2009), or embeds it with Qwen3-Emb-0.6B, -4B, or -8B (Zhang et al., 2025) (and gte-modernbert-base (Li et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024) in Figure 1) or with the embedding model Qwen3-VL-2B (Li et al., 2026). We also fuse the ranking of Qwen3-VL-2B with that of BM25 by reciprocal rank fusion (Cormack et al., 2009). A screenshot 

index embeds the full screenshot with the NaFlex variants of SigLIP2-B and SigLIP2-So400m (SigLIP2-So) (Tschannen et al., 2025), VultronRetriever-Flash (Vultron) (Vultr, 2026), or Qwen3-VL-2B or -8B (Li et al., 2026). Qwen3-VL-2B serves as both a text and a screenshot encoder in the main comparison, used base or fine-tuned with LoRA (FT). 

Implementation. STREAM EMBED uses τ = δ = 8 , tiles of 3×3 blocks, ρ = 0 .9, and R = 10 , chosen on the SME-H1 validation questions, and encodes screenshots with the consistency adapter (§4.3). The Qwen3-VL screenshot baselines run as released, with a Conv3d patch embedding. STREAM EMBED computes it with an equivalent linear one (§6.5). LoRA and the consistency adapter are merged into the weights and do not change latency. 

Metrics. We report (i) Recall@10 of each dataset for ac-curacy and (ii) indexing latency for cost, the mean time to encode a screenshot and add it to the index on the GPU and CPU of an Apple M2 Pro, including OCR for the text index. We measure latency over the first 50 consecutive screenshots, or all if fewer, of 30, 30, and 20 recordings sampled from SME-H1, SME-H2, and SME-Agent.

Table 5 applies S TREAM EMBED with the same thresholds to four other screenshot encoders, each fine-tuned with LoRA on SME-H1, and compares it with each encoder on full screenshots. 

STREAM EMBED generalizes to every encoder, staying close to its full-screenshot accuracy. It lowers the GPU latency of all five encoders, by 1.3–1.9 × on SigLIP2 and Vultron and by 2.5–3.7 × on Qwen3-VL, while losing only 1.3 R@10 on average. 

The speedup grows with the computation that reuse can skip. STREAM EMBED speeds up the Qwen3-VL encoders by 2.5–3.7 × on the GPU, since temporal reuse also saves computation in the language model. On SigLIP2, which has no language model, and on Vultron, whose language model mostly uses linear attention, S TREAM EMBED reuses keys and values only in the vision encoder and still speeds them up by 1.3–1.9 × on the GPU and 1.5–2.2 × on the CPU. On Vultron, which stores one vector per visual token, pruning also stores 23–30% fewer vectors per screenshot, trading some accuracy on SME-Agent for a smaller index. 6.5 Ablation 

Table 6 adds the components of S TREAM EMBED one at a time to the released Qwen3-VL-2B, fine-tuned with LoRA, starting with the equivalent linear patch embedding that STREAM EMBED uses for Qwen3-VL (§6.1). 

STREAM EMBED speeds up encoding by 2.8–3.7 × over the released model, mostly through spatial pruning and temporal reuse. Spatial pruning removes the repetition within a screenshot and temporal reuse removes the rep-etition across screenshots, each speeding up encoding by about 1.5 × at about 1 R@10. Their savings thus multiply, to 2.0–2.6 × together. Their overhead is small, as pruning and change detection add about 0.01 s per screenshot, and in the final configuration the vision encoder and the language model each run two to three times faster than without them. 

Grid keep and the consistency adapter recover the accu-racy lost due to pruning on SME-Agent. Pruning every redundant block costs far more on SME-Agent than on the human histories, suggesting that dropping every redundant block shifts the embedding. Grid keep recovers most of this loss for about 0.15 s per screenshot, and the consis-tency adapter recovers more at no latency cost, leaving STREAM EMBED within 1.3 R@10 of the full screenshot on all three histories. 

## 7 RELATED WORK 

Screen history. Re-finding what was once on a computer screen has long been studied, with people searching the files, e-mail, and web pages they have opened (Dumais et al., 2003) or browsing past screenshots (Hu & Lee, 2020). Such histories serve not only people but also agents, which complete more later tasks when they reuse past experi-ence (Wang et al., 2024; Agashe et al., 2025) or see past screenshots rather than summaries (Luo et al., 2026). Recent systems build such histories using vision-language models (VLMs), turning screenshots into the text memory of an agent (Wang & Chen, 2025) or a log of each task (Yin et al., 2026), or answering questions over them (Li et al., 2025). These systems rely on large cloud models (Wang & Chen, 2025; Li et al., 2025) or, to run on the device, process only some screenshots (Yin et al., 2026), and either way repre-sent screenshots as text. Yet retrieving from pixels is more accurate than from text on documents and the web (Ma et al., 2024; Faysse et al., 2025). We thus keep every screenshot as an embedding of its pixels, computed on the device. 

Growing indexes. Earlier work has studied indexes that grow over time, but it rarely treats the cost of encoding each new item as the bottleneck. Real-time search indexes text, which is cheap (Busch et al., 2012), and updatable vector indexes leave embedding as a separate step (Xu et al., 2023). Work on evolving corpora studies how to add documents to a trained retriever (Kishore et al., 2023) and when to update it (Ko et al., 2025; Kim et al., 2024). For screenshots, however, encoding is the bottleneck. Work that faces this cost encodes less or defers it, using cheaper models (Hsieh et al., 2018), fewer frames (Kang et al., 2020), early exit (Cai et al., 2025), smaller encoders (Teiletche et al., 2025), or lower resolution (Wang et al., 2026c). Instead, we remove only the computation that screens repeat, which is combined with a smaller encoder, lower resolution, or a skip gate. A growing index must also stay small. Lyu et al. (2026) re-rank compressed codes from disk, and Wang et al. (2026b) recompute embeddings at search time on a laptop. We study how to fit a screenshot index in a laptop’s memory. 

Streaming VLMs. Streaming VLMs keep each arriving frame in their context to answer questions about the video at any time (Chen et al., 2024), so their main concern is what to keep. Instead, we store each screenshot in an index for later queries, as in Jeong et al. (2025), so our main concern is how fast each one is encoded. This leads to three differences. These models keep tokens or their keys and values, which they evict, compress (Xu et al., 2025a), or retrieve for each question (Di et al., 2025; Ning et al., 2026). Our index holds only one embedding per screenshot. Methods that cut computation across frames decide what to skip inside or after the vision encoder (Yao et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2026d). Others decide from pixels but reuse computation in at most one of the encoder and the LM (Choudhury et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2025b). In contrast, our method decides fast from pixels before the encoder and reuses unchanged blocks in both. Finally, prior work keeps accuracy with modules added at inference (Aggarwal & Welleck, 2026; Hwang et al., 2025; Soldan et al., 2025). Our adapter is instead a LoRA merged into the weights, adding no latency. 

## 8 CONCLUSION 

We introduced S TREAM EMBED , which indexes a grow-ing screenshot history in real time by encoding it as a stream. Since a rendered screen repeats most of its blocks, STREAM EMBED prunes blocks that repeat a neighbor, reuses keys and values of unchanged blocks in both the vision encoder and the language model, and keeps the re-duced embedding close to the full one at no added cost. On SCREEN MEM EVAL , built from the screenshot histories of human workers and a computer-use agent, S TREAM EMBED 

matches a text index in latency and a screenshot index in ac-curacy, while binary codes keep a year of screenshots within the laptop’s memory. Future work could learn what to re-compute from the stream itself, lowering cost and raising accuracy further. Agents could also search a person’s screen-shot history to complete long-horizon tasks that depend on screens seen days or weeks earlier. ## A BENCHMARK DETAILS 

Table 7 compares SCREEN MEM EVAL with Screen-shotVQA (Wang & Chen, 2025), a screen benchmark. 

Questions. The generator writes questions for 12 record-ings at a time from their annotations, which are the instruc-tion, application, times, and planned steps for SME-H1 and SME-H2, and the sub-task instruction, the agent’s actions, and the scheduled time for SME-Agent. To keep answers unique, the judge compares each question with up to 30 recordings of the same history chosen from annotations alone, such as recordings of the same task or on the same site, and always every sibling sub-task in SME-Agent. No evaluated index chooses them, so the labels favor neither index type. Each count recount (§5.2) runs over a shuffled list of the candidate recordings. 

Relevance and splits. For screenshot-level scoring on SME-Agent, GPT-6-astra marks the keyframes that show each question’s evidence, and each keyframe counts with its run of near-identical screenshots. Training uses the earliest 710 recordings of SME-H1, and the 1,121 questions about the 433 of them without training questions are test questions. Training and validation recordings stay in the index and are relevant to 54 test questions, and removing these questions changes R@10 by at most 0.03 for both the fine-tuned full screenshot and S TREAM EMBED .

Data. SME-H1 and SME-H2 come from Howland et al. (2025), recorded by consenting workers who authorized public release, under the MIT license. SME-Agent shows a synthetic persona and holds no data of real people. We release all three histories with their questions, relevance labels, and splits.

## E LATENCY BREAKDOWN 

Table 10 splits the GPU latency of S TREAM EMBED into its vision encoder (ViT) and language model (LM). With reuse in the ViT only, the LM reuses the prefix before the first changed token. Pruning and reuse make the ViT 2.8 × and the LM 2.3 × faster than with the linear patch embedding alone, and the ViT 4.9 × faster than in the released model. On the CPU of SME-H1, the ViT runs 3.2 × faster (3.72 to 1.16 s) and the LM 2.2 × faster (1.69 to 0.78 s). 

Speedup and redundancy. STREAM EMBED computes 1.95, 2.67, and 1.79 TFLOPs per screenshot on SME-H1, SME-H2, and SME-Agent, or 32%, 43%, and 29% of the 6.17 TFLOPs of a full screenshot. This follows the redun-dancy of each history, where 72.4%, 63.3%, and 75.6% of blocks repeat a neighbor or are unchanged (Appendix G), and grid keep, refresh, and the suffix add a little work to the remaining 24–37%. On the GPU, S TREAM EMBED is accordingly 3.5 ×, 2.8 ×, and 3.7 × faster than the released model. Its 99th-percentile latency, 1.47–1.77 s, stays below the mean of the released full screenshot, 2.33–2.34 s. 

Memory. By our count from the model configuration, the cache of temporal reuse holds at most about 0.46 GB in fp16 for Qwen3-VL-2B and does not grow with the history.
