Abstract 

Omni-modal agents are expected to move be-yond multimodal understanding toward com-pleting complex tasks in realistic multimodal environments. In these settings, information is distributed across heterogeneous files and modalities, with cross-file and cross-modal de-pendencies requiring agents to continually co-ordinate perception, reasoning, and tool use as newly gathered evidence informs subsequent actions. However, existing omni-modal bench-marks primarily focus on multimodal percep-tion and understanding, leaving complex task completion through sustained interaction with multimodal workspaces underexplored. We in-troduce Omni-Workspace , a benchmark com-prising 380 deep-research-style tasks across four multimodal workspace types and two dif-ficulty levels. These tasks require agents to inspect and process workspace files, retrieve external information when needed, integrate ev-idence across files and modalities, and produce artifacts satisfying explicit requirements. Eval-uating 18 representative models reveals limi-tations in long-horizon multimodal workspace execution, with the best model scoring only 65.93 on average. Further analysis identifies multimodal context inflation and task-state drift as recurring challenges. Motivated by these findings, we further propose OmniFocus , a lightweight approach combining multimodal working-memory management with explicit task-state tracking to improve performance and efficiency. Omni-Workspace advances the eval-uation and development of omni-modal agents toward autonomous completion of complex tasks in multimodal workspaces. 

1 Introduction 

Recent advances in omni-modal large language models (Omni-LLMs) enable unified processing of textual, visual, and auditory information (Fu et al., 2025a; Li et al., 2025b; Xu et al., 2025a,b; Qwen Team, 2026c; Google DeepMind, 2026a), provid-ing a foundation for agents that perceive and reason over multimodal content (Lin et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026; Xing et al., 2026). Real-world applications, however, require these agents to go beyond multi-modal understanding and complete complex tasks in multimodal environments. In applications such as Google Gemini Notebook, users bring together documents and media in a workspace for analysis and synthesis (Google, 2026). For example, pro-ducing structured meeting minutes may require an agent to locate key discussions in meeting record-ings and connect them with presentation slides and chat logs. An ambiguous reference in a record-ing may prompt the agent to consult the slides or subsequent messages before drawing a conclusion. Such tasks require agents to continually coordinate perception, reasoning, and tool use within multi-modal workspaces to gather, process, and integrate information across files and modalities and produce artifacts that satisfy user requirements. Yet existing omni-modal benchmarks primarily focus on multimodal perception and understand-ing (Zhang et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025a; Tao et al., 2026). OmniGAIA extends evaluation to multi-modal reasoning and multi-step tool use (Li et al., 2026), while Omni-DeepSearch combines multi-modal clue extraction with cross-modal search to produce concise answers (Yu et al., 2026). Both provide multimodal inputs directly in the initial context, rather than requiring agents to actively discover and process multiple multimodal files in the workspace to extract and integrate information across files and modalities. Workspace-Bench eval-uates workspace tasks with cross-file dependen-cies (Tang et al., 2026), but offers limited cover-age of audio and video. It remains underexplored whether omni-modal agents can complete com-plex tasks through sustained interaction with persis-tent multimodal workspaces, integrating evidence across files and modalities to produce artifacts that satisfy explicit requirements. Workspace , a benchmark of 380 deep-research-style tasks for evaluating omni-modal agents on cross-file and cross-modal information processing in multimodal workspaces. It spans two difficulty levels (L1 and L2), four workspace types, and 10 topics. Each task pairs a user instruction with a workspace containing files such as audio, video, images, and documents. As shown in Figure 1, agents must coordinate perception, reasoning, and tool use to autonomously discover, inspect, and process relevant files and search the open web for supporting information. They must continually in-tegrate evidence across files and modalities to guide subsequent actions and produce artifacts meeting task requirements. Construction involves collect-ing and annotating publicly available media, as-sembling workspaces through cross-file and cross-modal links, and designing tasks with multi-stage verification for validity and solvability. Evaluating omni-modal agents on Omni-Workspace reveals two challenges. First, raw mul-timodal observations can accumulate in context across turns, leading to multimodal context inflation 

and increased processing overhead. Second, agents may lose track of task requirements, collected ev-idence, unresolved information needs, and execu-tion progress as these become scattered across the growing interaction history, resulting in task-state drift . These challenges are accompanied by re-peated file inspection, overlooked information gaps, unnecessary searches, and premature or delayed termination. Motivated by these observations, we propose OmniFocus , a lightweight extension to the agent harness that combines multimodal working-memory management with explicit task-state track-ing to mitigate both challenges and improve task performance and execution efficiency. We evaluate 18 representative models under a unified agent harness, including native omni-modal models and vision-language models (VLMs) equipped with audio tools. Omni-Workspace re-mains challenging: the best-performing model achieves an average score of 65.93, while most models score below 60. OmniFocus also substan-tially reduces execution overhead on representa-tive backbone models while improving task perfor-mance. These results suggest that managing mul-timodal context and explicitly tracking task state can help agents complete complex workspace tasks more effectively and efficiently. Our main contri-butions are threefold: • We introduce Omni-Workspace , a bench-mark of 380 deep-research-style tasks for evaluating omni-modal agents in multimodal workspaces. These tasks require agents to co-ordinate perception, reasoning, and tool use across turns to gather, process, and integrate information across files and modalities and produce artifacts meeting task requirements. 2• We systematically evaluate 18 representative models, revealing agents’ limitations in com-pleting complex multimodal workspace tasks and identifying multimodal context inflation and task-state drift as recurring challenges. • We analyze execution failures and identify re-curring challenges in context management and task-state maintenance. These findings inform OmniFocus, a lightweight harness enhance-ment whose evaluation illustrates practical op-portunities to improve task completion and execution efficiency. 

2 Related Work 

Omni-modal benchmarks have expanded from me-dia understanding to tool-assisted problem solv-ing. OmniEval, OmniVideoBench, and LVOm-niBench assess multimodal comprehension and reasoning (Zhang et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025a; Tao et al., 2026), while OmniGAIA and Omni-DeepSearch incorporate multi-step tool use and external evidence retrieval (Li et al., 2026; Yu et al., 2026). Despite differing reasoning and tool require-ments, these benchmarks largely organize tasks around multimodal inputs supplied with individual questions. However, agents carrying out complex tasks in practical environments need to work with information distributed across multiple files. They must decide what to inspect and process as the task unfolds, integrating findings across files and modalities to fulfill the user’s request. Workspace-Bench takes a step toward evaluating such task execution through contextual reasoning over inter-dependent workspace files (Tang et al., 2026), but offers limited coverage of audio and video. Omni-Workspace examines this setting for omni-modal agents, requiring them to acquire, process, and in-tegrate information across multimodal workspace files to produce task-specified artifacts. 

3 Omni-Workspace 

3.1 Task Formulation 

Each Omni-Workspace task pairs a natural-language user goal with a multimodal workspace and requires one or more target files. We formulate a task as 

T = ( G, W 0, A, R ), (1) where G specifies the task objective and delivery requirements, including formats and required con-tent; W0 is the initial workspace containing het-erogeneous multimodal files; A is the task-specific tool and action space; and R is a task-specific eval-uation rubric hidden from the agent. The agent re-ceives G and accesses workspace contents through tool calls. At step t, executing an action at ∈ A 

returns an observation for subsequent decisions and may modify the workspace: 

Wt+1 = FW (Wt, a t), (2) where Wt denotes the workspace file state and FW

captures the effects of tool execution. Tasks are designed to require information integration across files and modalities, supplemented by information from the open Web, to produce target files. At termination, the target files are collected from the specified paths and evaluated against R.

3.2 Data Collection and Task Construction 

Figure 2 summarizes the construction pipeline. 

Multimodal source pool. We build an ini-tial source pool comprising approximately 10K videos from public video platforms and FineV-ideo (Farré et al., 2024), 20K audio tracks extracted from FineVideo videos, and 20K images from LiveVQA (Fu et al., 2025b) and COCO 2017 (Lin et al., 2014). Gemini 3.1 Pro (Google DeepMind, 2026a) generates modality-specific structured anno-tations that capture temporal, semantic, and entity-level information. Using the media annotations as search cues, a Search Agent based on Qwen3.7-Max (Qwen Team, 2026d) identifies related people, organizations, locations, works, and events through Web search and browsing. It also collects veri-fiable facts about these entities to support subse-quent workspace construction and task design. The Search Agent is instructed to prioritize information that requires multiple search steps to obtain and to avoid facts that are common knowledge or directly available in a single search-result snippet. 

Entity-linked workspace construction. We nor-malize entity mentions across the annotated me-dia and retrieved web information, and establish cross-file and cross-modal links through shared entities and semantic relations. Related files are organized into groups based on these links, and groups with compatible entities and relations are combined into candidate workspaces. Workspace-grounded task design. We use GPT-5.5 (OpenAI, 2026) as the Task-Generation Agent. For each candidate workspace, the agent receives media annotations, cross-file relationships, re-trieved Web information, and specifications of the tool subset available to the evaluated agent. It uses these relationships to design a task requiring in-formation integration across files and modalities, supplemented by information retrieved from the open Web. The agent generates a user instruction specifying the task objective and delivery require-ments for target files. It also generates gold result files and a task-specific evaluation rubric for sub-sequent validation. Candidate workspaces are dis-carded when the generator cannot formulate a task meeting these requirements with the assigned tools. Further details are provided in Appendix B.1. 

Quality control. Candidate tasks undergo three types of LLM-based checks. Solvability and ne-cessity checks assess whether the required infor-mation can be obtained from the workspace and open web using the provided tools, and whether the specified files, modalities, and tool contribute substantively to task completion. Answer leakage 

checks inspect user instructions, filenames, meta-data, and templates for unintended answer disclo-sure. Evaluation correctness checks assess whether the gold result files are correct and satisfy the task instruction. They also examine whether the rubric covers the required content and format, with clear scoring criteria consistent with these files. Three annotators with graduate-level research experience subsequently review task clarity, feasibility, and rubric quality, including the alignment of scoring criteria with task requirements. Issues identified at either stage are addressed through revision by the task-generation agent and subsequent recheck-ing; tasks are discarded when reliable repair is in-feasible. Together, these checks help ensure task validity and consistency among task requirements and evaluation rubrics. Detailed are provided in Appendix B.2.

3.4 Evaluation Harness Agent harness. Each task runs in an isolated, persistent multimodal workspace. The agent re-ceives the user instruction and tool schemas, and uses tools to inspect and process workspace files, retrieve web information, and produce the re-quired output files. Tools support workspace ac-cess, multimodal perception, code execution, me-dia processing, web search, and execution control (Appendix C.1). The harness follows an itera-tive reasoning–action–observation loop (Yao et al., 2023): it executes the agent’s tool calls and appends the results to the interaction context for subsequent decisions. The agent must invoke tools to read mul-timodal files, and the returned media are inserted into the context as multimodal inputs for the model to inspect. Execution terminates when the agent invokes the finish tool or reaches the maximum number of interaction turns. 

Evaluation protocol. After execution, the eval-uator collects the required output files. If any re-quired file is missing or unreadable, the entire task receives a score of zero. Otherwise, the submit-ted files are assessed using a task-specific rubric comprising atomic Content and Format checks. Rule-based verifiers evaluate deterministic require-ments, including file types, required fields, struc-tural constraints, and numerical results with spec-ified tolerances. We use an LLM-as-a-judge ap-proach (Zheng et al., 2023) to assess semantic requirements. The judge evaluates each atomic check using the submitted files and the task-specific rubric, assigning a binary score: satisfied (1) or not satisfied (0). Model settings and the agent and judge prompts are provided in Appendix C.2. Atomic check scores are aggregated within the con-tent and format categories according to the rubric to obtain Scontent and Sformat , both on a 0–100 scale. The overall task score combines these components using a weight λ (0 ≤ λ ≤ 1): 

Soverall = λS content + (1 − λ)Sformat (3) 

3.5 OmniFocus 

Long-horizon tasks in Omni-Workspace require agents to inspect multimodal files and integrate information across interaction turns. Raw media returned by tools can remain in subsequent inputs after relevant information has been extracted, lead-ing to multimodal context inflation and increased processing overhead. Meanwhile, observations and execution progress become scattered across the in-teraction history, making it difficult to track com-pleted requirements, remaining gaps, and termi-nation conditions. This task-state drift can lead to repeated file inspection, unnecessary searches, and delayed termination. To address these chal-lenges, we introduce OmniFocus , a lightweight extension to the agent harness with two comple-mentary mechanisms that help keep subsequent reasoning and action focused on task-relevant evi-dence and unfinished requirements. 

Multimodal working-memory management. 

OmniFocus helps the agent focus on task-relevant evidence by retaining key observations from in-spected media as textual records for subsequent reasoning. When tools return images, audio, or video, the harness prompts the agent to record key observations. The agent generates this record to-gether with its next action in the same response. Explicit task-state maintenance. OmniFocus maintains a structured task state to track which requirements have been satisfied and what remains to be done. The agent initializes this state from the user goal. For each requirement, it records the completion condition, acquired information, processing progress, and remaining gaps or con-flicts. The agent updates the state based on new observations and execution results, and the harness includes the latest state in subsequent model inputs. This requirement-wise summary helps the agent focus on unfinished requirements and decide when to gather additional information, generate and val-idate output files, or terminate. Together, these mechanisms aim to improve both task performance and execution efficiency. Tool interfaces and the execution workflow are detailed in Appendix D.

4.2 Main Results Overall performance. Table 1 reports the main results on Omni-Workspace. Gemini 3.5 Flash achieves the highest overall score of 65.93, closely followed by Qwen3.7-Plus at 64.78. Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview and Qwen3.6-Plus rank next, scor-ing 59.89 and 58.34, respectively. Only the top two models score above 60, indicating that Omni-Workspace remains challenging for current agents. 

Native omni-modal agents and VLM+ASR agents. The strongest agents from the two categories achieve comparable overall perfor-mance, with different strengths across task sub-sets. Qwen3.7-Plus leads on V+A+T, V+I+T, and L2, whereas Gemini 3.5 Flash leads on the other two workspace types and L1. Among native omni-modal agents, the strongest performance is concen-trated in the Gemini family; other models, partic-ularly smaller open-weight models, score substan-tially lower. Although these models support native multimodal perception, they struggle to coordinate perception and tool invocation over multiple steps to produce the required deliverables. 

Performance across difficulty levels. All eval-uated models score lower on L2 than on L1. This gap is also evident among the strongest agents: Gemini 3.5 Flash scores 72.77 on L1 and 57.38 on L2, while Qwen3.7-Plus scores 68.70 and 59.88, respectively. L2 tasks require agents to transform or analyze media using code and media-processing tools and integrate the resulting information into subsequent reasoning. The consistently lower L2 scores indicate that these processing-dependent tasks remain more challenging for current agents. 

Performance across workspace types. No sin-gle workspace type consistently yields the lowest scores across models. A notable contrast emerges on A+I+T, where native omni-modal agents gener-ally maintain relatively strong performance, while several VLM+ASR agents obtain their lowest workspace scores. Gemini 3.5 Flash achieves its highest workspace score of 68.42 on A+I+T, whereas Qwen3.6-Plus, Kimi K2.6, and MiniMax-M3 obtain their lowest scores on this subset, at 54.37, 43.92, and 49.37, respectively. This pat-tern motivates a closer comparison of native omni-modal and VLM-based agents on audio-focused tasks in Section 5.1.

5.2 Error Analysis 

To understand how agents fail in long-horizon mul-timodal tasks, we analyze two types of errors: local execution errors and task-state drift errors . Lo-cal execution errors concern individual operations, including perception, search, and tool invocation. Task-state drift errors concern failures to maintain requirements, evidence, and execution progress across reasoning steps, manifested as requirement omission, evidence loss or misuse, and redundant tool calls. As shown in Figure 4(b), multimodal perception and search errors dominate local exe-cution failures, while requirement omission is the most common form of task-state drift. Evidence loss or misuse and redundant tool calls reveal fail-ures that extend across steps: agents may misapply acquired evidence or continue using tools without substantive progress. These findings highlight the need to track requirements, retain and correctly use evidence, and update actions based on execu-tion outcomes, providing motivation for the design of OmniFocus. More details are provided in Ap-pendix E.3. 

5.3 OmniFocus: Effectiveness and Efficiency 

We evaluate OmniFocus on Gemini 3 Flash and Gemini 3.1 Pro against three ablations: without task-state maintenance, without memory manage-ment, and without both components (the baseline). As shown in Table 2, full OmniFocus achieves the highest reported score and the lowest input-token use and estimated cost for both models. Relative to the baseline, scores increase by 5.11 and 5.06 points, input tokens decrease by 24.6% and 56.0%, and estimated costs decrease by 16.2% and 47.0%, respectively. The two ablations reveal distinct con-tributions. Removing task-state maintenance low-ers scores by 3.63 and 1.72 points and increases tool calls from 25.62 to 32.17 for Gemini 3 Flash and from 27.62 to 29.51 for Gemini 3.1 Pro. This pattern is consistent with explicit task state helping agents organize actions around task requirements. Removing memory management has a smaller ef-fect on score (0.68 and 0.75 points), but raises input-token use by 71.2% and 114.0% relative to full OmniFocus. It also raises estimated cost by 58.4% and 87.9%, respectively, suggesting that compacting multimodal observations primarily con-trols context and expense. Detailed comparisons are provided in Appendix E.4. 

6 Conclusion 

We introduced Omni-Workspace , a benchmark of 380 deep-research-style tasks requiring omni-modal agents to continually coordinate perception, reasoning, and tool use in multimodal workspaces. Agents must gather, process, and integrate informa-tion across files and modalities, retrieve supporting web information, and produce artifacts meeting explicit requirements. Evaluation of 18 representa-tive models reveals substantial room for improve-ment, with a best average score of 65.93. Our analysis identifies multimodal context inflation and task-state drift as recurring challenges, motivating OmniFocus, which combines multimodal working-memory management with explicit task-state track-ing. OmniFocus improves scores while substan-tially reducing input-token use and estimated cost on both evaluated backbones. We hope Omni-Workspace supports further evaluation and develop-ment of omni-modal agents capable of completing complex tasks in multimodal workspaces. 8Limitations 

There are several limitations in this work. First, although the benchmark requires sustained inter-action with multimodal workspaces, the current workspaces remain moderate in scale, containing 5–12 initial files per task. As a result, they do not yet capture the full range of challenges that may arise in much larger and noisier real-world workspaces containing hundreds or thousands of files. Second, Omni-Workspace primarily focuses on deep-research-style tasks that require agents to gather, process, verify, and synthesize informa-tion across multimodal files and the Web before producing structured artifacts. In real-world ap-plications, omni-modal agents may also support a broader range of workflows beyond information-centric tasks, such as video editing, multimedia content creation, game development, and other forms of multimodal artifact creation and manipu-lation. Given the substantially greater complexity of these workflows and the challenges of construct-ing and reliably validating them at scale, Omni-Workspace does not currently cover these task types.

Table 3 compares representative benchmarks for omni-modal understanding, multimodal agents, and workspace task execution along four dimen-sions: modality coverage, task dependencies, agen-tic operations, and output forms. Existing omni-modal understanding benchmarks primarily evaluate perception and reasoning over task-provided multimodal inputs, while multimodal agent benchmarks further incorporate multi-step reasoning, external information retrieval, and tool use. Workspace-Bench extends evaluation to per-sistent file environments, emphasizing cross-file de-pendencies and workspace-level task completion, but provides limited coverage of audio, video, and cross-modal media processing. Omni-Workspace focuses on dependency-driven execution across heterogeneous multimodal files. Its tasks require agents to integrate evidence across files and modalities, perform media-processing op-erations when needed, and reuse intermediate ar-tifacts in subsequent steps. By combining these requirements with Web retrieval and structured ar-tifact generation, Omni-Workspace evaluates sus-tained coordination of perception, reasoning, and tool use in multimodal workspaces. 

B Dataset Construction and Quality Control 

B.1 Construction Details Media annotation. We use modality-specific an-notation procedures to capture both semantic con-tent and perceptual cues, and organize the results into structured entity records for subsequent link-ing. Videos are first segmented into scenes using PySceneDetect 1. Each segment receives a chrono-logical description integrating visual events, visible text, speech, and non-speech sounds. Segment de-scriptions are then combined with their timestamps into a video-level narrative, from which entities are extracted and consolidated across segments. Au-dio is annotated along its timeline, capturing spo-ken content, speaker turns, non-speech events, and acoustic characteristics. Images receive descrip-tions of their scenes, objects, spatial relationships, and textual or graphical content, together with en-tity annotations. Across modalities, entity records include descriptions, semantic tags, and searchabil-ity information to support Web-based entity expan-

sion and cross-file association. Available reference text assists interpretation, while descriptions are grounded in the media. 

Workspace assembly. We merge entity-expansion records from video, audio, and image sources and normalize entity names into shared identifiers. Files are linked through shared entities or related entities discovered during expansion. Generic and excessively frequent entities are filtered to reduce uninformative connections. We construct connected groups of two to four files and assemble candidate workspaces from one or more groups. Sampling accounts for modality coverage, link strength, file counts, overlap between groups, and per-file reuse budgets. 

Task specification. Task generation is carried out through a multi-step agent workflow. The task-generation agent receives file annotations, cross-file entity links, retrieved Web evidence, and a task-specific set of tool schemas. Across successive steps, it examines these materials, selects relevant files and evidence paths, and uses available tools to inspect metadata, check computations, or cre-ate supporting files as needed. It progressively constructs an internal reference chain specifying the input file or information, tool operation, ex-pected observation, and downstream dependency at each step. Observations from one file guide subse-quent file selection, interpretation, or Web retrieval; where processing is needed, media transformations or code execution produce intermediate results con-sumed by later steps. Evidence from the selected files is combined through comparison, filtering, ver-ification, or synthesis into a concrete deliverable. Candidate workspaces are discarded if they can-not support a cross-file, cross-modal task requiring additional Web information and a file deliverable with the assigned tools. For retained candidates, the agent translates the reference chain into a user instruction specifying the objective, output paths, formats, and required content without revealing the solution steps. It also creates supporting templates or constraint files when needed, expected output contents, and a corresponding rubric. Construc-tion annotations and the reference chain remain separate from the solver-visible workspace.

B.4 Validating the Workspace Setting 

We examine the role of an accessible, operable file workspace in task completion through a struc-tural audit and a comparison without workspace ac-cess. The audit characterizes dependencies among files and operations on them; the comparison tests whether initial multimodal context, web search, and general computation can substitute for continued interaction with workspace files. All 380 tasks require cross-file integration : infor-mation from multiple workspace files must be com-bined to produce the requested result. We further characterize strong cross-file dependency, media-file processing, and intermediate-artifact reuse. Ta-ble 5 summarizes their prevalence. 

Strong cross-file dependency. A strong cross-file dependency occurs when information from one input file is needed to select, locate, decode, align, or interpret evidence in another. This property ap-pears in 297 tasks (78.16%). For example, an auto-motive research task requires identifying a manu-facturer in an audio recording and using its name to select an auto-show photograph with a matching overhead brand sign. 

Media-file processing. The 169 L2 tasks (44.47%) require transformation or analysis of me-dia files to obtain information needed for the re-quested result. These files serve as inputs to tool or code operations. Such processing may directly re-turn measurements or other analytical results with-out producing a new file. L2 therefore concerns required operations on media files, independently 15 of whether a generated file is reused. 

Intermediate-artifact reuse. Intermediate-artifact reuse occurs when a file generated during execution serves as input to a subsequent operation. This property appears in 103 tasks (27.11%). For example, a cross-program research task requires using supplied calibration parameters to separate two audio channels with crosstalk and saving the recovered recording as an audio file. The agent then inspects this file to identify the speaker, program, and spoken content for subsequent verification. The generated file thus connects signal processing to semantic analysis. Unlike media-file processing alone, this property requires a generated file to be used in a later step. 

Comparison without workspace access. We compare Workspace , which allows continued in-spection and processing of input files and reuse of intermediate files, with No workspace , which re-moves file-based access to task materials. To make the initial materials available after removing the workspace, the latter supplies all initial file con-tents in the first model request: text is provided in full, and images, audio, and video as native multi-modal inputs. Filenames identify the materials but do not provide access to file objects. The supplied inputs remain in context throughout execution. Both settings permit multi-turn reasoning, web search, general-purpose computation, and final output-file creation. In No workspace , computation can operate on information available in context, but tools and code cannot read, transform, or analyze workspace files or consume generated intermedi-ate files. Final output-file creation is retained to preserve the same deliverable requirements and evaluation. Both settings use Gemini 3 Flash Preview on all 380 tasks, with at most 50 model requests per task, including the initial request. Workspace scores are reused from the original evaluation.

D OmniFocus Implementation Details 

We implement OmniFocus by introducing two tools into the agent harness: commit_media_evidence 

preserves task-relevant media observations as tex-tual records, while update_task_state maintains task requirements, acquired evidence, and com-pletion progress. Together, they provide working memory for subsequent reasoning and action selec-tion. 

D.1 Multimodal Working Memory 

When inspecting media returned by tools, the agent uses commit_media_evidence to record task-relevant observations and their sources, prefer-ably alongside its next actions. After this response, the harness replaces the inspected raw media with textual placeholders, retaining the observations for subsequent interactions. Source files remain in the workspace and can be revisited when the records are insufficient for later decisions. The tool takes four required arguments: 

media_action_id identifies the media from the current read, source_path specifies its source, 

observations is a list of task-relevant observa-tion strings, and task_relevance explains their relationship to the user request. 

D.2 Explicit Task-State Maintenance 

As evidence or progress changes, the agent uses 

update_task_state to update a checklist of re-quirements, their status, findings, and remaining gaps. Each submission replaces the previous check-list in full while retaining completed items. The harness appends the latest checklist to every model request, making updates visible from the next re-quest onward. The checklist reflects the model’s assessment of progress, rather than verified comple-tion, and does not directly determine termination. The tool takes one required argument, items , an array of objects representing the complete checklist. Each item has three required fields: requirement 

describes the task requirement; status is todo ,

done , or blocked ; and note preserves findings, source or artifact paths, and remaining gaps.

E.4 Further Analysis of OmniFocus Efficiency metric definitions. Input and output tokens are accumulated over all model requests within a task; output tokens include thinking tokens reported in the usage records, and k denotes 1,000 tokens. Tool Calls exclude internal media-record and task-state updates. Their token consumption, cost, and elapsed time remain included. Cost is estimated from input and output token usage using the corresponding model’s token prices, without accounting for caching or other pricing adjustments. It covers agent-model inference only, excluding judging and other tool costs. Time denotes per-task elapsed time.
