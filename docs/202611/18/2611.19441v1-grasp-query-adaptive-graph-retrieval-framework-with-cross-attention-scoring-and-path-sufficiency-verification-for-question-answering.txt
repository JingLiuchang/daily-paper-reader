A B S T R A C T 

Retrieval quality is a key factor in the reliability of retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), particularly when retrieval is performed over structured knowledge graphs. Graph-based retrieval requires deci-sions about candidate-edge scoring, traversal termination, and the sufficiency of the retrieved evidence. We propose GRASP, a query-adaptive graph retrieval framework that jointly addresses these decisions during the retrieval process. A cross-attention scorer models fine-grained interactions between individual query tokens and candidate edges, providing query-specific signals for edge selection. A learned stopping gate conditions traversal termination on the query, traversal state, and hop depth, allowing the retrieval depth to adapt to the current retrieval state. A sufficiency classifier further evaluates the assembled path before generation and triggers re-retrieval when the evidence is predicted to be incomplete. We evaluate GRASP on three biomedical question-answering benchmarks with different graph scales and answer lengths, using biomedical question answering as a representative setting for evaluating graph-based RAG retrieval. GRASP consistently improves over the strongest learned baseline across the evaluated retrieval and generation metrics. The unsupported-claim rate is reduced by 8.9 to 13.2 percentage points across the three benchmarks, with the largest reduction on the benchmark with the longest generated answers. The retrieval gains are more pronounced under higher candidate density, while ablation results indicate that the three components provide complementary improvements. 

## 1. Introduction 

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) grounds large language model (LLM) outputs in externally retrieved ev-idence, making retrieval quality an important factor in the reliability of knowledge-intensive tasks [1, 2]. Recent work has explored query reformulation, retrieval enhancement, and adaptive retrieval to improve the evidence supplied to the generator [3, 4]. When retrieval is performed over a knowledge graph (KG), however, retrieval quality involves more than identifying individually relevant facts. The re-triever must construct a connected path that provides suf-ficient evidence for answering the query. In particular, the relevance of retrieved edges does not necessarily imply that the assembled path covers the evidence required by the answer. Graph-based retrieval represents knowledge through en-tities and typed relations, enabling multi-hop retrieval when a query requires several connected facts [5, 6, 7]. This setting involves three related but distinct decisions: selecting relevant candidate edges, determining when to terminate traversal, and assessing whether the resulting path provides sufficient evidence for answering the query. These deci-sions address different aspects of retrieval. Candidate-edge scoring estimates the relevance of individual retrieval ac-tions, while traversal termination determines the depth of the current search. Neither decision, on its own, establishes

whether the accumulated path provides adequate support for the answer. A retrieval process may therefore terminate with a path containing relevant edges but lacking one or more necessary relations. If this incomplete path is passed directly to the generator, the resulting answer may contain claims that are not supported by the retrieved evidence. Existing approaches address different aspects of graph retrieval. Embedding-based scoring and graph-aware repre-sentations support candidate selection, while learned traver-sal policies condition path construction on the query and traversal history [8, 9, 10]. Other methods investigate graph-based evidence organization, subgraph or path selection, and iterative retrieval [11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17]. These ap-proaches provide complementary strategies for selecting and organizing graph evidence. However, candidate relevance and traversal termination do not necessarily establish path-level evidence sufficiency. In particular, when a retrieved path is incomplete, simply terminating traversal or passing the path to the generator does not resolve the missing evi-dence. This motivates a retrieval mechanism that not only constructs a path but also evaluates its sufficiency before generation and attempts to correct insufficient retrieval re-sults. To address this problem, we propose GRASP, a query-adaptive graph retrieval framework comprising five stages, labelled A to E. Stage A encodes the input query into representations that guide the subsequent graph retrieval.

Stage B constructs a candidate evidence path through query-conditioned edge scoring and adaptive traversal termination. Stage C evaluates whether the assembled path is structurally sufficient to support answering the query. Based on this assessment, Stage D determines whether the retrieved path should be accepted or another retrieval attempt should be ini-tiated. When the path is predicted to be insufficient, GRASP initiates a bounded re-retrieval procedure: it identifies a low-confidence retrieval action using its hop-level score margin, excludes that action in subsequent retrieval attempts, and restarts traversal from the entry entity to seek an alternative path. Once a path is accepted, Stage E uses the retrieved evidence as input to the language model to generate the clinical answer. Thus, Stages A and E provide the query-encoding and answer-generation interfaces of GRASP, re-spectively, while the central methodological contribution lies in coordinating path-level sufficiency assessment with targeted retrieval correction before generation, rather than relying solely on edge relevance and traversal termination. Stage B employs a cross-attention scorer to model in-teractions between individual query tokens and candidate edges, together with a learned stopping gate conditioned on the query, traversal state, and hop depth. The scorer provides fine-grained signals for selecting candidate actions, while the stopping gate controls the depth of the current traversal. Stage C addresses a separate question: whether the path assembled by these retrieval decisions provides sufficient structural support for the query. It encodes the ordered retrieval path and combines path-level information with retrieval confidence statistics, including edge scores, hop-level score margins, candidate uncertainty, and path length. The resulting sufficiency estimate is used to decide whether the path can proceed to generation or whether another re-trieval attempt is needed. Stage B is trained using hop-level supervision, whereas Stage C is trained separately using confidence statistics produced by the converged retrieval model. At inference time, the two stages interact through a retrieval verification and correction loop. Importantly, this mechanism treats insufficient retrieval as a retrieval-level problem to be addressed before generation, rather than relying on the generator to compensate for missing path evidence. We evaluate GRASP on three biomedical question-answering benchmarks: RiTeK, MedKGent-KG, and a PMC-based biomedical corpus [18, 19, 20]. The benchmarks cover different graph scales, retrieval settings, and answer-generation requirements. Across the evaluated benchmarks, GRASP improves over the strongest learned baseline on the reported retrieval and generation metrics. The unsupported claim rate decreases by 8.9 to 13.2 percentage points, with the largest reduction observed on the benchmark with the longest generated answers. Retrieval gains are also more pro-nounced in higher-density candidate settings, although this observation alone does not establish a causal relationship between graph density and performance. Ablation results in-dicate that the three components contribute complementary improvements. The main contributions of this paper are summarized as follows: 1. A query-adaptive graph retrieval framework, GRASP, is proposed to coordinate candidate-edge selection, traversal termination, and path-sufficiency verifica-tion. The framework distinguishes the relevance of individual retrieval actions from the sufficiency of the assembled evidence path, addressing the limitation that a traversal may terminate with relevant but in-complete evidence. Experiments on three biomedical question-answering benchmarks demonstrate consis-tent improvements over the strongest learned baseline across the evaluated retrieval and generation metrics. 2. A cross-attention-based edge scoring mechanism cou-pled with a learned stopping gate is developed to support fine-grained query-edge matching and adap-tive traversal depth. The scorer explicitly models token-level query-edge interactions, while the stop-ping gate conditions termination on the query and current traversal state. Ablation results demonstrate the complementary contributions of edge scoring and adaptive stopping to retrieval performance. 3. A path-sufficiency verification and retrieval correc-tion mechanism is introduced to assess assembled evidence before answer generation. Unlike a stopping decision based only on traversal state, this mechanism evaluates the retrieved path as a whole and identi-fies whether its structural evidence is predicted to be insufficient. When insufficiency is detected, GRASP uses hop-level confidence margins to exclude a low-confidence retrieval action and initiates a bounded al-ternative retrieval attempt. Experimental results show that GRASP reduces the unsupported-claim rate by 8.9 to 13.2 percentage points across the evaluated benchmarks, supporting the benefit of incorporating explicit path-sufficiency assessment and retrieval cor-rection into graph-based retrieval. Section 3 formulates the retrieval problem and introduces the training objectives. Section 4 describes the GRASP framework and its inference procedure. Section 5 presents the experimental results, in-cluding the ablation studies. The final section discusses the findings and concludes the paper.

2.2. Graph-Based Retrieval for Structured Knowledge 

Graph-based retrieval represents knowledge through en-tities and typed relations, allowing retrieval methods to ex-ploit connections among facts. Biomedical resources such as UMLS and curated biomedical networks provide structured knowledge for retrieval and reasoning [5, 6]. Graph neural networks, including graph convolutional and attention-based models, provide mechanisms for propagating information over graph structures and learning representations of related entities [29, 30]. Recent graph-based RAG methods differ in how they or-ganize graph information and select evidence for generation. GraphRAG, LightRAG, and HippoRAG use graph-based representations to organize related information for retrieval and generation [11, 12, 13], while KG2RAG incorporates knowledge-graph structure into evidence organization [14]. Other methods focus more directly on retrieving graph struc-tures or paths for language model reasoning. G-Retriever retrieves graph structures as context, whereas PathRAG selects path-based evidence [16, 15]. GRAG and QMKGF investigate graph retrieval under different graph structures and retrieval settings [17, 31]. Graph foundation models and language model-based approaches have also been explored for graph retrieval, including GFM-RAG and ByoKG-RAG [32, 33]. Taken together, these methods cover several aspects of graph-based evidence retrieval, including graph organiza-tion, representation learning, subgraph selection, and path-based retrieval. Their retrieval strategies vary with the graph representation and task setting, providing different ways to construct structured context for downstream generation. 

2.3. Learned Traversal for Multi-Hop Retrieval 

Learned traversal methods formulate graph retrieval as a sequence of decisions conditioned on the query and the cur-rent traversal state. MINERVA models multi-hop reasoning as a Markov decision process and trains a recurrent policy with REINFORCE to select relations and entities [9, 34].   

> Rui Xu et al.: Preprint Page 3 of 20 GRASP

DeepPath learns relation paths through policy gradients [8], while M-Walk incorporates Monte Carlo Tree Search to improve exploration during path construction [35]. GNN-RAG combines learned traversal with graph neural network reasoning over retrieved subgraphs [26]. These methods illustrate different approaches to query dependent path con-struction and graph reasoning. Two related aspects of traversal are candidate selection and termination. Candidate selection methods must distin-guish edges that may be semantically similar but differ in their relevance to the current query. Attention mechanisms model interactions between query tokens and candidate in-formation [10], while retrieval and reranking studies have explored query evidence alignment to improve evidence selection [3, 4]. For traversal termination, learned policies such as MINERVA incorporate a STOP action to determine whether to continue path expansion [9]. Candidate selection and termination therefore address different stages of the traversal process: one determines which edge to follow, while the other determines whether to continue exploring the graph. These decisions are also distinct from assessing whether the retrieved path contains sufficient evidence to answer the query. A traversal policy determines when to stop expanding a path, but that stopping decision does not by itself establish that the collected evidence supports an answer. This dis-tinction provides the context for studying retrieval methods that combine query dependent edge selection and traversal control with an explicit assessment of evidence sufficiency before generation.

This paper formulates graph retrieval as a sequential deci-sion problem with adaptive traversal and path-sufficiency verification.

## 4. Methodology 

GRASP is designed to improve graph-based retrieval by addressing three interdependent decisions: candidate-edge selection, traversal termination, and retrieved-path suf-ficiency. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the framework consists of five stages. Stage A encodes the input query and provides its representations to the retrieval process. Stage B per-forms adaptive graph retrieval through query-conditioned edge scoring and multi-hop traversal. Stage C evaluates the structural sufficiency of the retrieved path, and Stage D determines whether the path can be accepted or whether another retrieval attempt is required. Once a path is accepted, Stage E uses the retrieved evidence to generate the clinical answer. Thus, Stages A and E define the input and output interfaces of GRASP, while Stages B, C, and D form the retrieval, verification, and correction process. The framework contains two closely connected learning components within this five-stage process. The retriever in Stage B combines query-adaptive edge scoring with a learned stopping mechanism to construct a query-specific path. The verifier in Stage C evaluates the resulting path using its structural representation and retrieval confidence statistics. Stage D connects these two components at in-ference time: it accepts a path that meets the sufficiency criterion or initiates another retrieval attempt when the path is predicted to be insufficient. The accepted path is then passed to Stage E for answer generation. The two learning components are trained separately in two stages and coupled during inference through the verification and re-retrieval process. 

4.1. Query-Adaptive Edge Scoring 

The first component of Stage B determines which outgo-ing edge should be selected at each traversal step. In graph-based retrieval, the relevance of a candidate edge depends not only on its semantic similarity to the query as a whole, but also on how its relation and destination entity correspond to specific query tokens. A single global similarity score may overlook these fine-grained interactions. GRASP therefore combines global semantic matching with token-level cross-attention to obtain a more query-specific assessment of each candidate action.

4.2. Adaptive Graph Traversal and Termination 

Selecting relevant individual edges does not by itself determine whether the resulting path is appropriate for a given question. Questions differ in the amount of graph evidence required to reach a useful answer: some can be addressed through a short path, whereas others require mul-tiple relational steps. A fixed traversal depth may there-fore introduce unnecessary edges for simpler questions or terminate before sufficient evidence has been collected for more complex ones. GRASP addresses this issue through a learned stopping gate that adapts traversal termination to the current retrieval state. After an action is selected, its representation is incorpo-rated into the recurrent traversal state. The state is subse-quently refined by attending over the action history, allowing the retriever to retain information about previously selected edges rather than making each decision independently. This history-aware state provides a representation of the current traversal context and serves as an input to the stopping gate. In this way, termination decisions can reflect both the query and the evidence accumulated along the current path. Let ̂ 𝐬 𝑡 denote the refined traversal state after selecting the 

𝑡 -th action. The stopping probability is computed as 

𝑝 (𝑡 )stop = 𝜎 (𝑔 𝜃 (𝐜 𝑞 ,̂ 𝐬 𝑡 , 𝑡 )) , (12) where 𝑔 𝜃 is a learned scoring function, 𝜎 is the sigmoid function, and 𝑡 represents the current hop depth. The stop-ping gate jointly considers the global query representation, the refined traversal state, and the number of actions already selected. Traversal terminates when 𝑝 (𝑡 )stop > 𝜏 stop or when the maximum hop count 𝑇 max is reached. The combination of query information and traversal context is important because the stopping decision should not depend on retrieval depth alone. A path may reach a meaningful state after relatively few hops, while another query may require further exploration. The learned gate provides a query- and state-dependent termination decision, while 𝑇 max imposes an upper bound on the traversal length. If no action remains available at the current entity, the attempt ends as a dead end and its incomplete prefix is not passed to the sufficiency verifier. 4.3. Path-Sufficiency Verification 

Although adaptive edge selection and traversal termina-tion help construct query-relevant paths, high edge scores do not necessarily indicate that the assembled path contains all the evidence required to support an answer. A path may

include relevant entities while omitting an important relation or an intermediate connection. GRASP therefore introduces a path-sufficiency verifier in Stage C, as illustrated in Fig. 1. Instead of treating retriever confidence as a direct measure of evidence sufficiency, the verifier evaluates the assembled path using both its sequential representation and statistics derived from the retrieval process. For a retrieved path 𝑃 = ( 𝑎 1, … , 𝑎 𝑇 ), a bidirectional GRU encodes the ordered action sequence into a path rep-resentation 𝐡 𝑃 . The sequential encoder preserves the order of the selected actions, providing a representation of the re-trieved path rather than an unordered collection of candidate edges. In addition to this path representation, the verifier uses six retrieval confidence statistics: mean edge score 𝑠 , mean hop margin 𝛿 , mean candidate entropy 𝐻 , maximum edge score 𝑠 max , minimum hop margin 𝛿 min , and path length 𝑇 .Each hop margin 𝛿 𝑡 is defined as the score difference between the selected action and its strongest competing candidate at the corresponding traversal step. These statistics capture different aspects of the retrieval process. The mean edge score summarizes the overall scoring level, whereas the maximum edge score records the strongest local retrieval signal. The mean and minimum hop margins characterize the separation between selected actions and their alternatives, providing information about average and weakest-step selec-tion confidence. Mean candidate entropy reflects uncertainty in candidate selection, while path length provides informa-tion about the extent of the retrieved traversal. The path representation and retrieval statistics are con-catenated to form the verifier input:

The output of the sufficiency classifier determines the subsequent decision and routing process in Stage D of Fig. 1. A retrieved path is accepted when 𝑝 ins < 𝜏 cls , in which case it is passed to the LLM for answer generation. If 𝑝 ins ≥

The re-retrieval procedure is designed as a confidence-guided heuristic for exploring alternative paths. 4.5. Two-Stage Training 

GRASP adopts a two-stage training strategy to learn retrieval and path verification separately. In Stage B, the candidate-edge scorer and stopping gate are jointly opti-mized using the retrieval-related objectives described in Section 4.2. After the retriever converges, its retrieval out-puts and confidence statistics are used to construct path-level features for Stage C. The sufficiency classifier is then trained using these features and the path-quality proxy labels described in the experimental protocol. The two stages do not share a joint end-to-end training objective. Instead, Stage C learns to assess paths produced by the trained Stage B retriever. This separation allows the verifier to use retrieval-specific confidence information while maintaining a distinct training objective for path-level sufficiency assessment. During inference, the two trained components are coupled through the verification-driven re-retrieval process. ## 5. Experiments and Results 

This section tests whether the three design choices of GRASP improve retrieval and generation: token-level can-didate scoring with adaptive stopping (Stage B), sufficiency verification (Stage C), and local re-retrieval (Stage D). We first compare GRASP with the baselines, then isolate the contribution of each component through a controlled com-parison and ablation studies, and finally examine the reliabil-ity of the verification stages and the sensitivity of the loop. 

5.1. Experimental Protocol 

We evaluate GRASP on three biomedical question-answering benchmarks with different graph structures and annotation properties. The protocol covers dataset prepara-tion, model training, structural sufficiency supervision, and generation evaluation. 

Datasets and data splits. We use RiTeK, MedKGent-KG, and a PMC-based biomedical corpus [36, 37, 38]. Their main characteristics and data splits are summarized in Table 2.

RiTeK provides annotated reasoning paths for evaluating terminal entity retrieval and intermediate evidence recov-ery. MedKGent-KG provides a larger and denser biomedi-cal graph, together with 1,060 expert-scored triple records. These annotations are used as query-associated reference evidence rather than being treated as exhaustive graph-level or ordered path annotations. The ratio of triples to entities is approximately 19.04, indicating the larger candidate space considered in this benchmark. PMC does not provide a fixed knowledge graph or an-notated reasoning paths. We therefore construct a query-specific graph for each question by retrieving relevant full-text articles, extracting biomedical entities and relations from their sentences, and assembling the extracted relations into a directed graph. Entity normalization is applied before graph assembly, and extracted edges retain their source-sentence information for evidence inspection. Reference an-swers are excluded from test-time graph construction. Since PMC lacks annotated reasoning paths, Path Recall is not reported for this dataset. RiTeK and PMC each contribute 5,000 instances for training and development, whereas MedKGent-KG con-tributes its 1,060 available expert-annotated records. The training and development portions follow a 70:30 split, with an additional 100 held-out test instances per benchmark. Splitting is performed before generating path perturbations. For PMC, questions associated with the same source article are kept within the same partition. The held-out test sets are excluded from training, checkpoint selection, and threshold optimization. For MedKGent-KG, target annotations are separated before training so that the target evidence of a held-out query is not exposed to Stage B. 

Baselines and implementation. We compare GRASP with five baselines: BFS-2, BFS-3, G-Retriever, GNN-RAG, and MINERVA [16, 39, 9]. BFS-2 and BFS-3 use fixed traversal depths of two and three hops, respectively. G-Retriever and GNN-RAG represent graph-based retrieval for language model reasoning, while MINERVA provides a learned multi-hop traversal baseline. All methods use the same benchmark queries, graph resources, and answer-generation model. A separate controlled comparison uses MINERVA equipped with PubMedBERT to distinguish the effect of the encoder backbone from that of the GRASP architecture. GRASP uses PubMedBERT as its shared textual encoder [25]. Stage B jointly trains the candidate-edge scorer and stopping gate, while Stage C independently trains the suffi-ciency classifier. Both stages use AdamW optimization [40]. The pretrained encoder uses a lower learning rate than the newly initialized layers, with linear warm-up followed by cosine decay. The principal inference settings are 𝐼 max = 3 

total retrieval attempts, including the initial attempt, and 

𝜏 cls = 0 .50 . The stopping threshold and maximum hop count are selected using development data. All experiments use an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3080 GPU with 16 GB system memory. Gemini-2.5 Flash is used as the common answer generator without task-specific fine-tuning. The same prompt structure and decoding configura-tion are applied across the compared retrieval methods. 

Sufficiency supervision and generation evaluation. Stage C is trained using structural proxy labels rather than generation-level Unsupported Claim Rate, which is available only after answer generation. Complete reference evidence is labelled sufficient ( 𝑦 = 0 ), while evidence that fails to cover the required structural information is labelled insufficient (𝑦 = 1 ). RiTeK provides ordered reference paths, whereas MedKGent-KG provides query-associated annotated evi-dence triples. The latter are used to assess evidence coverage and are not treated as complete ordered gold paths. PMC is excluded from supervised gold-path construction because it has no corresponding path annotations. Insufficient examples are generated through six pertur-bations: 75% truncation, 50% truncation, node substitution, relation swap, over-extension, and reverse-path corruption. Truncation removes required evidence from the path, while node and relation substitutions alter intermediate retrieval decisions. Over-extension introduces an irrelevant or con-flicting continuation, and reverse-path corruption changes the direction or order of required relations. Additional hard negatives are obtained from paths generated by Stage B. Perturbed examples derived from the same source query or path remain within the same data partition to prevent source-level leakage. These structural labels serve as proxies for retrieval sufficiency and are not treated as guarantees of factual correctness. Generated answers are decomposed into atomic claims and evaluated against the retrieved path using natural lan-guage inference. The microsoft/deberta-xlarge-mnli check-point is used for the NLI assessment and BERTScore [41]. Each claim is classified as entailed, neutral, or contradicted by the retrieved evidence. Only entailed claims are consid-ered supported. Neutral and contradicted claims contribute   

> Rui Xu et al.: Preprint Page 9 of 20 GRASP

to the Unsupported Claim Rate. Accordingly, the Path-Supported Atomic Fact Rate (PSAFR) is the proportion of generated atomic claims entailed by the retrieved path, while the Path-Neutral Rate (PNR) and Path-Contradiction Rate (PCR) denote the proportions of neutral and contradicted claims, respectively. We therefore obtain 

UCR = PNR + PCR = 1 − PSAFR . (18) A neutral claim is not necessarily factually incorrect; it indicates that the retrieved path does not provide sufficient support for that claim. 

Metrics and statistical analysis. Retrieval performance is measured using Hits@1, Hits@3, MRR, and Path Recall. Path Recall measures the proportion of query-associated reference triples covered by the retrieved path. It is a set-based coverage measure rather than an exact ordered-path match. For PMC, the paragraph-level answer entity recall defined in Eq. (6) is used as a separate retrieval proxy and is not directly compared with terminal-entity Hits@1 on the other benchmarks. Generation quality is evaluated using Token F1 and BERTScore, while evidence groundedness is measured by UCR. Higher values are preferable for retrieval and gener-ation quality metrics, whereas lower UCR indicates better evidence support. Absolute differences between percentage-valued metrics are reported in percentage points (pp). Each trainable configuration is evaluated using five inde-pendent random seeds, and retrieval results are reported as mean ± standard deviation. For the BFS baselines, the seeds control tie-breaking among equally ranked candidate edges rather than model initialization. Generation results are first aggregated over repeated decoder runs within each question and then summarized across questions. Paired bootstrap tests use the same held-out questions for both systems, with 10,000 question-level resamples. This separates training and decoding variability from uncertainty associated with the test queries. 

5.2. Main Results 

Figure 2 compares GRASP with the strongest baseline on every dataset. GRASP leads on all reported metrics, and the advantage covers accuracy, retrieval quality, complete-ness, and groundedness rather than terminal accuracy alone. Table 3 reports the retrieval results. GRASP improves Hits@1 over MINERVA by 10.0% on RiTeK and 10.4% on MedKGent-KG, and Path Recall by 13.9% and 13.0%. The Path Recall gain exceeds the Hits@1 gain on both datasets, which indicates that token-level scoring with adaptive stop-ping recovers the intermediate relations of the annotated chain and not only the final entity. The gains are similar on the two datasets, and we did not test whether they differ. On MedKGent-KG, held-out triples are separated before path construction and Stage B never sees the target triple during training. A blind-tail evaluation (Section 5.3.1) fur-ther tests whether the query template leaks the answer. 

5.3. Generation Results and Blind-Tail Evaluation 

Table 4 reports generation quality. Unsupported Claim Rate is the fraction of generated claims that are not entailed by the retrieved path. It counts claims that are neutral to the path as well as claims that contradict it, so it is not a factual-error rate. Relative to MINERVA, GRASP lowers the Unsupported Claim Rate by 10.5 percentage points on RiTeK, 8.9 on MedKGent-KG, and 13.2 on PMC. The largest reduction oc-curs on PMC, where structural supervision is weakest. This agrees with the role of Stage C: checking the retrieved path matters most when generated answers contain more claims that can exceed the retrieved evidence. Paired bootstrap tests against MINERVA (Table 5) give 𝑝 < 0.001 for every comparison. They establish the complete-system advantage, while component-level effects are examined in the ablation studies. 

5.3.1. Blind-Tail Evaluation 

The MedKGent-KG query template contains the target entity, which could serve as a shortcut for terminal entity prediction. In the blind-tail evaluation, the tail entity is removed from the query while the semantic relation being asked is preserved. Masking the tail entity lowers Hits@1 for both models, which confirms that the original query offers a partial short-cut. The drop is smaller for GRASP (17.1% versus 19.1%). Path Recall does not depend on the tail entity appearing in the query, and it falls by only 2.1% for GRASP and 3.4% for MINERVA-PubMed-BERT, with GRASP keeping its lead. The retrieval advantage of GRASP is therefore not explained by copying the target entity from the input. The experiment does not rule out every effect of query construction. 

5.4. Controlled Comparison: Backbone versus Architecture 

The main comparison cannot separate the retrieval ar-chitecture of GRASP from the switch to a PubMedBERT backbone. Table 7 therefore compares the original MIN-ERVA, MINERVA-PubMed-BERT, three GRASP variants that each lack one Stage B component, and the full model, with the training data and retrieval task held fixed. Replacing the original encoder with PubMedBERT raises Hits@1 by 2.3% and Path Recall by 2.4%, which leaves most of the gap to GRASP unexplained by the backbone. Figure 3 decomposes the 10.4% total Hits@1 gain over the original MINERVA. The backbone accounts for 2.3% (22%), and the GRASP architecture, comprising cross-attention scoring, the stop gate, history modulation, and the sufficiency loop, accounts for the remaining 8.1% (78%). This split holds for this dataset and backbone pair and need not transfer to other settings. G-Retriever and GNN-RAG differ in encoders, training objectives, and graph representations. Their results in Ta-ble 3 are therefore end-to-end benchmark comparisons and not causal component comparisons.

5.5.2. Removing Stage C and D Components: Sufficiency Classifier and Re-retrieval Loop 

Removing the sufficiency classifier lowers Hits@1 by only 1.7% (76.8% to 75.1%) but raises the Unsupported Claim Rate by 6.2% (15.1% to 21.3%), the largest increase among all components. Its Hits@1 effect is not treated as a reliable retrieval effect, and the paired test is reported in Appendix Appendix B. Removing only the re-retrieval loop, with the classifier kept, lowers Hits@1 by 1.2% and raises the Unsupported Claim Rate by 3.5% (15.1% to 18.6%), so the loop adds a benefit beyond classification. As Fig-ure 4 shows, the two verification components act mainly on whether generation stays grounded in the retrieved path, while cross-attention and the stop gate shift retrieval and generation to a similar degree. 

5.6. Reliability of Sufficiency Verification 

The sufficiency classifier cannot be trained on Unsup-ported Claim Rate, which is observable only after genera-tion. It is instead trained on structural sufficiency, defined by the correspondence between the retrieved linear path and the annotated reasoning chain. The corruption pool con-tains six operations: 75% truncation, 50% truncation, node substitution, relation swap, over-extension, and reverse-path corruption. The classifier reaches an F1 of 0.87 on the synthetic pool but 0.78 on hard negatives generated by Stage B. Hard negatives contain errors from the learned traversal policy and not from predefined corruption rules, so the synthetic score overstates deployment performance. The classifier also relies on Stage B confidence statistics such as action margins and entropy, which are informative when the scorer is correct but can be over-confident on wrong actions, so it is not an independent oracle. Each corruption type has few develop-ment examples, and the per-type values carry no confidence intervals. The structural proxy is useful only if it tracks the support of generated claims. We correlate the proxy score with per-question Path-Supported Atomic Fact Rate (PSAFR), which measures the proportion of generated atomic facts supported by the retrieved evidence path. PSAFR is computed from the same held-out generations, claim decomposition, and NLI decisions as Table 4, so that 

Unsupported = 1 − PSAFR = PNR + PCR ,

where PNR denotes Path-Neutral Rate, measuring the pro-portion of claims neither supported nor contradicted by the retrieved path, and PCR denotes Path-Contradiction Rate, measuring the proportion of claims contradicted by the re-trieved path. The correlation is positive on all three datasets (Table 10). The lower value on PMC agrees with its automat-ically extracted graph and the absence of path annotations. The proxy is therefore a structural predictor of generation support and not a substitute for generation-level evaluation. The analysis covers the full validation distribution and may not hold on failure subsets such as hard negatives, whose upstream errors the corruption pool may not represent. 

5.7. Re-retrieval Loop: Which Failures Can Be Recovered 

The loop preserves the traversal prefix before the se-lected hop and replaces the action at the lowest-margin location, so it performs local correction and not global backtracking. On datasets with gold paths, failed paths are assigned post hoc to three categories, using the observed traversal prefix and the gold path. • Edge-selection error : the traversal follows the gold prefix up to the selected hop but chooses a different action, which tends to be locally reroutable. • Premature stopping : the traversed prefix is correct but the stop decision comes before the gold path is complete, so replacing one action cannot force another hop. • Wrong region entry : the traversal leaves the gold path before the weakest margin hop, so replacing a later action cannot restore the lost prefix. The overall recovery rate of 38.5% is far below the 64.8% for edge-selection errors. 5.8. Re-retrieval Loop: Sensitivity to 𝐼 max and 𝜏 cls 

We vary the maximum number of re-retrieval attempts 

𝐼 max ∈ {1 , 2, 3, 4} and the classifier threshold 𝜏 cls ∈{0 .40 , 0.45 , 0.50 , 0.55} .At the default threshold, raising 𝐼 max from one to three lowers the Unsupported Claim Rate from 17.3% to 15.1% and raises Hits@1 from 75.3% to 76.8%. 5.9. Candidate Density: Where Token-Level Scoring Helps 

Token-level cross-attention is designed for neighbour-hoods in which many plausible edges compete. To sepa-rate this factor from cross-dataset differences, queries in MedKGent-KG are grouped by the out-degree of their starting entity, and GRASP is compared with MINERVA-PubMed-BERTwithin each bin. As out-degree grows, the Hits@1 of MINERVA-PubMed -BERTfalls from 70.8% to 61.8%, while that of GRASP rises slightly from 75.1% to 77.4% (Figure 6). The gap widens from 4.3% in the sparsest bin to 15.6% in the densest, in line with the motivation for token-level cross-attention: when more locally plausible edges compete for the same node, a pooled query representation is less discriminative. 5.10. Efficiency 

Stage C and the re-retrieval loop add computation. Ta-ble 14 reports the average inference latency and the number of re-retrieval attempts. Most of the added latency comes from the cross-attention scorer and the sufficiency evaluation. The loop adds 85 ms per query on average and lowers the Unsupported Claim Rate by 3.5% (Table 8), and it is invoked only when a path is rejected. 

5.11. Summary of the Experimental Evidence 

The experiments support three conclusions, each tied to a design choice of GRASP. First, token-level scoring with adaptive stopping improves path retrieval. GRASP raises Path Recall by 13.9% and 13.0% over MINERVA, the controlled comparison attributes 78% of the Hits@1 gain to the architecture and not to the backbone, and the gap to MINERVA-PubMed-BERTwidens from 4.3% to 15.6% as candidate density grows. Second, adaptive stopping and sufficiency verification play different roles. The stop gate sets how long the traversal continues, whereas the sufficiency classifier judges the com-pleted path. Removing the stop gate mainly lowers retrieval accuracy, and removing the classifier raises the Unsupported Claim Rate by 6.2%, so the two components are not inter-changeable. Third, the re-retrieval loop reduces unsupported claims but repairs only local failures. Recovery is 64.8% for edge-selection errors and 38.5% overall, so the loop is targeted local correction and not general backtracking. The structural proxy correlates with generation-level PSAFR ( 𝑟 = 0.73 

to 0.83 ), which supports its use as a training signal, but its weaker performance on hard negatives and on PMC shows that it does not replace generation-level verification. 

## 6. Conclusion 

This work introduced GRASP, a graph-based retrieval framework that combines query-adaptive edge scoring, learned traversal termination, and path-sufficiency verification. Ex-periments across RiTeK, MedKGent-KG, and PMC show improvements in retrieval quality and reductions in Unsup-ported Claim Rate over the evaluated learned baselines. The controlled MINERVA comparison supports the contribution of the proposed retrieval mechanisms beyond the choice of encoder, while the component analysis indicates that edge scoring, stopping, and sufficiency verification contribute in complementary ways. The blind-tail experiment further shows that the retrieval advantage persists when the target entity is removed from the query. These findings support the value of integrating adaptive traversal with path-level verification, although they do not establish that the approach

6.1. Limitations 

GRASP has three main limitations. First, the structural proxy used to train the sufficiency classifier only approxi-mates generation-level evidence support, and its reliance on upstream retrieval confidence makes it vulnerable to confi-dently incorrect actions. Second, the re-retrieval mechanism modifies a low margin action locally and may fail to recover from premature stopping or entry into an incorrect graph region. Third, PMC lacks gold path annotations and uses an automatically constructed graph that may contain missing or spurious relations; moreover, automatic NLI evaluation only approximates human judgement. Consequently, the classi-fier’s reliability, the recovery capability of re-retrieval, and the interpretation of Unsupported Claim Rate remain lim-ited. 

6.2. Future Work 

Future work will address these limitations by evaluating the structural sufficiency proxy on larger, naturally occur-ring sets of retrieval failures and calibrating the Stage B confidence features under distribution shift. The re-retrieval mechanism could be extended with explicit backtracking, path extension, and abstention actions to better handle pre-mature stopping and incorrect-region entry. Further work could also establish more controlled comparisons with G-Retriever and GNN-RAG under a shared encoder and train-ing protocol, and validate automatic NLI based support assessments through human annotation on a larger, stratified biomedical sample.

## Appendix D. Proxy and NLI Verification 

The human validation yields an overall Cohen’s 𝜅 of 0.78. These results are used only to assess the automatic verifier, and are not used to redefine the main Unsupported Claim Rate. They provide the supporting evidence for treating Unsupported Claim Rate as an approximation to, rather than an exact measurement of, clinical hallucination, a caveat raised in the paper’s limitations discussion. The lower per class scores on PMC are consistent with its longer, less structured reference answers, which make claim decomposition and NLI judgment comparatively harder.
