Abstract
AI agents are commonly used for everyday information retrieval and coding. We ask
whether they can also interpret human requests and turn them into complete videos.
We introduce VAHBench, a benchmark that tests this ability through five short-drama
briefs specifying story, characters, dialogue and duration. Agents plan and carry out
production using shared generation tools. Evaluation combines video completeness and
basic quality with checks of production reliability and reporting accuracy. Across 240 runs
covering 48 configurations of general-purpose and video-specific agents, higher quality
scores do not necessarily mean more complete delivery. Missing episodes, repeated
footage and static filler reveal gaps between producing a video file and fulfilling the brief.
Added composition tools help some agents but do not consistently improve performance.
VAHBench measures how well agents translate user intent into finished video content
and helps identify where that process breaks down.

1. Introduction
Video generation systems increasingly target multi-scene narratives rather than isolated clips.
Recent agents refine prompts through feedback, maintain memory across segments or learn
to coordinate generation tools [14, 18, 19]. A finished series, however, also requires scripts,
recurring characters, dialogue, captions and episodes of the requested length, requirements that
extend beyond the quality of any single clip. General-purpose harnesses such as Claude Code
[1], Codex [23] and OpenClaw [24] can call the same generators as purpose-built video agents
[9, 11, 15]. We evaluate whether these systems can deliver a complete series from a brief.
VAHBench measures this native production capability in terms of completion and basic
quality. Completion requires every episode at the requested length, without looped, frozen
or blank filler. Basic quality requires consistent characters, plausible visual contact, audible
dialogue and the specified format. We focus on completion and basic defects; aesthetic quality
lies outside this evaluation, although orchestration can affect it even with fixed generators.
General-purpose harnesses receive a brief, generation endpoints and a standard media tool, but
the benchmark supplies no composition helpers, workflow or scoring rubric.

Existing benchmarks cover related settings (Table 1). VBench and later suites evaluate gener-
ated output [12, 17, 37]. FilmBench assesses cinematic craft, while Statebench tests world-state
reasoning across video segments [21, 30]. Agentic benchmarks evaluate production workflows
or supply a pipeline or editing skills [5, 27, 31]. We instead focus on the agent’s ability to
construct the delivery under the stated tool constraints, separate log parsing from numerical
scoring, and verify reported delivery against the saved files. Table 1 summarises the task scope
and evaluation protocol of each benchmark. DirectorBench compares four workflows and six
base models separately, and DramaChain counts models evaluated by both humans and its
automated judge; VAHBench evaluates 48 configurations on every brief with eight scored leaves.
Our evaluator locates episode files, credits genuinely delivered seconds and checks visual
and audio defects against the brief and the decoded media, treating the agent’s log as a claim to
be verified. We compare general-purpose harnesses, purpose-built video agents and harnesses
equipped with the OpenMontage tool library [2], all with shared generators. Across 240 runs,
the results separate completeness from measured quality and show that the benefit of the tool
library depends on the harness.

2. Related work
Evaluating generated video.
VBench [12] and VBench-2.0 [37] assess generated video along
multiple quality dimensions. Recent benchmarks extend evaluation to multi-shot, minute-
scale and audio-visual generation [3, 10, 17, 32]. FilmBench adds criteria drawn from cinematic
practice [30]. Statebench evaluates whether a continuation reflects both prior actions and the next
instruction [21]. PhyWorldBench measures physical realism, and BrokenVideos localises artifacts
[8, 16]. These studies inform our defect checks, but our evaluation unit is the series assembled
by an agent, including missing episodes and footage used to fill runtime requirements.
Video production agents.
Multi-agent systems organise scripts and generate shots [29, 33, 35],
while UniVA and ViMax integrate production pipelines [11, 15]. VISTA iteratively refines
generation prompts using specialised feedback [19]. A2RD maintains multimodal memory
and refines successive segments [18]. VideoGen-Agent learns tool use through reinforcement
learning [14]. These approaches improve generation within their own production systems. We
instead compare native execution across purpose-built agents and general-purpose harnesses
[22, 28] with fixed generators.
Agentic video benchmarks and evaluation.
DirectorBench evaluates long-video workflows
with a multi-agent judge [5]. DramaChain Bench evaluates stages of a fixed short-drama pipeline
[27], and VideoWeaver supplies editing skills and studies their evolution [31]. AgenticVBench
targets the post-production of supplied footage [4]. VideoArgus uses sample-specific rubrics
and visual tools, while HarnessEval-W grounds visual-world evaluation in collected evidence
[6, 36]. Evidence-grounded evaluation is therefore not unique to our work; our distinction
is fixed numerical scoring of completion and basic defects under a protocol that supplies no
composition helpers.
Evaluating general-purpose agents.
SWE-bench tests repository changes, and Terminal-Bench
evaluates realistic command-line tasks [13, 20]. Both motivate checking completed work against
executable criteria. LLM- and agent-as-a-judge methods offer broader rubric-based assessment
[38, 39], but their reported agreement with human judgements varies across generation tasks
[25]. We therefore specify the measurements and validation scope of each check.

3. VAHBench
3.1. Protocol and tree structure
The agent receives a short-drama brief, shared generation endpoints and a standard command-
line media tool. General-purpose harnesses construct their own editing operations, with no
supplied composition helpers, output schema or access to the scoring rubric. Three of these
harnesses receive a minimal skill that only documents endpoint calls, whereas purpose-built
agents and the OpenMontage track retain their own composition routines. Each configuration
runs once per brief, without human creative guidance.
Figure 1 organises evaluation around two evidence branches. The video branch measures
completeness 𝐶and basic quality 𝑄from the delivery, while the trajectory branch measures
process 𝑃from task outcomes and verifiable claims. This separation allows a complete file to fail
a quality check, and a successful delivery to coexist with an inaccurate self-report. The evaluator
locates candidate episodes without assuming a directory layout. LLM assistance converts
heterogeneous logs into structured statements with source excerpts, preserving what the agent
declared; it assigns neither quality scores nor success or compliance verdicts. Subsequent checks
verify these declarations against saved artifacts and measurements, and fixed rules normalise
and aggregate the results. We therefore distinguish LLM-assisted evidence extraction from
LLM-based scoring: “No LLM judge” in Table 1 refers to score assignment, not to an entirely
LLM-free evaluation pipeline. Figure 2 illustrates the full workflow and the distinctions drawn
by individual checks.

Basic quality.
The quality subtree has three dimensions. Specification checks measurable
format requirements and decidable clauses in the brief, including required deliverables and
timing. Consistency checks face identity across shots [7] and continuity at cuts. Plausibility
checks physical contact, audio defects and subtitle alignment. Depth estimates support contact
checks [34], and transcription supports subtitle alignment [26]. Figure 2 places these checks
within the evaluation workflow. Reference-image similarity, requested generation parameters
and speaker-face proxies remain diagnostic, because they depend on optional production choices
or do not directly establish a delivered defect.

assets, are recorded separately and receive no score. The tree thus evaluates the outcome of a

5. Conclusion
VAHBench evaluates whether an agent delivers the requested series by separating genuine
completeness, basic quality and verifiable process evidence. Our experiments show why these
dimensions must remain visible: high measured quality can accompany incomplete delivery,
and composition tools can improve completeness while lowering quality or the total score, with a
benefit that depends on the harness rather than on tool access alone. Production examples show
how agents address the same delivery request through generated shots, looping or still-image
assembly. These differences motivate checking genuine footage and reporting individual task
outcomes alongside averages, rather than relying on container duration or a single favourable
task.
By making these distinctions explicit and auditable, the benchmark provides a basis for
diagnosing incomplete delivery and comparing production strategies. Broader task coverage,
repeated trials and human-annotated defects will extend the evaluation of robustness and
perceptual validity.
Ethics statement
The briefs are original fictional texts written for this benchmark, and no third-party creative
work is reproduced. Generated media depicts fictional characters, and reference portraits
are themselves model-generated. We do not collect, store or match against images of real
people, although the identity checks use standard face-recognition components that should
not be repurposed onto real-person footage without separate review. Systems under test are
open-source repositories or publicly available tools used under their own licences.
Reproducibility statement
We document the benchmark tasks, execution protocol and scoring procedure to support
replication. The five briefs are reproduced in Appendix G. Section 4.1 specifies the tested systems,
model configurations and shared generators; Appendix E details runtime adaptations and
completion criteria. Section 3 and Appendices A–B define the scoring rules, missing-observation
handling and aggregation. The run-level measurements allow the reported summary statistics
to be recomputed. Independent reruns may produce different videos and scores because agent
execution and media generation are stochastic.
AI use statement
The systems under test are AI agents, and generated video is model output. Inside the evaluator,
language-model assistance parses logs but does not assign numerical scores. AI assistance was
used for implementation, literature discovery, language editing, figure formatting and portions
of this manuscript. The authors verified the test results and remain responsible for the analysis
and manuscript.

A. Scoring definitions and analysis protocol
The scoring protocol in §3 has eight scored leaves: format compliance and brief compliance; a
consistency leaf combining face identity and cut-continuity evidence; physical contact, audio
defects and subtitle alignment; and reliability and honesty. Optional planning artifacts, reference
anchoring and requested generation parameters remain diagnostics. An observed invalid result
contributes zero at its leaf; an unavailable measurement leaves the denominator. Root-level
factors use the stated numerical floor.
A.1. From production evidence to separate measurement roles
The evaluator follows the production chain rather than treating all checks as interchangeable
penalties. It first associates requested episodes with candidate deliveries, then decodes the
accepted media, constructs a duration account, measures quality from eligible observations and
checks task outcomes and final claims in the execution record. Numerical aggregation follows
these measurements; log parsing does not itself award quality scores. Table 3 connects each
scoring stage to its rationale and supporting evidence. The support has three levels: a definition
fixes what is measured, an explicit-input control checks how a rule responds, and the test results
reveal whether dimensions distinguish observed profiles. The empirical analysis uses the 240
recorded runs; analytical controls isolate the response of each scoring rule.

Defect ownership prevents duplicate penalties.
Episode count and duration shortfall belong
to 𝐶, not to a second specification-duration score. Pixel intervals already deducted from genuine

independently observed defects remain reportable. These ownership rules preserve distinct

single event.
Observation eligibility precedes normalisation.
Consistency requires usable identity or cut
evidence; physical checks require resolvable contact evidence; subtitle checks require correspon-
dence between text and audio. A missing correspondence is not a clean measurement. Likewise,
reliability requires an identifiable production-task denominator, and honesty requires an agent-
authored claim aligned to the same delivered artifact. Unresolved attribution abstains. Eligible
measurements are mapped to [0, 1], combined within leaves and then within dimensions. This
separates the question “could this check be answered?” from “what did the check find?”.
A.2. From observations to the eight scored leaves
Normalisation preserves the meaning of an observation before aggregation. An exact Boolean
predicate receives one if satisfied and zero if violated. For a measured deficit fraction 𝑟, direct
satisfaction is 𝑠(𝑟) = max{0, min(1, 1 −𝑟)}. This rule applies to cut defects, resolved contact
defects, decidable brief deficits, subtitle-alignment deficits and unrecovered-task fractions.
Applying a generic fail threshold to these fractions would collapse, for example, 20% and 80%
defective contacts to the same score. Table 4 shows their distinct outputs.
For band-based sources, the score is one below the clean cut 𝑙, zero above the suspect cut
𝑢, and (𝑢−𝑥)/(𝑢−𝑙) between them. The current noise-ratio cuts are (1.5, 3.0); the ordinary
defect-rate cuts are (0.05, 0.20). Identity ratios and graded audio defects therefore use a tolerance
band, whereas direct satisfaction fractions retain their native scale. This is a fixed conversion

versus claims about those outcomes. This section states the corresponding mathematical require-
ments and checks their implementation. The numerical controls are explicit equation inputs,
not additional benchmark videos. Empirical discrimination on our test results is examined
separately in the following section.

B.2. Tolerance, assembly and overlap have separate roles
The three-band map separates small timing deviations, moderate shortfalls and large deficits
without collapsing every off-specification delivery to zero. It joins continuously at 𝑏(𝜏) = 1 and
𝑏(0.2) = 0.5, and reaches 𝑏(1) = 0. Thus an honest 40-second delivery retains more credit than a
ten-second loop even though both fall outside the tolerance band. The default 𝜏= max(0.05, 2/𝑇),
bounded below 0.2, combines relative tolerance with an absolute timing allowance. These cut
points are transparent design choices, not thresholds fitted to human preferences.
21


The cap 𝑔/𝑑prevents tolerance from granting full credit to known filler. For a 60-second
target, 59 genuine seconds delivered as 59 seconds receive 𝐶= 1; extending the same footage
to 60 seconds with one filler second receives 𝐶= 59/60 = 0.983. A correct container duration
therefore cannot erase an evidenced content defect. Assembly asks a different question: complete
loose material receives 0.9, whereas the same footage assembled into the requested episode
receives 1.0. The additive cost is limited to 0.1 and cannot make assembling a heavily padded
delivery equivalent to supplying genuine material. A measured empty delivery receives raw
zero and the common reported floor 0.05; the floor is not evidence of delivered content.
Defects are combined as a union of intervals before any duration score is computed. If held
footage occupies [10, 20] seconds and a repeat occupies [15, 25], their union removes 15 seconds,
not 20. This makes the credited duration invariant to duplicate reports of the same interval.
When only unaligned scalar defect counts are available, the implementation uses the largest
established lower bound rather than assuming the counts are disjoint. Verified slowdown is
applied to the remaining clean picture, so overlapping pixel defects are not charged twice. The
missing-episode denominator and evidence-gap flags remain necessary: a perfect surviving
episode does not establish completion of an entire requested series.

B.5. Missing evidence and executable boundary checks
Missing observations are not evidence of failure or success. Renormalising the original weights
over the observed set gives Equation 6 while preserving the relative weights of the surviving
facets. For example, 𝐶raw = 0.8, observed specification and plausibility both 0.8, and absent
consistency and process yield 𝑆= 64 with weights (1/2, 1/2) on the two observed facets. If no
facet is observed, 100 max(𝐶raw, 𝜖) = 80 is explicitly completeness-only. These different evidence
masks cannot be treated as equally complete evaluations. A score may rise or fall when evidence
is removed; reporting the mask and coverage is therefore essential.
The accompanying analysis checks the analytical cases against the local scoring implemen-
tation. A fixed grid of 624 valid (𝑔, 𝑑) combinations checks completeness calculations and
non-improvement under padding; 64 combinations of completeness, quality and process inputs
check the full root expression and its bound. Separate checks cover overlapping intervals,
absent facets and completeness-only output. Agreement is required to the implementation’s
four-decimal completeness and two-decimal total precision. These checks establish agreement
between the formulas and implementation on the specified inputs.

E. Running the systems under test
The protocol uses fixed system checkouts, shared media backends and environment or import-
time adapters. Purpose-built pipelines retain their composition logic; native harnesses receive
endpoint documentation without composition helpers. The OpenMontage track explicitly
supplies a tool library and is evaluated separately. These setup details describe the execution
protocol.
Runtime adaptations.
ViMax uses extended polling and request timeouts and disables the
portrait-orientation rejection so that vertical briefs can execute. Media backends are redirected to
the common provider, and default daily generation limits are removed. VideoClaw is exercised
through its backend API, bypassing a human-confirmation layer; this changes the interaction
protocol and limits conclusions about the interactive product. UniVA uses an import-time
bootstrap to propagate adapters to subprocesses. The tool-library adapter rewrites model
identifiers and supplies the token-counting endpoint needed for context compaction.
Completion criteria.
The batch driver can resume execution while background generation or
rendering remains unfinished. Resumption prompts state environment facts without creative
guidance. The driver checks that expected episode files exist and exceed 100 KB before declaring
completion; subsequent decoding and evaluation determine whether these files contain valid
delivered video. This driver check establishes file availability; the evaluator subsequently
measures genuine completeness.
F. Limitations
Experimental scope.
Evaluation covers five English drama briefs with one run per configu-
ration and brief. Duration varies with story, cast and format, so comparisons do not isolate its
causal effect. Results describe the tested systems and tasks; broader coverage and repeated runs
are needed to assess generalisation and variability.
Measurement validity.
Scores target completion and basic defects rather than overall aesthetic
or narrative quality. Detector accuracy and threshold validity require independent human
annotation. LLM-assisted log parsing can affect the evidence supplied to fixed scoring rules.
Component analyses cover completeness and quality; unavailable process scores and incomplete
facet coverage limit finer-grained interpretation.
