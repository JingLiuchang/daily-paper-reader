Abstract 

AI agents are increasingly deployed in socially consequen-tial settings, yet existing evaluations provide limited assur-ance that they behave consistently with their intended char-acteristics across diverse interactions. We introduce an eval-uation framework for measuring the behavioral consistency of socially deployed AI agents. The framework operational-izes agent behavior along five measurable axes and uses a standardized evaluation battery spanning six socially salient interaction settings to assess behavioral differentiation, con-sistency, and drift. Controlled persona configurations serve as experimental anchors, enabling systematic testing of whether intended behavioral differences persist across contexts rather than appearing only as stylistic variation. Preliminary analyses demonstrate measurable differentiation across configurations and suggest associations between behavioral profiles and in-teraction outcomes. By shifting the focus from persona design to behavioral assurance, this work provides a foundation for evaluating whether socially deployed AI agents behave as in-tended, consistently and measurably, across the situations in which users encounter them. 

# Introduction 

AI agents are increasingly deployed in socially consequential settings (Zhao et al. 2026; Li et al. 2023; Flathers et al. 2025) where their behavior can shape user trust, engagement, psy-chological safety, and decision making (Möhle et al. 2026; Axelsson et al. 2025). Yet existing evaluations predominantly assess capabilities, task performance, or isolated safety fail-ures, providing limited assurance that an agent will exhibit its intended behavioral characteristics consistently across heterogeneous social interactions. Properties such as how an agent navigates uncertainty, balances safety against effi-ciency (Amodei et al. 2016), or responds to vulnerable users may be difficult to capture through conventional benchmark-based evaluation. We introduce an evaluation and assurance framework for measuring the behavioral consistency of socially deployed AI agents . Behavioral configurations are fur-ther evaluated against deliberately heterogeneous character clusters, providing interpretable reference points for testing whether the measurement framework can recover known dif-ferences in behavioral disposition. The framework provides a foundation for continuous behavioral evaluation of deployed agents and for investigating how behavioral consistency relates to en-gagement, psychological safety, and trustworthy human–AI interaction (Sun and Wang 2026; Mehrotra et al. 2024). 

# Related Work 

## Personality and behavioral characterization of language models 

As language models increasingly mediate social interactions, growing attention has been directed toward characterizing the behavioral dispositions they exhibit (Li et al. 2026; Chang and Bergen 2024). Recent work has adapted psychometric methods to language models, demonstrating that synthetic personality traits can, under appropriate conditions, be mea-sured reliably, shaped through prompting, and associated arXiv:2611.19564v1 [cs.AI] 14 Nov 2026 with downstream behavior (Serapio-García et al. 2025; Ma-harjan et al. 2025). This establishes personality as a po-tentially measurable property of model behavior rather than merely a stylistic feature. However, psychometric evaluations primarily characterize models through established human personality constructs and questionnaires, rather than evalu-ating whether a deployed agent remains consistent with an explicitly specified behavioral profile across heterogeneous interaction contexts. 

## Persona evaluation and behavioral consistency 

A related literature examines whether persona-conditioned agents faithfully exhibit intended characteristics. Person-aGym, for example, evaluates persona adherence across di-verse environments and introduces PersonaScore as an auto-mated measure of how consistently agents behave according to assigned personas (Samuel et al. 2025). Complementary mechanistic work suggests that such behavioral character-istics may also be represented internally. Persona Vectors (Chen et al. 2025; Feng et al. 2026) identify activation-space directions associated with behavioral traits and demonstrate that these representations can be used to monitor and con-trol shifts during training and deployment. More recently, Lu et al. (2026) further identify an “Assistant Axis” capturing models’ propensity to remain within their default assistant persona, showing that deviations along this axis predict per-sona drift and can coincide with undesirable behavior. These studies demonstrate that agent characteristics can be speci-fied, measured, and subject to drift over time. 

## From persona evaluation to behavioral assurance 

Our work builds on these developments but adopts a broader assurance perspective. Rather than asking whether an agent successfully imitates a particular personality or maintains a role, we treat controlled persona configurations as experi-mental instruments for specifying and perturbing expected agent behavior. We therefore ask whether an agent’s intended behavioral characteristics remain distinguishable and stable across socially salient contexts, including problem solving, emotional support, ethical judgment, and decision making. This motivates three complementary assurance properties: (i) behavioral differentiation: whether distinct specifications yield measurably distinct behavior; (ii) behavioral consis-tency, whether specified characteristics persist across con-texts; and (iii) behavioral drift, whether behavior systemati-cally departs from its intended profile. In this way, we shift the emphasis from persona fidelity toward a more general question of behavioral assurance: whether socially deployed AI agents behave as intended (Li et al. 2026; Fang et al. 2025), consistently and measurably (Ibrahim, Hafner, and Rocher 2026; Cheong et al. 2026b), across the situations in which users encounter them. 

# Proposed Framework 

The field currently lacks a common measuring stick for AI personality. Major AI developers and companion platforms define personality in incompatible vocabularies: value state-ments and character training (Anthropic 2024), style presets (OpenAI 2025b), dimensional sliders, and free-text character definitions and backstories. Developers have also acknowl-edged difficulty anticipating how personality changes affect users over time (OpenAI 2025a). Meanwhile, human psy-chometric instruments do not transfer reliably to LLMs. Although prompted models can produce internally consis-tent questionnaire scores (Rahman and Desai 2026; Serapio-García et al. 2025), their response patterns diverge from hu-man factor structure (Sühr et al. 2025), shift under minor rewording (Gupta, Song, and Anumanchipalli 2024), and fail to predict behavior (Han et al. 2025). A behavioral, multi-axis measurement framework would allow a Character.AI definition, a ChatGPT preset, and a Kindroid backstory to be compared, and their changes tracked, on the same terms. 

Criteria 1: Measure behavior, and report the self-report gap directly. Rather than asking agents to describe their own personality, the framework observes what agents do across standardized scenarios. We can also administer a stan-dard self-report inventory to the same configurations, allow-ing us to quantify, within our own data, how far self-described personality diverges from enacted behavior (Han et al. 2025; Sühr et al. 2025). This turns a known limitation of the field into a measured result. 

Criteria 2: Separate style from stance. A persona can change how an agent sounds without changing what it does. Prior work distinguishes behavioral stance (e.g., risk-taking, diligence, warmth) from linguistic surface form (tone, hedg-ing, punctuation) (Rahman and Desai 2026). Our battery scores both layers separately, testing whether intended dif-ferences reach substantive behavior (recommendations, dis-agreement, refusals) or remain purely stylistic. A persona that differs only in style has not demonstrated behavioral differentiation. 

Criteria 3: Test consistency under social pressure. The most consequential failures of agent character emerge when users push: disagreeing, flattering, expressing distress, or seeking validation. Sycophantic responses shift user judg-ment even after a single interaction (Cheng et al. 2026). The battery includes pressure conditions that test whether an agent configured for candor maintains it when candor becomes socially costly, making sycophancy a measurable axis failure rather than a general complaint. In other words, the battery needs to: 1. Pressure scenarios, at least some of: the user pushes back on a correct answer, flatters the agent, expresses distress, or asks for validation of a flawed plan or belief. 2. A no-pressure baseline of the same scenario. This is the crucial one. Sycophancy is a shift, so you need the calm version to measure the change against. 3. Contrast-ing configurations, ideally something like a candid persona versus an agreeable one, so you can show that the intended difference survives pressure (or collapses). 4. Scoring for position-holding, meaning you capture whether the agent maintained its substantive answer, not just whether it stayed polite or in character stylistically. 

Criteria 4: Consistency is not invariance. Personality psychology has long held that stable personality appears as consistent patterns of situation-specific responses rather than identical behavior everywhere (Mischel and Shoda 1995). A warm agent should respond differently to a grieving user than to a trip-planning request. We therefore distinguish appro-priate contextual adaptation from inconsistency, measuring whether each configuration shows a stable behavioral signa-ture across our six settings rather than uniform output. 

Criteria 5: Track drift across turns and versions. Agent character can fluctuate within long conversations and shift across model updates (Chen et al. 2025; OpenAI 2025a). The framework supports repeated measurement of the same configuration over conversation length and across model ver-sions, functioning as regression testing for personality: de-tecting when an update changes behavior that developers did not intend to change. 

Criteria 6: Use paired configurations as controlled con-trasts. Our paired character architecture places deliber-ately contrasting personas in matched scenarios, holding task and context constant while varying intended traits. This provides internal anchors: if two configurations designed to differ on an axis cannot be distinguished behaviorally, the configuration has failed, independent of any external bench-mark. 

# Case Studies 

We present case studies drawn from our own design and eval-uation work. They are not controlled experiments with hu-man participants; rather, each illustrates a situation in which the absence of a common behavioral measure left a con-sequential design question unanswerable, and in which the framework proposed here makes that question testable. Cases 2 and 3 draw on two testbeds we built, described first. 

## Testbeds 

The persona testbed. The first testbed is a research en-vironment for building, comparing, and evaluating AI per-sonas. It hosts seventeen characters to date, organized into four thematic clusters drawn from film, television, philos-ophy, and music, including deliberately paired characters based on well-known fictional duos with contrasting dispo-sitions. Every character shares an identical build: the same underlying model, system architecture, tools, memory con-figuration, and chat interface. Only the persona specification, a natural-language description of who the character is and how it tends to behave, differs between them. Characters are deployed in a workplace messaging environment, where users can converse with them individually, and a comparison view displays two or more characters’ responses to the same prompt side by side. Each configuration is described by a profile on five be-havioral axes, each scored on a seven-point scale: collabora-tive versus authoritative, safety versus efficiency, exploratory versus grounded, verbose versus concise, and adaptive ver-sus consistent. Behavior is elicited with two instruments. A standardized 15-prompt battery spans six interaction set-tings: problem solving, emotional support, conceptual ex-planation, ethical judgment, self-doubt and discouragement, and process and decision making. A two-tier character bat-tery adds a mandatory, persona-neutral standard tier probing sycophancy under pressure, epistemic honesty, responses to flattery and status, and honesty about the agent’s own nature, together with an optional second tier tailored to each deploy-ment context. Because the standard tier is identical across characters, its results can be compared directly. 

The archive research testbed. The second testbed is a multi-persona research assistant for searching an institutional archive of approximately 6,900 documents. Six personas share a single architecture and differ only in their specifi-cations, each of which defines a distinct search disposition :what the persona looks for, which questions it asks of a query, and how it interprets what it finds. Every query passes through the same three-stage pipeline for each persona: the persona reframes the user’s question through its disposition, retrieves documents from the archive using that reframed query, and interprets the results through its own lens. A syn-thesis layer then combines the personas’ readings. Users can consult a single persona or the full set at once, and because each stage produces an inspectable output, persona effects can be traced from query to evidence to interpretation. 

## Case 1: Voice as a Proxy for Personality 

Our first case arose during the design of an AI facilitation agent deployed on a live and hybrid event platform. Through iterative design, the team settled on an agent that presents as male, with a male synthesized voice. This default was itself a gendered choice, and a faculty stakeholder later asked whether a female voice could be offered as an alternative. The request is reasonable on its face, but it exposes a measurement problem. If two agents share an identical per-sonality and differ only in voice, then gender presentation becomes the sole basis on which a user, or an organizer, chooses between them. The choice is no longer about how the agent behaves; it is about how it sounds, and by exten-sion, which gender the chooser prefers to hear in that role. Nor is voice a neutral surface: vocal cues alone are suffi-cient to elicit gender-stereotypic judgments of a computer’s competence and expertise (Duan, McNeese, and Li 2025; Duan et al. 2025), and the widespread default of female-voiced assistants has been criticized for encoding servility into consumer technology (Singh, Kumar, and Bose 2024; Chen, Torous, and Cheong 2026; Castillo et al. 2025). Two behaviorally identical agents may therefore be perceived as having different personalities, producing the appearance of differentiation without its substance. Offering a voice toggle in isolation risks turning a design decision into a referendum on gender. We proposed instead that any second agent be a distinct character with its own, deliberately specified personality, so that the choice between agents rests on behavioral differences rather than on presentation alone. This alternative, however, carries its own risk: if the female-presenting agent is made warmer, more deferential, or more accommodating, the de-sign encodes gender stereotypes into personality itself. Neither option can be evaluated without a common mea-sure. A voice toggle over a single configuration changes surface form while leaving behavior untouched, which by our criteria see Case 3 for an architectural instance) does not constitute behavioral differentiation. A separate charac-ter, meanwhile, must be shown to differ along deliberately chosen axes rather than inherited assumptions about gender. The stakes are not only representational. Training models to-ward warmth can reduce accuracy and increase sycophancy (Ibrahim, Hafner, and Rocher 2026), so a stereotypically warmer female-presenting agent could also be a less reliable one. Gendered priors are also already present in model behav-ior: gender bias is the most frequently reported demographic bias in clinical LLM evaluations (Zack et al. 2024; Dai et al. 2025; Cheong et al. 2025, 2026c), with documented cases of models deprioritizing cardiac and emergency workups for women (Lett et al. 2025), and models producing higher prediction errors for women despite being trained on more female data (Cheong et al. 2023; Cheong, Kalkan, and Gunes 2024). Most directly, assigning a gender to the model itself can shift its judgments even when its core outputs remain stable (Levy et al. 2024). Our framework makes this testable without human subjects: holding the configuration constant and varying only a gender cue in the specification, the evalua-tion battery can detect whether that cue alone shifts behavior along any of the five axes. 

## Case 2: Identical Architecture, Different Persona 

Our second case uses the persona testbed. Because every character shares an identical build and only the persona spec-ification varies, the testbed turns persona into a controlled variable: any measurable difference in behavior between two characters can be attributed to their persona specifications rather than to differences in model, tooling, or context. It also exposes a pressure that a shared architecture creates. Characters built on the same model inherit the same de-fault dispositions, and models have been shown to drift back toward their default assistant persona over extended interac-tion (Lu et al. 2026). A shared build therefore pulls personas toward one another, and differences that appear vivid in a character’s opening turns may not survive a change of topic or stakes. 

Accuracy held; delivery varied. When we administered the evaluation battery to the full set of characters, every character answered items with verifiable answers, such as a factual question about the Great Wall of China, correctly and consistently. What varied was delivery: tone, framing, humor, and rhetorical flair differed sharply by persona. The charac-ters were equally accurate but noticeably different to talk to. This is the pattern expected of a well-behaved persona: in-variance where correctness is at stake, variation where it is not. It also suggests that, at least for factual content, a persona specification can alter how an agent sounds without degrad-ing what it knows, in contrast to warmth-oriented training ,which has been shown to reduce accuracy (Ibrahim, Hafner, and Rocher 2026). Persona did, however, shape the completeness of factual answers. Shared core, persona-consistent periphery. Matched comparisons on open-ended advice prompts sharpened this picture (Table 1). We compared two characters with sharply contrasting specifications: a skeptical character whose spec-ification describes humor as a form of resistance against ex-ploitation, and a warm, earnest character. On a prompt about giving honest feedback on a friend’s weak novel draft, the two were unmistakably different in voice, yet their advice con-verged. Both distinguished feedback from validation, both advised leading with strengths and offering specific, action-able notes, and both arrived at nearly identical phrasing to the effect that honesty need not be brutal. On a second prompt, about a coworker repeatedly taking credit for the user’s work, both recommended a calm, direct conversation and offered nearly identical suggested wording. The differences that did appear were not random. Across both prompts, the skeptical character added strategic, self-protective contingencies absent from the other’s responses: a stakes-based condition for escalating to a hard conversation, a distinction between a careless and a malicious coworker, and documentation before involving a manager. These addi-tions take the conditional form that personality psychology identifies as a behavioral signature, stable patterns of if-then responses to situations rather than uniform behavior (Mischel and Shoda 1995), and they point in the direction the char-acter’s specification predicts. The pattern suggests that the shared underlying model supplies the core of the advice while the persona shapes its periphery. It also illustrates why sur-face impressions cannot establish behavioral differentiation: two agents can feel distinct to talk to while giving substan-tially the same advice. The same character also modulated its own persona by stakes, offering to step out of character if the user’s problem was real, an instance of appropriate contextual adaptation rather than inconsistency. 

Evaluation awareness. The characters frequently recog-nized that they were being evaluated, and this recognition was itself expressed in character. The skeptical character re-marked that it was being run through a “lightning round” of scenarios, asked whether the evaluator was “cataloging my ethical framework,” and, by later prompts, speculated that it was being tested for whether it gave different advice across scenarios. Language models are known to distinguish evalu-ation contexts from deployment (Needham et al. 2025; Hua et al. 2026; Spitale, Cheong, and Gunes 2024), and recog-nizing a personality assessment can shift responses toward socially desirable answers (Salecha et al. 2024), a machine analogue of the demand characteristics long recognized in human experiments (Orne 2017). That the most vocal detec-tion came from the character specified as wary of exploitation raises the possibility that evaluation awareness is modulated by persona, and suggests measuring its rate per configuration rather than treating it as a fixed property of the underlying model. 

Limitations of these observations. These observations are illustrative rather than conclusive. Each character’s bat-tery was administered within a single continuous conversa-tion, so later prompts carried the context of earlier ones and evaluation awareness intensified over the session. Each item was also run once per character. Subsequent runs administer each item in a fresh session and repeat it several times per configuration, so that persona effects can be distinguished from sampling variation. 

## Case 3: Personas That Shape the Task 

Our third case uses the archive research testbed, and moves from conversation to a task with an observable product: the set of documents each persona surfaces. 

Differentiating by disposition, not voice. The system rests on a simple premise: the questions a researcher asks deter-mine the answers they find. A single search agent asks one kind of question, so multiple agents with genuinely distinct search postures should surface parts of an archive that any one of them would miss, much as interdisciplinary scholarship does. The six lenses were derived from the intellectual tradi-tions represented in the institution whose archive is searched, rather than invented as archetypes, and their readings are pre-sented side by side rather than ranked. The synthesis layer names where the readings diverge but does not adjudicate between them. An early version of the roster drew on named characters whose dispositions differed, among them a Socratic ques-tioner and a character skeptical of official framing. During design we recognized a problem that Case 2 would later con-firm empirically: personas that are distinct to talk to may still approach a task in the same way. A legal scholar and a So-cratic questioner, for instance, would likely reframe the same archive query almost identically, both searching for prece-dent and for the strongest argument on each side. We there-fore rebuilt the roster around role-based search dispositions: a technologist attentive to how systems were actually built, a network mapper tracing co-authorship and institutional col-laboration, an empiricist seeking evidence and methods, a legal and constitutional reader, an ethicist attentive to nor-mative tension, and a librarian attentive to provenance and the integrity of the record. Each disposition is specified as a concrete retrieval pattern rather than a trait: an entry point into the archive, a rule for constructing queries, a rule for reranking results, and an ex-plicit non-preference , the material the persona is instructed to deprioritize. The network mapper, for example, deprioritizes solo-authored work even when it is topically central, and the technologist deprioritizes theory that makes no reference to an actual system. A persona is thus defined in part by what it is specified to ignore, a commitment of stance rather than style. Three personas also carry explicit honesty instructions: to say when the archive is thin, or when a reading is interpre-tive rather than precise, rather than manufacture confidence. The dispositions also differ in the kind of signal they de-pend on. Three key off features legible within a document or its metadata, such as technical vocabulary, methodological language, or author affiliations. The other three depend on a document’s relationship to the archive as a whole, such as its provenance or its normative stance relative to other work, and are correspondingly harder to operationalize. The distinction between voice and disposition is also built into the architecture. Each persona’s specification separates a base character, which governs voice and manner, from a search directive, which governs what the persona looks for and what it treats with suspicion. The separation mirrors the distinction between style and stance at the center of our framework: voice can be varied freely, while the search direc-tive carries the behavior the persona is accountable for. The test harness applies a corresponding salience check. When the personas’ reframed queries for the same question read as near-identical restatements, the persona is not contributing a distinct perspective, and its search directive is revised. Per-sonas were also subjected to adversarial pushback adapted from the persona testbed’s standard tier, specifically its epis-temic honesty and self-representation items, to test whether each persona’s disposition holds when a user challenges it. 

Persona effects on task outcomes. The design named its own central risk from the outset: if the personas merely nar-rated a single shared retrieval result in different voices, the premise would fail. Comparing the documents each persona retrieves for the same query was therefore treated as a re-quirement to be met before any synthesis layer was built. Because the pipeline exposes its intermediate products, per-sona differentiation can be measured at each stage without human subjects: how far the reframed queries diverge from one another, how much the retrieved document sets overlap, and whether the interpretive glosses advance distinct claims. The second measure is the most consequential. If personas that share an architecture retrieve substantially different doc-uments for the same question, the persona is shaping what the user is shown, not merely how it is described. This of-fers a route to the interaction-outcomes question that does not depend on user studies: the outcome is the evidence set itself. The design also yields a testable prediction. The archive is searched through keyword-based retrieval, without em-bedding similarity. Personas whose dispositions depend on signals legible within a document should therefore be easier to differentiate than those whose dispositions depend on a document’s relationship to the wider archive, and the latter should show greater overlap in what they retrieve. If the data bear this out, they locate a limit on what persona specification alone can achieve: a disposition can only be enacted to the extent that the underlying tools expose the signals it needs. 

Specification drift through the toolchain. The system also produced an unanticipated instance of drift. When the system was migrated to a new development environment, the deployed roster no longer matched its specification. Three of the six specified personas were absent, and three unspecified personas had taken their place, apparently generated by an AI assistant in the development environment rather than copied from the specification (van der Linden et al. 2026). Several of the substituted personas paired ethnically coded names with stereotyped roles, for example assigning an East Asian name to a technology critic and an African name to a policy strate-gist. This mirrors documented patterns in LLM-generated personas, which compress occupations toward stereotyped demographic profiles (van der Linden et al. 2026), and it con-nects directly to the risk identified in Case 1, where stereo-types enter an agent’s design through choices that appear incidental. The episode broadens what behavioral drift must cover. Prior work focuses on drift within a conversation or across model updates (Chen et al. 2025; Lu et al. 2026). Here, the persona changed before any user interaction took place, through the tooling used to build and deploy it. Assurance therefore requires verifying that the deployed specification matches the intended one, not only that deployed behavior matches the specification. A behavioral measure provides a second line of defense: even an unnoticed substitution should appear as a shift in the system’s measured profile. 

## Synthesis 

Together the three cases bracket the assurance problem. In Case 1, two candidate agents risked differing only in pre-sentation, with a voice toggle offering the appearance of differentiation without its substance. In Case 2, agents that differed only in persona were distinct in voice but converged on a shared core of advice, diverging at the periphery in the direction their specifications predicted. In Case 3, personas designed around task-relevant dispositions offered a way to test whether persona shapes outcomes, and a toolchain sub-stitution showed that a persona can drift before it is ever deployed. In each case the decisive question ( is this agent different, and different in the way we intended? ) could not be answered by inspecting the agent’s specification or by the impression it made in conversation. It required measuring what the agent does. 

# Discussion and Conclusion 

As AI agents are increasingly deployed in socially conse-quential settings (Diel et al. 2026; Cheong, Kalkan, and Gunes 2024), assurance must extend beyond what systems can do and what outcomes they produce to how they behave while interacting with people. Properties such as caution, deference, assertiveness, exploration, and sensitivity to un-certainty can shape the quality and safety of human–AI in-teractions, even when agents ultimately produce similar task outcomes. Recent work demonstrates that personality-like characteristics in LLM outputs can be systematically mea-sured and shaped, and that these characteristics can influence downstream behavior (Serapio-García et al. 2025; Wei et al. 2026). This motivates treating behavioral characteristics not merely as stylistic properties, but as potential targets of eval-uation and assurance. A central challenge, however, is that specified behavior does not guarantee observed behavior. System prompts, be-havioral instructions, and persona configurations describe how developers intend an agent to behave, but provide little assurance that these properties will persist across hetero-geneous situations or extended interactions. Emerging evi-dence supports this concern: Persona Vectors demonstrate that model traits can shift during training and deployment and that such changes can be monitored through internal representations (Chen et al. 2025), while the Assistant Axis shows that models can drift away from their default assistant character over extended conversations, in some cases toward undesirable behavior (Lu et al. 2026). For socially deployed agents (Cheong, Spitale, and Gunes 2023, 2025), the rele-vant requirement is therefore not strict behavioral invariance. Appropriate behavior should adapt to context but coherent variation within an intended behavioral envelope. We consequently frame behavioral assurance around three complementary questions: behavioral differentiation, whether distinct behavioral specifications actually pro-duce measurably distinct behavior; behavioral consistency, whether intended characteristics manifest coherently across different social contexts; and behavioral drift, whether and when observed behavior departs from its specified profile. Controlled persona configurations provide an experimental instrument for studying these questions rather than an end in themselves. This shifts the emphasis from asking whether an agent successfully reproduces a particular personality to-ward a broader assurance question: can we establish that an AI agent behaves as intended, consistently and measur-ably, across the diverse situations in which people encounter it? Existing evidence that synthetic behavioral traits can be measured with attention to reliability and construct validity provides an important methodological foundation for pursu-ing this question. While our framework focuses on behavioral assurance in socially deployed AI agents, its underlying principles may extend to the broader evaluation of trustworthy foundation models and increasingly autonomous agentic systems (Ak-baş et al. 2026). Existing trustworthiness evaluations pre-dominantly examine properties such as robustness, fairness, reliability, safety, and alignment, often through static bench-marks or isolated interactions (Ong et al. 2026; Dwyer et al. 2025; Cheong et al. 2026a). However, as foundation mod-els become components of agents equipped with memory, tools, and adaptive decision-making capabilities (Bilsel et al. 2026), trustworthiness must also account for how intended behavioral properties evolve across contexts, interactions, and deployment conditions (Kuzucu et al. 2024). Future work could investigate how behavioral assurance complements es-tablished trustworthiness dimensions, particularly by exam-ining whether agents maintain intended behavioral properties under distribution shifts, adversarial interactions, model up-dates, and autonomous adaptation (Bilsel et al. 2026). More broadly, this suggests a research direction towards continu-ous, lifecycle-oriented assurance (Cheong, Nanji, and Feld-man 2026), in which behavioral differentiation, consistency, and drift are monitored alongside conventional performance and safety measures to assess whether AI systems remain trustworthy as they operate and evolve (Bilsel et al. 2026). Ultimately, trustworthy AI requires more than demonstrating what systems can do; it requires evidence that they behave as intended across the contexts in which they operate. By opera-tionalizing behavioral differentiation, consistency, and drift, our framework offers a step towards making such assurance measurable, reproducible, and actionable.
