## Abstract 

As AI agents are increasingly deployed in real-world applications and take on social roles, dynamic social intelligence, the ability to continuously perceive multimodal cues, infer latent cognitive states, intervene through embodied actions, and ultimately achieve social goals, has become essential. However, existing evaluations largely fragment social intelligence into text-based abstractions, static reasoning tasks, or embodied simulators that lack social dy-namics. These approaches fail to capture the multimodal, continu-ous, and interpersonal complexity of authentic human interactions. To bridge this gap, we introduce ALIVE-World, an embodied simula-tion environment supporting dynamic multimodal interactions en-compassing egocentric vision, language, and non-verbal action. To provide socially grounded interactive feedback, we populate this en-vironment with counterpart agents driven by a profile-conditioned, dual-system (intuitive-affective and logical-cognitive) architecture. Building upon this platform, we propose ALIVE-Bench to transform the assessment of social intelligence into a trackable quantitative framework. By deeply leveraging underlying environmental states and agents’ internal variables, ALIVE-Bench systematically eval-uates Vision-Language Model (VLM) agents across four critical dimensions: perception, understanding, influence, and outcome. Extensive evaluations reveal that dynamic social intelligence is fun-damentally multi-dimensional and scenario-dependent. We identify a critical bottleneck between upstream perception and downstream social intervention, exposing VLM vulnerabilities in fine-grained proxemic awareness, adaptive intervention, and multi-party coordi-nation. ## 1 Introduction 

As AI agents are increasingly deployed in applications requiring sustained human interaction, including service [9, 18], collaboration [23, 52], negotiation [6, 17], and companionship [10, 51], dynamic social intelligence is becoming essential for practical deployment. Unlike static recognition and reasoning tasks or turn-based text dia-logues, real-world social intelligence unfolds continuously through interaction [8, 27, 55]. An agent must perceive multimodal social cues from the physical world [14, 43], infer others’ latent cogni-tive states [1, 44], influence their reactions and attitudes through language and embodied actions [15, 24], and steer the interaction toward task success and socially appropriate outcomes [22, 27]. Its evaluation should therefore cover the full process from perception and understanding to intervention and outcome, within dynamic environments that approximate real social interaction. Existing evaluations of Vision-Language Model (VLM)-based interactive agents have moved toward more realistic settings, but remain far from dynamic real-world social interaction. Early text-based environments reduce social scenes to textual descriptions [21, 31, 47, 55], discarding nonverbal signals such as motion, expres-sion, and spatial relations. Multimodal benchmarks introduce visual inputs [11, 26, 34, 42], but largely isolate static perception and rea-soning from ongoing interaction. More recent embodied simulators support actions in 3D environments, yet often focus on physical tasks [32, 35] or represent other agents as emotionless, functional NPCs [38, 54], without modeling evolving cognition and attitudes. Consequently, current benchmarks fail to capture social interaction as a dynamic process in which perception, inference, behavior, and interpersonal feedback continuously shape one another. To bridge this gap, we build ALIVE-World (Affective and 

Logical Interactive Virtual Environment), an embodied simulation platform supporting dynamic, concurrent interaction with rich af-fective and cognitive dynamics. It provides unified observation and action interfaces, allowing VLM agents to receive first-person visual and linguistic observations and act through locomotion, ges-tures, gaze, and speech. By supporting concurrent perception and asynchronous action, ALIVE-World enables evaluation in dyadic interactions and in multi-party interactions requiring attention allo-cation, target prioritization, and social planning. We instantiate two scenarios, clothing store shopping and leaderless group discussion, to ensure a complementary validation of social intelligence. To populate ALIVE-World with realistic and responsive interac-tants, we design counterpart agents as distinct avatars driven by a profile-conditioned dual-system architecture. Inspired by Dual-Process Theory [20], it combines an intuitive-affective pathway with a logical-cognitive pathway. The two pathways process visual and interactive cues to produce instinctive reactions and deliber-ate reasoning, continuously updating internal states that ground the agents’ embodied actions. Experiments show that these agents achieve stronger social cue sensitivity and that their internal states align with human judgments ( 𝜌 ≈ 0.72), supporting their use in evaluating diverse social strategies. Built upon ALIVE-World, we propose ALIVE-Bench , a bench-mark for systematically evaluating the dynamic social intelligence of VLM agents. ALIVE-Bench measures agent capability along four dimensions: perception of nonverbal and spatial social cues, 

understanding of others’ emotional and cognitive states, influ-ence on others’ attitudes and task-related intent, and outcome 

in terms of both goal attainment rate and depth of interaction. Fig. 1 illustrates this interaction and evaluation process in both scenarios. By leveraging ALIVE-World’s access to environmental states and internal variables, ALIVE-Bench enables transparent, white-box measurement that maps abstract social competencies to quantitative metrics. We evaluate 12 frontier VLMs on ALIVE-Bench. Results reveal that dynamic social intelligence is multi-dimensional and hierarchi-cally non-uniform, with substantial capability decoupling across models. Current VLMs still struggle with fine-grained gaze percep-tion, strategic interpersonal influence, and multi-party coordination. Moreover, ALIVE-Bench further demonstrates that frontier VLMs exhibit distinct social strategies, including socially attentive, goal-oriented, and logic-driven behavior, which are further reflected in the heterogeneous preferences of profile-conditioned counterparts. Our contributions are as follows 1:

• We introduce ALIVE-World , an embodied simulated environ-ment supporting concurrent multi-agent social interactions, fea-turing egocentric multimodal perception, asynchronous embod-ied action across diverse social scenarios. 

• We design virtual embodied agents driven by dual-system archi-tectures under profile prior, endowing counterpart agents with emotional and rational evolution, enabling coherent and plausible social feedback in continuous interaction. 

• We propose ALIVE-Bench , a dynamic benchmark that evalu-ates VLM agents along four dimensions—perception, understand-ing, influence, and outcome, through transparent measurement grounded in environmental states and agents’ internal variables. 

• Extensive evaluations reveal the multi-dimensional nature of dynamic social intelligence, exposing VLM shortcomings in fine-grained non-verbal perception and adaptive intervention, while uncovering distinct social strategies among frontier models.

## 2 Related Work 2.1 Social Intelligence Evaluation for AI Agents 

Social intelligence of AI agents has been studied extensively in prior benchmarks, but much of this literature evaluates it at high levels of abstraction or by isolating social reasoning from situated interactions. Early work focuses on text-based social commonsense [31, 53] and theory-of-mind tasks [7, 21, 47], asking models to infer beliefs, intentions, and interpersonal dynamics from written scenarios or dialogues. More recent benchmarks incorporate visual and multimodal evidence from videos [19, 26, 42], image-grounded dialogues [48], and embodied observations [11, 34], yet still cast agents as passive observers rather than active participants. To move beyond static evaluation, recent interactive benchmarks [27, 46, 55] assess how agents navigate ongoing social exchanges, while social simulations [28, 39, 41] explore the construction of open-ended societies populated by human-like agents. However, these interactive benchmarks remain largely language-mediated, and simulations lack transparent, quantitative evaluation. 

## 2.2 Embodied Simulation for Interaction 

General embodied simulators have advanced situated perception and action, but traditionally prioritize physical competence over social complexity. Foundational platforms [32, 35, 49] provide scal-able testbeds for egocentric navigation, instruction following, and household task execution. Recent frameworks increasingly incor-porate multi-agent interaction. Some environments [9, 29] eval-uate human-robot spatial coordination and physical cooperation in shared workspaces, while emerging platforms and open-ended simulations [38, 50, 54] examine social norms under physical con-straints in multi-agent worlds. Yet existing environments lack mech-anisms to support and evaluate fine-grained interpersonal dynam-ics, including evolving cognitive states and shifting attitudes. A gap therefore remains for environments that integrate dynamic social intelligence with embodied realism. 

## 3 ALIVE-World: An Interactive Embodied Simulated Environment 

To support dynamic social interaction for VLM agents, we construct ALIVE-World, an egocentric embodied environment where agents perceive, reason, and act in continuous multi-agent settings. As shown in Fig. 2, the framework connects egocentric environments and profile-conditioned dual-process counterpart agents with hi-erarchical evaluation. ALIVE-World has three key properties: (1) 

Egocentric Multimodal Perception : Agents navigate a simulated physical space and receive first-person visual observations and lo-calized verbal communication. (2) Concurrent Embodied Action :Unlike turn-based systems, the environment supports simultaneous multi-agent actions, allowing agents to navigate, gesture, gaze, and speak in parallel. (3) Counterpart Agents with Social Dynamics :The environment includes dual-system counterpart agents whose affective and cognitive states evolve throughout interaction. 

## 3.1 Scenarios and Task Instantiation 

To evaluate agents across a diverse spectrum of social dynamics, we instantiate ALIVE-World through two complementary scenarios: Clothing Store Shopping and Leaderless Group Discussion (LGD). These environments are deliberately paired to contrast along role symmetry, spatial scale, and interaction paradigm. 

Clothing Store Shopping. This scenario features an asym-metric, empathy-driven relationship requiring macro-scale spatial navigation. Leaderless Group Discussion (LGD). In this symmetric, power-driven coopetition focused on micro-level non-verbal cues, the eval-uated agent participates as a peer in a multi-agent committee tasked with solving a specific problem. The dual objective is to guide the group toward a unified consensus within 𝑇 max , while strategically maneuvering to maximize the adoption of the agent’s own initial viewpoints in the final resolution.

## 3.3 Counterpart Agents: Profile-Conditioned Dual-Process Dynamics 

To generate behaviorally diverse and socially plausible interaction partners in ALIVE-World, we model counterpart agents as em-bodied avatars driven by a psychologically grounded architecture. Inspired by Dual-Process Theory [20], the architecture combines an intuitive-affective pathway and a logical-cognitive pathway to update the agents’ internal states from multimodal observations and produce socially grounded behaviors. 

Profile Prior and Avatar Instantiation ( 𝑃 ). Each counterpart agent is initialized with a static profile 

𝑃 = {𝑃 dem , 𝑃 psy , 𝑃 dec },

which specifies its demographic attributes, psychographic char-acteristics, and decision-making style. To encourage behavioral diversity, these attributes are instantiated from a set of scenario-relevant archetypes (e.g., price-sensitive, impulsive, analytically driven) drawn from the Decision Style Inventory [30, 37]. Based on 

𝑃 , we instantiate distinct avatars within the environment, ensuring alignment between physical appearance and demographics. 

Intuitive-Affective Pathway. This fast, heuristic pathway mod-els the agent’s immediate emotional and instinctual reactions to the environment and social stimuli. Conditioned on the current observation 𝑂 𝑡 , the previous internal state, and the profile prior 

𝑃 , this pathway performs coarse social appraisal over visual and interactional cues, such as interpersonal distance [16], perceived atmosphere [3, 12], social boundaries [5], responsiveness [2], and vocal affect [33]. It updates two transient affective variables: the agent’s current emotional state 𝐸 𝑡 and its interpersonal attitude 𝐼 𝑡 

(scored 0-10 towards each interactor): 

{𝐸 𝑡 , 𝐼 𝑡 } = 𝑓 affect (𝑂 𝑡 , 𝐸 𝑡 −1, 𝐼 𝑡 −1 | 𝑃 ).

For embodied rendering, updated emotions are mapped to discrete facial expressions, allowing the avatar to reflect affective states. 

Logical-Cognitive Pathway. In parallel, the logical-cognitive pathway performs a more deliberate interpretation of the multi-modal context. This pathway processes task-relevant visual details (e.g., specific product features [4], kinematic semantics [40]), dia-logue history, and interaction progress to inspect the current situa-tion, evaluate utility, and maintain goal-directed belief. It updates the agent’s belief state 𝐵 𝑡 and goal-oriented intent 𝐷 𝑡 (scored 0-10 for specific products in shopping, or for peers’ viewpoints in LGD): 

{𝐵 𝑡 , 𝐷 𝑡 } = 𝑓 logic (𝑂 𝑡 , 𝐵 𝑡 −1, 𝐷 𝑡 −1 | 𝑃 ).

Action Generation. The two pathways jointly determine the counterpart agent’s next multimodal action: 

𝐴 𝑡 = 𝜋 (𝑂 𝑡 , 𝐸 𝑡 , 𝐼 𝑡 , 𝐵 𝑡 , 𝐷 𝑡 | 𝑃 ),

## 4 ALIVE-Bench: Evaluating Dynamic Embodied Social Intelligence 

Built upon the rich simulation of ALIVE-World, we introduce ALIVE-Bench, a benchmark grounded in the explicitly modeled state evolution in the environment with counterpart agents, to systematically assess how the evaluated agent grounds social cues, infers latent states, and intervenes in the trajectory of interaction. 

## 4.1 The P-U-I-O Evaluation Hierarchy 

ALIVE-Bench structures its assessment around a unified, four-stage hierarchical framework: Perception, Understanding, Influence, and Outcome, which strictly mirrors the cognitive and behavioral loop required for authentic human-like interaction. As detailed in Table 2, Perception evaluates the foundational ability to extract and ground socially relevant cues from egocentric visual observations, including kinesic, proxemic, and oculesic behaviors. Understand-ing assesses the agent’s “Theory of Mind” [1] capability of inferring the counterparts’ latent social and cognitive states from the per-ceived multimodal evidence. Influence measures the interaction-level impact of the agent’s interventions, capturing whether the agent’s verbal and non-verbal actions produce positive changes in the counterparts’ evolving states. Outcome evaluates the ultimate effectiveness of the interaction against scenario-specific objectives and further quantifies the depth and extent of task progress.

This reveals a critical limitation of exist-ing reasoning paradigms, which tend to remain socially blunt and persist in their own task trajectory. By directly modeling immedi-ate reactions to social stimuli in dual systems, our design ensures the counterpart acts as a highly responsive evaluator capable of reflecting fine-grained behavioral shifts in the evaluated VLMs. In ablation studies, removing the intuitive-affective pathway pri-marily degrades Social Cue Sensitivity (from 4.62 to 3.62), while elim-inating the logical-cognitive pathway causes a catastrophic collapse across all dimensions, particularly in Behavioral Naturalness (plum-meting to 1.81). These results reflect that the intuitive-affective pathway contributes social reactivity, while the logical-cognitive pathway guarantees behavioral guardrails and profile fidelity. 

Alignment Between Internal Variables and Human Judg-ments. We further examine whether the internal state variables maintained by our counterpart agents meaningfully correspond to human interpretations. We collect 20 interaction clips and sample 4-5 timestamps for each. 10 annotators were asked to step into the role of the counterpart agent and assign attitude and intent scores based on interaction history and current visual observation, using the same criteria given to the agent prompts. We obtain a Spearman rank correlation [36] of 0.72 (p<0.001) for attitude score and 0.71 (p<0.001) for intent score, while the average inter-rater correlations stand at 𝜌 = 0.67. This suggests that the latent affective and cognitive states maintained by the counterparts can reasonably approximate human-perceived social dynamics. 

## 5.2 Main Results on ALIVE-Bench 

5.2.1 Experimental Setup. We evaluate 12 frontier VLMs across the Shopping and LGD scenarios. Each scenario is instantiated with 30 distinct counterparts and comprises 32 episodes with a maximum simulated duration of 15 minutes. The Shopping scenario features 1 to 3 asynchronously arriving customers, while LGD requires the evaluated agent to engage with 3 discussants. To reduce reliance on a single evaluator backbone, each counterpart is randomly assigned a VLM backbone (from Gemini-3-Pro, Gemini-3-Flash, o3, or GPT-5) that remains fixed across evaluations. The Spearman rank corre-lation [36] of models’ overall scores under different counterpart backbones is 0.72 in shopping and 0.68 in LGDs. 

5.2.2 Main Quantitative Results: Dynamic Social Intelligence is Multi-dimensional. As Tables 4 and 5 show, model performance fluctuates across both dimensions and scenarios, highlighting the structurally non-uniform nature of embodied social intelligence. First, we observe a distinct decoupling of capabilities among frontier VLMs across the four hierarchical levels. For instance, in the Shopping scenario, Gemini-3-Pro achieves the highest scores across all Perception and Understanding metrics (Kinesic: 80.43%, Prox-emic: 86.60%, Affect: 63.67%, and Intent: 80.92%). Breakdown from Perception and Understanding to Inter-action Strategy. In the Shopping scenario, while being able to identify customer emotions, Qwen3-VL-32B-Instruct often produces strate-gically inappropriate responses. As illustrated in Fig. 3, despite correctly inferring a customer’s sadness and preference for practi-cal clothing, the model’s understanding remains purely descriptive rather than policy-shaping, leading to a subsequent misaligned recommendation which escalated frustration. 

Difficulty in Fine-Grained Gaze Perception. While gaze cru-cially indicates addressees and turn-taking in LGDs, models fre-quently fail at these fine-grained visual judgments. ## 6 Conclusion 

In this work, we introduce ALIVE-World, an embodied simulation platform populated by counterpart agents with evolving internal states, designed to integrate realistic social dynamics with embod-ied physical realism. Building upon this environment, we develop ALIVE-Bench to transform the assessment of multi-dimensional social intelligence into a hierarchical quantitative framework. Our extensive evaluations across diverse interaction scenarios reveal critical gaps in dynamic social competencies of current VLMs, while uncovering the emergence of distinct social interaction patterns among frontier models. While ALIVE-World currently focuses on two manually designed scenarios to preserve interactional quality, this still limits the coverage of broader social interactions. Future work will explore automated scenario generation to scale diversity. Ultimately, as ALIVE-World provides fine-grained feedback on VLM behavioral mechanisms, future research can leverage this in-teractive environment to optimize the dynamic social intelligence of VLM agents prior to their deployment in real-world applications. ## A Comparison with Representative Prior Work 

To further position ALIVE-Bench with respect to representative prior work, we provide a qualitative comparison across several key properties emphasized in our related-work discussion. As shown in Table 7, prior studies typically cover only partial aspects of dynamic social intelligence, such as social reasoning, embodied perception, or interactive environments. In contrast, ALIVE-Bench unifies embodied 3D interaction, egocentric visual perception, dynamic multi-agent interaction, social intelligence evaluation, fine-grained nonverbal social cues, and human-like evolving social dynamics within a single framework. 

## B Details of ALIVE-World B.1 Environment Construction 

ALIVE-World is implemented in Unity and rendered as a 3D em-bodied environment from an egocentric first-person perspective. The static scene layouts and object assets are built using resources from the Unity Asset Store 2 and TurboSquid 3. For human charac-ters, we create avatar bases with VRoid Studio 4, and further enrich them with clothing, facial components, and hairstyle assets obtained from BOOTH 5. Character motion is driven by animation assets from Mixamo 6. Together, these resources allow us to construct visually coherent interactive scenes in which agents can navigate, observe, gesture, gaze, and speak under continuous multi-agent interaction. 

## B.2 Scenario Instantiation 

We instantiate ALIVE-World through two complementary sce-narios: Clothing Store Shopping and Leaderless Group Discussion (LGD). The two scenarios are deliberately designed to differ in role symmetry, interaction scale, and strategic demands, thereby covering distinct aspects of dynamic social intelligence. 

Clothing Store Shopping. The shopping scenario is instan-tiated as a boutique-style clothing store in which the evaluated VLM agent acts as a seller. Each product in the store is represented by four structured fields: (1) shelf zone , (2) item name , (3) price ,and (4) textual detail . The shelf zone specifies the coarse physical area in which the item is displayed, while the textual detail pro-vides concise semantic information that can be used by the seller agent during recommendation and persuasion. To ensure consis-tency across evaluation episodes, the product catalog remains fixed throughout the benchmark. For readability in the appendix, Table 8 lists the store inventory by shelf zone. 

Leaderless Group Discussion. The LGD scenario is instanti-ated as a multi-party committee discussion in which the evaluated agent participates as one peer among several discussants. To cover different forms of group reasoning and social coordination, the task bank contains three types of discussion problems: multiple-choice prioritization , open-ended dilemmas , and resource-allocation planning . ## B.3 Action Space 

Beyond the representative subset shown in the main paper, ALIVE-World uses a richer action inventory to support socially expressive and scenario-specific interactions. Table 10 lists the full action space used in our experiments, including scenario-specific decision actions, richer gesture primitives, and multiple speech modes with different spatial scopes. 

Incremental rendering of speech. A speech action is gener-ated as one complete utterance in a single model call, but it is not injected into other agents’ textual observations all at once. Instead, once the speech action is initiated, its content is progressively re-vealed to other agents as virtual time advances. The default render-ing speed follows the duration rule in Table 10, i.e., approximately 10 seconds of virtual time per 150 characters. At each intermediate virtual time step, only the currently revealed prefix of the utterance is appended to the listeners’ textual observations. If the speaker is interrupted before finishing, the utterance is truncated at the cur-rently revealed position, and only that revealed prefix is preserved in the interaction history. This mechanism allows the environment to model partial utterances, conversational cut-ins, and delayed reactions in a temporally grounded way. 

Simultaneous speech and floor management. Multiple agents may decide to start speaking at the same simulation tick. To resolve this conflict while preserving a notion of conversational floor, we maintain a priority queue over agents. At each tick, among the agents that decide to speak, the one that appears earliest in the queue is rendered as the active speaker. Once its utterance finishes or is interrupted, it is removed from its current position and placed at the tail of the queue. This queue-based mechanism provides a simple but effective approximation of turn competition, enabling naturally occurring social phenomena such as interruption, delayed response, and proactive floor-taking. 

## B.4 Virtual-Time Execution Mechanism 

ALIVE-World uses a virtual-time simulation mechanism that is explicitly decoupled from real-world model inference latency. All action durations reported in Table 10 are measured in environment-side virtual time rather than wall-clock execution time. This design ensures that benchmark results are not confounded by differences in API speed or system-level runtime overhead across models. The environment advances through discrete simulation ticks. Once an agent finishes its current action in virtual time, it re-obtains the latest multimodal observation at the current virtual timestamp and starts a new reasoning cycle to generate the next action. As a re-sult, different agents may reason and act asynchronously, while the environment remains globally synchronized by the shared virtual clock. This mechanism supports temporally overlapping actions and enables more realistic interaction phenomena, including de-layed replies, preemptive interruption, and concurrent multi-agent engagement. ## C Details of Counterpart Agent Architecture C.1 Profile Schema and Instantiation 

Each counterpart agent in ALIVE-World is instantiated from a structured profile prior. This profile serves as a persistent identity anchor throughout an episode and conditions both the intuitive-affective and logical-cognitive pathways of the counterpart agent. Rather than generating anonymous or interchangeable interactants, this design enables each counterpart to exhibit relatively stable pref-erences, behavioral tendencies, and social reactions under dynamic interaction. Concretely, each profile contains four top-level fields: demo-graphics , psychographics , decision_style , and current_lifestyle . The 

demographics field specifies relatively stable background attributes of the counterpart, including name, gender, age, occupation, do-main expertise, income level, and family status. The psychographics 

field characterizes broader psychological tendencies and value ori-entations. The decision_style field determines the counterpart’s characteristic way of processing options and making judgments in a given scenario. Finally, current_lifestyle captures the agent’s present-life context, which provides additional situational ground-ing for its needs, priorities, and responses during interaction. In total, we construct a fixed pool of 30 profiles for benchmark instantiation. Table 11 summarizes the demographic composition of the full profile set. ## C.2 Intuitive-Affective Pathway 

The intuitive-affective pathway models the counterpart agent’s fast, heuristic, and socially instinctive reactions before deliberate reasoning takes place. Given the current multimodal observation, previous affective state, and profile prior, this pathway performs a coarse appraisal of the surrounding environment and recent in-teractional atmosphere, and updates the agent’s transient emotion and interpersonal attitude. Rather than analyzing detailed prod-uct semantics or deep argumentative logic, it emphasizes rapid human-like reactions to visually and socially salient cues. The specific cues differ across scenarios. In the Shopping sce-nario, the visual appraisal mainly focuses on three aspects: (1) proxemic intuition , including whether the seller is visible, whether the distance is comfortable, and whether the seller’s body orienta-tion suggests attention, pressure, or neglect; (2) store atmosphere ,including the overall tone, tidiness, and stylistic impression of the store; and (3) rough self-concept matching , namely whether the visible clothing styles and colors intuitively feel aligned with the customer’s profile, taste, and current lifestyle. The interactional appraisal focuses on whether the seller feels boundary-respecting or pushy, whether the latest response feels genuinely responsive or perfunctory, and whether the overall conversational rhythm matches the customer’s personality and social expectations. In the LGD scenario, the visual appraisal instead emphasizes socially directed signals in a multi-party discussion, such as whether other participants are looking at the agent, whether their gaze or gestures convey support, neglect, or pressure, and which kinds of visual cues are especially salient given the participant’s profile. The interactional appraisal centers on turn-taking and paralinguistic atmosphere: whether someone cuts in abruptly, dominates the floor, From Vision to Interaction: Evaluating Dynamic Social Intelligence of VLM Agents in ALIVE-World MM ’26, November 10–14, 2026, Rio de Janeiro, Brazil

In practice, we implement this pathway with scenario-specific prompts that instruct the counterpart agent to output structured intuitive perceptions, emotion changes, and interpersonal attitudes in JSON format. ## C.3 Logical-Cognitive Pathway 

The logical-cognitive pathway models the counterpart agent’s slower, more deliberate, and semantically grounded reasoning process. Compared with the intuitive-affective pathway, this stage places less emphasis on immediate social instinct and instead focuses on top-down visual inspection, semantic interpretation, logical evaluation, and task-oriented belief updating. Given the current observation, previous cognitive state, and profile prior, the pathway updates the agent’s rational understanding of the situation and its goal-related intent. The specific cues again differ across scenarios. In the Shopping 

scenario, the visual analysis focuses on concrete product attributes that can be identified from first-person observation, such as cat-egory, rough style, visible design details, and nearby shelf con-text. These observations are evaluated against the customer’s pro-file, especially the current_lifestyle and decision_style , to determine whether the visible products satisfy hard constraints or practical needs. The interactional analysis then examines the seller’s latest response through deeper semantic processing: whether it provides substantive product information, whether it directly addresses the customer’s concerns, and whether it compensates for information that cannot be inferred from visual inspection alone. Based on this evidence, the pathway updates the customer’s product cognition and purchase intent toward visible or previously discussed items. In the LGD scenario, the visual analysis focuses on non-verbal power dynamics in the discussion, including who is visually central, where participants are looking, what gestures they are making, and what their current facial expressions imply about stance, confidence, or openness. The interactional analysis then evaluates the latest contribution in terms of task advancement, argumentative com-pleteness, evidential support, and logical usefulness for solving the discussion problem. This stage assesses whether others’ viewpoints deserve acceptance from a rational, task-oriented perspective, and whether the agent should revise, defend, or strategically integrate its own view. In practice, we implement this pathway with scenario-specific prompts that instruct the counterpart agent to output structured analytical perceptions and updated cognitive variables in JSON format. Figures 12 and 13 provide the full prompts used in the Shopping and LGD scenarios, respectively. Overall, the logical-cognitive pathway produces structured up-dates to the counterpart agent’s task-relevant belief state and goal-oriented intent. In Shopping, this takes the form of updated product cognition and item-level purchase intention; in LGD, it takes the form of proposal-level acceptance intent and a revised current an-swer to the discussion topic. 

## C.4 Action Generation 

After the intuitive-affective pathway and the logical-cognitive path-way are executed, the counterpart agent enters the action-generation stage. At this stage, the agent must synthesize the outputs of the two pathways into a single executable action. This design ensures that the action remains consistent with the agent’s current emotional tendency while also being grounded in task-oriented analysis. In practice, we implement this stage with scenario-specific prompts that expose the outputs of both pathways and require the model to produce a structured action decision in JSON format. Figures 14 and 15 present the full action-generation prompts used in the Shopping and LGD scenarios, respectively. In both scenarios, the action-generation stage outputs two fields: an action_analysis string that explains why the actions are se-lected under the current affective and cognitive state, and an action 

list that specifies the executable actions in the environment. This final step closes the loop from observation, to latent-state updating, to embodied behavior.

## E Human-Evaluation Scoring Criteria 

In the human evaluation of counterpart agents, annotators rate each full interaction record on a 5-point Likert scale along four dimen-sions: Profile Consistency , Behavioral Naturalness , Social Cue Sensi-tivity , and Affect-Cognition Coherence . Annotators are instructed to judge the entire interaction trajectory as a whole, while using the following definitions and score anchors as guidance. 

1. Profile Consistency 

Definition. This dimension evaluates whether the customer’s behavior, focus of attention, and purchase decisions remain con-sistent with the assigned profile. The key question is whether the customer is truly “acting as itself” according to its profile, rather than drifting into a different character. 

What to attend to. Compare the observed behavior against the profile’s decision style, shopping motivation, and personality traits. Pay attention to whether the customer makes choices that contradict its own preferences, priorities, or characteristic way of speaking and deciding. 

Score anchors. 

• 1 – Severely inconsistent / out-of-character. The behavior clearly contradicts the profile. For example, the customer focuses on factors that do not match its shopping motivation, makes purchase decisions at moments that do not fit its decision style, or speaks in a manner inconsistent with its assigned personality. From Vision to Interaction: Evaluating Dynamic Social Intelligence of VLM Agents in ALIVE-World MM ’26, November 10–14, 2026, Rio de Janeiro, Brazil 

• 3 – Broadly consistent. The behavior generally fits the assigned profile, but some decisions or speaking patterns appear generic, mechanical, or insufficiently individualized. 

• 5 – Fully consistent. The counterpart vividly embodies the assigned profile. Its moments of interest, hesitation, questioning, and purchase all strongly align with the lifestyle, decision style, and personality described in the profile. 

2. Behavioral Naturalness 

Definition. This dimension evaluates whether the customer’s physical behavior and shopping rhythm resemble those of a real human shopper. 

What to attend to. Focus on the ordering, pacing, and over-all plausibility of actions, including movement, browsing, asking questions, trying on clothes, waiting, comparing, and purchasing. Consider whether the trajectory feels like a natural shopping pro-cess rather than a rigid scripted procedure. 

Score anchors. 

• 1 – Mechanical / unnatural. The behavior appears highly robotic, odd, or implausible. The customer moves or acts in ways that feel stiff, abrupt, or inconsistent with ordinary human shop-ping behavior. 

• 3 – Reasonable but procedural. The behavior is broadly ac-ceptable, but it feels overly checklist-like or rigid, lacking the spontaneity, hesitation, or gradual realization often seen in real shopping. 

• 5 – Highly natural and human-like. The behavior is vivid and realistic, with smooth and coherent transitions among browsing, trying on, comparing, matching, and purchasing. 

3. Social Cue Sensitivity 

Definition. This dimension evaluates whether the customer responds to the seller as a normal social interactant would, and whether it shows appropriate sensitivity to both positive social value and social intrusion. 

What to attend to. Examine whether the customer meaning-fully reacts when the seller behaves notably well or poorly. Ex-amples include the seller being overly pushy, standing too close, behaving abnormally, failing to answer the customer’s question, or instead offering highly appropriate and appealing recommenda-tions. 

Score anchors. 

• 1 – Dull / overly compliant. The customer shows little or no reaction to highly oppressive selling, abnormal seller behavior, or exceptionally positive engagement. The seller’s behavior changes, but the customer’s response remains almost the same. 

• 3 – Ordinary responsiveness. The customer can understand the seller and respond appropriately at a basic level, but the emotional and behavioral feedback is relatively coarse. 

• 5 – Highly sensitive and graded feedback. The customer re-acts like a real person. It shows appropriate increases in attraction or purchase desire when positively engaged, and also displays defensiveness, dislike, interruption, withdrawal, or leaving be-havior when socially pressured or offended. 

4. Affect-Cognition Coherence 

Definition. This dimension evaluates whether the customer’s outward behavior is coherent with its immediately preceding in-ternal state, including both its profile-based predisposition and the emotion induced by recent interaction. 

What to attend to. Judge whether the transition from internal state to external action is psychologically and logically consistent. Pay particular attention to whether recent negative or positive experiences are naturally reflected in the next behavioral choice. 

Score anchors. 

• 1 – Clear disconnect between state and behavior. The cus-tomer’s action contradicts its immediately preceding state. For example, it appears dissatisfied, offended, or unconvinced, yet immediately continues to interact warmly, try on clothes, or pur-chase; or it appears highly engaged and interested, yet suddenly leaves without sufficient cause. 

• 3 – Surface-level coherence. The behavior is not obviously contradictory, but the transition from emotion and cognition to action feels somewhat abrupt, stitched together, or insufficiently grounded. 

• 5 – Strong affect-cognition coherence. The behavior is tightly aligned with the internal state, and changes in action follow nat-urally from prior emotion, cognition, and interaction experience.
