# Abstract 

Dense retrieval is increasingly used for biomedical literature search and retrieval-augmented generation (RAG). Most biomedical bi-encoders are built on BERT Base -sized encoders pretrained with masked language modeling (MLM). These encoders are costly for corpus-scale indexing and latency-sensitive query encoding, and MLM does not explicitly train the [CLS] vector that a bi-encoder uses to represent a whole text. We present Bioformer-RetroMAE , a compact (43M-parameter) retrieval-oriented biomedical encoder obtained by continually pretraining Bioformer 8L with RetroMAE on approximately 32 million PubMed titles and abstracts. After bi-encoder fine-tuning with BM25 hard negatives, we evaluated it on BioASQ Task 12B test questions by retrieving from all PubMed abstracts. Bioformer-RetroMAE 

outperformed BioBERT and PubMedBERT bi-encoders fine-tuned under identical settings on all metrics, despite having about 40% of their parameters and producing 512-dimensional rather than 768-dimensional vectors. Compared with PubMedBERT , it improved mean average precision (MAP) by 37.0% and Recall@100 by 29.1%. Compared with the original Bioformer 8L , RetroMAE pretraining improved MAP by 53.6% and Recall@100 by 32.8% without changing model size or inference cost. In hybrid retrieval, fusing Bioformer-RetroMAE with BM25 through weighted reciprocal rank fusion improved MAP and NDCG@10 over BM25 alone by 4.9% and 4.6%, respectively, larger gains than those obtained by fusing BM25 with PubMedBERT or BioBERT .

# 1 Introduction 

PubMed indexes more than 40 million citations and grows by several thousand articles each day. Retrieving relevant evidence from this collection underpins literature curation and evidence synthesis and, increasingly, retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), in which the outputs of large language models (LLMs) are grounded in retrieved documents to reduce hallucinations and make answers verifiable. Unlike offline literature curation, RAG retrieves evidence in real time while the user waits for a response, so retrieval is latency-sensitive. Consequently, the quality, speed and cost of the retrieval step directly affect the reliability and scalability of biomedical question-answering systems and literature-based assistants. Dense retrieval uses a bi-encoder to map queries and documents into a shared vector space and ranks documents by vector similarity [1]. Whereas lexical methods such as BM25 [2] rely on exact term overlap, dense retrieval can match semantically related texts that use different wording. Because the two approaches capture complementary relevance signals, they are often combined in hybrid retrieval, for example through reciprocal rank fusion (RRF) [3]. Biomedical dense retrievers, including MedCPT [4], are typically built 1arXiv:2611.19862v1 [cs.CL] 15 Nov 2026 on domain-specific encoders such as PubMedBERT [5], which adopt the BERT Base architecture (110M parameters) [6]. For retrieval, encoder size matters in two respects. Offline, every PubMed record (and, increasingly, chunks of PMC full-text articles) must be embedded, and the index must be refreshed as new articles appear. Online, every query must be encoded before search, often repeatedly in multi-hop or iterative RAG workflows, which places query encoding on the critical path of response latency [7, 8]. In addition, compact encoders typically have smaller hidden sizes and therefore produce lower-dimensional vectors, which reduce index memory and search cost. In previous work, we developed Bioformer , a family of compact biomedical BERT models pretrained from scratch on PubMed abstracts and PMC full-text articles with a dedicated biomedical vocabulary [9]. One member of this family, Bioformer 8L (8 layers, hidden size 512, 43M parameters), has approximately 40% of the parameters of BERT Base and runs 3 times faster at inference than BioBERT and PubMedBERT ,while achieving comparable accuracy on a range of biomedical NLP tasks [9]. Bioformer 8L has since been adopted as a backbone in biomedical named entity recognition and normalization tools such as GNorm2 [10], AIONER [11] and PhenoTagger [12]. These properties make Bioformer 8L an attractive candidate backbone for corpus-scale indexing and low-latency query encoding. However, Bioformer 8L , like BioBERT and PubMedBERT , was pretrained with masked language modeling (MLM). MLM predicts masked tokens from token-level representations and does not explicitly train the [CLS] vector that a bi-encoder uses to represent an entire text. Retrieval-oriented pretraining objectives are designed to address this mismatch. RetroMAE [13] pairs the encoder with a lightweight decoder that must reconstruct a heavily masked copy of the input from the encoder’s [CLS] vector alone, forcing this vector to capture the content of the full text. Because the decoder is discarded after pretraining, RetroMAE improves single-vector representations without increasing the size or inference cost of the encoder, making it particularly well suited to compact encoders. Here, we present Bioformer-RetroMAE , a compact retrieval-oriented biomedical encoder. We continually pretrained Bioformer 8L with RetroMAE on approximately 32 million PubMed titles and abstracts published through August 2026 and then fine-tuned it as a bi-encoder with BM25 hard negatives. In full-corpus retrieval over all PubMed abstracts on the BioASQ Task 12B golden test sets, we show that (i) 

Bioformer-RetroMAE outperforms BERT Base -sized biomedical bi-encoders ( BioBERT and PubMedBERT )fine-tuned under identical settings on all evaluation metrics, despite having about 40% of their parameters; (ii) RetroMAE continual pretraining substantially improves dense retrieval over the original MLM-pretrained Bioformer 8L ; and (iii) when BM25 is weighted more heavily in weighted RRF, fusing it with 

Bioformer-RetroMAE yields the strongest pairwise hybrid configuration and improves on BM25 alone in MAP, MAP@10 and NDCG@10 metrics. We release the model weights to support efficient biomedical literature search and RAG pipelines. 

# 2 Results 

2.1 Overview of Bioformer-RetroMAE 

Bioformer-RetroMAE was built in two stages (Figure 1; see Section 4). In the first stage, we continually pretrained the released Bioformer 8L checkpoint with RetroMAE on approximately 32 million PubMed records (title plus abstract) published through August 2026. In RetroMAE, the encoder reads a moderately masked input (masking probability 0.25), and its [CLS] vector is passed to a one-layer decoder that must reconstruct an independently and more aggressively masked version of the same text (masking probability 20.50). The training loss combines the encoder MLM loss and the decoder reconstruction loss with equal weights. The decoder is used only during pretraining, so the resulting encoder keeps the architecture, vocabulary and inference speed of Bioformer 8L (Table 1). In the second stage, we fine-tuned separate query and document encoders, both initialized from the RetroMAE-pretrained encoder, on BioASQ Task 12B training questions using an InfoNCE objective with one relevant article and 100 BM25 hard negatives per question. The baseline backbones were fine-tuned under exactly the same setting.

2.2 Dense retrieval performance 

We trained and evaluated the dense retrievers on BioASQ, a long-running community challenge on large-scale biomedical semantic indexing and question answering [14]. In its Task B, biomedical experts write English questions that reflect real information needs, and in Phase A, systems must retrieve the PubMed articles relevant to each question. We used Task 12B, the 2024 edition, which provides 5,049 training questions with annotated relevant articles, accumulated over previous editions, and 340 new test questions released in four official golden test batches. Because the questions are written by domain experts and the relevant articles must be found in the entire PubMed collection, Phase A closely reflects the full-corpus retrieval setting of biomedical literature search and RAG pipelines. We split the 5,049 training questions into 4,049 training and 1,000 validation questions. Each backbone (Bioformer-RetroMAE , the original Bioformer 8L , BioBERT and PubMedBERT ) was then fine-tuned as a bi-encoder with separate query and document encoders. For each training question, one relevant article served as the positive, and 100 BM25 hard negatives, highly ranked by BM25 but not annotated as relevant to any training or validation question, served as negatives. Because hard negatives share many terms with the question, they force the model to learn finer relevance distinctions than random negatives would. The model was optimized with an InfoNCE objective to score the positive above all hard negatives. All backbones were fine-tuned with identical training data, hyperparameters and checkpoint-selection criteria, and the checkpoint with the highest mean reciprocal rank on the validation questions was retained (Sections 4.3-4.4). We then evaluated the fine-tuned bi-encoders on the 340 test questions: for each question, each bi-encoder retrieved the top 1,000 articles from an index of all PubMed abstracts (Section 4.4). 2.3 Lexical and hybrid retrieval performance 

We next compared the dense retrievers with lexical retrieval. BM25 [2] is a classical lexical ranking function that scores a document by the query terms it contains, weighting each term by its frequency in the document and its rarity across the corpus and normalizing for document length. Despite its simplicity, BM25 remains a strong baseline that dense retrievers often fail to surpass outside their training domain, and BioASQ is among the benchmarks on which this advantage is most pronounced [15]. Consistent with this, BM25 alone achieved a MAP of 0.280, an NDCG@10 of 0.391 and a Recall@100 of 57.9% on the BioASQ Task 12B test questions (Table 3), substantially exceeding every dense retriever used alone (Table 2). Because dense retrieval matches texts by semantic similarity rather than exact term overlap, we asked whether it could nonetheless add complementary evidence to BM25. We combined BM25 with each dense retriever through weighted RRF and evaluated two settings of the BM25/dense weights (0.75/0.25 and 0.50/0.50). With BM25/dense weights of 0.75/0.25, every pairwise hybrid configuration outperformed BM25 alone in MAP, MAP@10 and NDCG@10, confirming that lexical and dense rankings provide complementary signals. Among these configurations, Bioformer-RetroMAE achieved the highest MAP (0.294), MAP@10 (0.295) and NDCG@10 (0.409), corresponding to relative gains of 4.9%, 6.9% and 4.6% over BM25, respectively. It also achieved the highest Hit@1 (46.5%, tied with Bioformer 8L ), Hit@100 (92.1%), Recall@10 (25.6%) and Recall@100 (58.6%), while its Hit@10 and Recall@1 differed only marginally from the best values. # 3 Discussion 

In this study, we developed Bioformer-RetroMAE , a compact retrieval-oriented biomedical encoder obtained by RetroMAE continual pretraining of Bioformer 8L on approximately 32 million PubMed records. With 43M parameters, about 40% of the size of BERT Base -sized biomedical encoders, it achieved the best dense retrieval performance on all evaluation metrics in full-corpus retrieval on the BioASQ Task 12B golden test sets. Its MAP and Recall@100 exceeded those of PubMedBERT by 37.0% and 29.1%, and those of the original Bioformer 8L by 53.6% and 32.8%. Its advantage carried over to hybrid retrieval: with BM25/dense weights of 0.75/0.25, fusing Bioformer-RetroMAE with BM25 produced the strongest pairwise hybrid configuration, improving MAP and NDCG@10 over BM25 alone by 4.9% and 4.6%. RetroMAE continual pretraining substantially improved the dense retrieval performance of Bioformer 8L .Before RetroMAE pretraining, the MLM-pretrained Bioformer 8L performed slightly below PubMedBERT 

as a bi-encoder backbone. After RetroMAE pretraining, it outperformed both BERT Base -sized baselines. This is consistent with the view that MLM leaves the [CLS] vector under-trained for retrieval, and that an objective which explicitly forces the [CLS] vector to reconstruct the text yields better single-vector representations. Because the RetroMAE decoder is discarded after pretraining, these gains come at no cost in inference speed or index size. The encoder retains the architecture of Bioformer 8L , which was previously shown to be approximately 3 times faster than BERT Base -sized models at inference [9], and its 512-dimensional vectors require one third less index memory than the 768-dimensional vectors of 

BERT Base -sized encoders. These properties are well suited to indexing tens of millions of PubMed records, to keeping the index up to date as new articles are published, and to latency-sensitive query encoding in interactive or iterative RAG workflows. A notable observation is that BM25 alone substantially outperformed every dense retriever on the BioASQ test sets (MAP of 0.280 versus at most 0.053, and NDCG@10 of 0.391 versus at most 0.100; Tables 2 and 3). This agrees with previous studies. In the BEIR benchmark, BM25 achieved an NDCG@10 6of 0.465 on BioASQ, exceeding all evaluated single-vector dense retrievers, including DPR (0.127), ANCE (0.306), TAS-B (0.383) and GenQ (0.398) [15]. Unsupervised domain adaptation narrowed but did not close this gap: even after adaptation with generative pseudo-labeling, the best dense model reached an NDCG@10 of 0.442 on the full BioASQ corpus, still below BM25 [16]. Similarly, the retriever component of MedCPT , although trained on large-scale PubMed search logs, achieved an NDCG@10 of 0.332 on BioASQ, and general-domain GTR retrievers with up to 4.8 billion parameters did not exceed 0.324 [4]. Several characteristics of BioASQ may explain the strength of lexical matching. The questions are written by biomedical experts and often contain specific, low-frequency terms, such as gene, protein, drug and disease names, for which exact term matching is highly discriminative. In addition, retrieval over tens of millions of PubMed abstracts requires dense retrievers to separate relevant articles from a very large number of semantically similar but non-relevant articles, which is challenging when only a few thousand training questions are available. Finally, the BioASQ gold standard itself may favor lexical retrieval. Relevant articles are first selected by biomedical experts from the results of keyword queries that they issue to PubMed through the BioASQ annotation tool; after each challenge round, the experts review the articles returned by the participating systems and enrich the gold standard with relevant articles identified by these systems [14, 17]. Because the expert queries are keyword-based and many systems participating in Task 12B Phase A used BM25 for retrieval [14], relevant articles that share few terms with the question are less likely to be found and annotated. Relevant articles retrieved only by a dense retriever may therefore be counted as non-relevant, which would underestimate dense retrieval performance relative to BM25. Because BM25 was much stronger than the dense retrievers, the benefit of hybrid retrieval depended on how the two components were weighted, consistent with previous analyses of fusion functions [18]. With equal weights (0.50/0.50), a document ranked first by the dense retriever alone received the same RRF contribution as the top-ranked document from BM25. Because the dense retrievers had low precision at the top ranks, non-relevant articles were promoted above relevant articles retrieved by BM25, and every pairwise hybrid configuration performed worse than BM25 alone (Table 3). Bioformer-RetroMAE showed the smallest declines in MAP ( −21.7%), MAP@10 ( −24.0%) and NDCG@10 ( −17.1%), compared with 22.5–31.9%, 24.3–40.5% and 18.6–30.5%, respectively, for the other bi-encoders. When the lexical signal was weighted more heavily (0.75/0.25), every pairwise configuration improved on BM25 in MAP, MAP@10 and NDCG@10, although not consistently in Hit@10 or Hit@100, and Bioformer-RetroMAE 

gave the largest improvements in these three metrics. Fusing BM25 with all four dense retrievers yielded the best overall MAP, MAP@10 and NDCG@10 and was insensitive to the weight setting, possibly because averaging over several dense rankings reduces the influence of errors made by any single model. In practice, these results suggest that a compact dense retriever is most useful as a complement to, rather than a replacement for, lexical retrieval in biomedical literature search, and that fusion weights should favor the stronger component. This study has several limitations. First, we evaluated retrieval on a single benchmark, BioASQ Task 12B, whose expert-written questions favor lexical matching; performance on other query types, such as natural-language clinical questions or queries generated within RAG pipelines, remains to be assessed. Second, we did not include MedCPT as a baseline because it was trained on large-scale PubMed search logs that are not publicly available, which would preclude a controlled comparison under identical training data; notably, MedCPT is itself initialized from PubMedBERT , which we included as a baseline. Third, the bi-encoders were fine-tuned only with BioASQ questions and BM25 hard negatives. Future work could use a cross-encoder as a teacher to train the bi-encoder through knowledge distillation; because a cross-encoder jointly encodes each query–document pair, it can provide more accurate relevance scores, 7and these soft labels could help the bi-encoder rank relevant articles higher while keeping the efficiency of dense retrieval. Larger and more diverse training data, such as weakly supervised pairs derived from PubMed titles and abstracts or citation links, may further narrow the gap between dense retrieval and BM25. Despite these limitations, Bioformer-RetroMAE provides an efficient, publicly available starting point for building dense and hybrid retrievers for biomedical search and RAG pipelines. 

# 4 Methods 

4.1 Base model 

We used the released Bioformer 8L checkpoint ( https://huggingface.co/bioformers/bioforme r-8L ) as the starting point. Bioformer 8L is a BERT encoder with 8 layers, a hidden size of 512, 8 attention heads and 43M parameters, using a cased biomedical WordPiece vocabulary of 32,768 tokens. It was pretrained from scratch with MLM and next sentence prediction objectives on all PubMed records available as of February 2021 and one million PMC full-text articles; details are given in [9]. The architecture and vocabulary were kept unchanged in this study. 

4.2 RetroMAE continual pretraining 

To improve the single-vector representations produced by Bioformer 8L for dense retrieval without increasing its model size, we conducted retrieval-oriented continual pretraining using the RetroMAE framework [13]. 

Pretraining corpus. We collected PubMed records published through August 2026 and excluded records without an abstract (i.e., title-only records) as well as records whose concatenated title and abstract contained fewer than 200 characters, yielding a corpus of approximately 32 million records. The title and abstract of each record were concatenated and treated as a single training instance. Instances were processed independently with dynamic padding and a maximum sequence length of 512 tokens, so that each [CLS] representation corresponded to one PubMed record. 

Objective. The encoder was initialized from the released Bioformer 8L checkpoint. Whole-word masking was applied to the encoder input with a masking probability of 0.25. A single-layer transformer decoder was used only during continual pretraining and was discarded before downstream retrieval. Given the final encoder [CLS] representation and an independently masked version of the input, the decoder reconstructed tokens masked at a probability of 0.50. The training objective was an equally weighted combination (0.5 each) of the encoder MLM loss and the decoder reconstruction loss. 

Optimization. The model was trained with AdamW using a learning rate of 5 × 10 −5, a weight decay of 0.01, cosine learning-rate decay and a warmup ratio of 0.10. The per-device batch size was 64, and gradients were accumulated over four steps, resulting in an effective batch size of 256. Training was performed on a single NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPU using BF16 mixed precision and TF32. Every 10,000 optimization steps, we evaluated the model on a held-out set of 10,000 PubMed records and monitored the total validation loss, encoder MLM loss, masked-token perplexity and decoder reconstruction loss. Based on validation-loss convergence, we selected the encoder checkpoint at step 600,000 (approximately 4.8 epochs) for downstream retrieval experiments; this checkpoint is released as Bioformer-RetroMAE .84.3 Bi-encoder fine-tuning with BioASQ hard negatives 

Training data. We fine-tuned the bi-encoders using a custom partition of 5,049 questions derived from BioASQ Task 12B [14]. The partition comprised 4,049 training questions and 1,000 validation questions. The training questions were associated with 36,783 relevant PubMed articles for which abstracts were available. 

Hard negatives. Hard negatives were mined from an index containing all PubMed abstracts. For each training question, BM25 [2] retrieved up to 10,000 articles, and the 200 highest-ranked unique PMIDs were retained as candidate negatives. To reduce false-negative contamination, we excluded the union of 43,109 articles annotated as relevant to any of the 5,049 training and validation questions. We then sampled up to 100 negatives per training question uniformly without replacement from the remaining candidates, using a deterministic question-specific random seed. Articles absent from the document store or having fewer than 50 characters in the concatenated title and abstract were excluded. This process yielded 403,568 question–negative pairs, corresponding to a mean of 99.67 negatives per training question. 

Model and loss. The bi-encoder used separate query and document encoders. For Bioformer-RetroMAE , both encoders were initialized from the encoder checkpoint obtained after 600,000 steps of RetroMAE pretraining; the baseline backbones were initialized from their corresponding pretrained checkpoints: BioBERT [19] from the cased BioBERT-Base v1.2 checkpoint ( https://huggingface.co /dmis-lab/biobert-base-cased-v1.2 ), PubMedBERT [5] from the uncased checkpoint pretrained on PubMed abstracts and PMC full-text articles ( https://huggingface.co/microsoft/BiomedN LP-BiomedBERT-base-uncased-abstract-fulltext ), and the original Bioformer 8L [9] from the released checkpoint described in Section 4.1. To ensure a fair comparison, all models were fine-tuned using the same training questions, relevant articles, BM25 hard negatives, bi-encoder architecture, optimization settings, validation candidate sets and checkpoint-selection criterion. The final-layer [CLS] hidden state was used as the representation and 𝐿 2-normalized, producing query and document vectors whose dimensionality equals the encoder hidden size (512 for the Bioformer 8L models and 768 for the 

BERT Base -sized baselines). Similarity was calculated as the inner product between normalized vectors and was therefore equivalent to cosine similarity. Each training instance contained one relevant article and 100 BM25 hard negatives. The model was optimized using the InfoNCE objective [20] with a temperature of 

𝜏 = 0.05. 

Optimization. Fine-tuning was conducted for 10 epochs using a learning rate of 3 × 10 −5, a weight decay of 0.01, linear learning-rate decay and a warmup ratio of 0.10. Each forward pass processed one question and its associated candidate documents, and gradients were accumulated over 16 question groups before each parameter update. Query and document inputs were limited to maximum lengths of 128 and 512 tokens, respectively. The negative candidates available to each question were reshuffled at the beginning of every epoch. For questions with fewer than 100 retained negatives, additional negatives were sampled with replacement from the available set. 

4.4 Validation and test settings 

We used different candidate collections for validation and testing. Validation was performed periodically on question-specific candidate sets, whereas testing was performed by retrieving from the complete indexed corpus. 

In-training validation. The validation partition contained 1,000 questions with 8,888 annotated 9relevant articles in total; individual questions could have multiple relevant articles. For each validation question, one relevant article was used as the positive candidate and ranked against up to 100 question-specific BM25 hard negatives. These fixed candidate sets enabled efficient validation every 250 optimization steps. We monitored mean reciprocal rank (MRR), calculated as the reciprocal rank of the selected relevant article, and Top-1 accuracy, defined as the proportion of questions for which it ranked first. 

Full-corpus test evaluation. The checkpoint with the highest validation MRR was selected for test evaluation. The test set contained 340 questions from the four official BioASQ Task 12B golden test batches, and each question was evaluated against an index of all PubMed abstracts. We considered three retrieval settings; results of dense retrieval are reported in Table 2, and those of lexical and hybrid retrieval in Table 3: 1. Lexical retrieval : BM25 retrieved the top 1,000 documents for each question from the same index and served as the lexical baseline. Duplicate PMIDs in the BM25 output were removed, retaining the first occurrence. 2. Dense retrieval : We encoded all documents into dense vectors (512-dimensional for the Bioformer 8L 

models; 768-dimensional for the BERT Base -sized baselines) and stored them in a FAISS [21] IndexFlatIP index. For each question, the query encoder retrieved the 1,000 documents with the highest inner-product scores. 3. Weighted reciprocal rank fusion (RRF) : To combine lexical and dense retrieval without directly calibrating their scores, we merged the BM25 and dense ranked lists using weighted RRF [3].
