# Leveraging Binary Hashing for Scalable Index Assignment in Semantic Communications

Abstract —Transmitting semantically structured embedding vectors over digital channels is costly in bit rate, while conven-tional vector quantization approaches face limitations in scalabil-ity, encoding complexity, and index assignment. This paper inves-tigates binary hashing techniques from the information retrieval literature as semantic indexing strategies for general-purpose networks. We study random Locality-Sensitive Hashing (LSH) along with Principal Component Analysis (PCA)-, Canonical Correlation Analysis (CCA)-, and ITQ (Iterative Quantization)-based variants in a semantic communication pipeline over a Binary Symmetric Channel. Embeddings are compressed into binary codes, reconstructed through Hamming retrieval from a shared table, and evaluated on downstream classification. Ex-periments on the Massive Text Embedding Benchmark (MTEB) and the Banking77 dataset show that data-dependent hashing improves upon random LSH, while ITQ refinement increases robustness to channel noise. Among the evaluated methods, CCA-ITQ achieves the best classification accuracy across the tested channel conditions. These results indicate that binary hashing is an efficient and scalable alternative to quantization-based embedding compression for semantic communications. 

Index Terms —Semantic Communications, Binary Hashing, Semantic Compression, Vector Quantization, Index Assignment 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

The increasing number of connected devices and the expo-nential growth of data traffic have motivated a paradigm shift in communication system design. Classical general-purpose networks focus on the reliable reconstruction of transmitted bits at the receiver, irrespective of their semantic content. In contrast, semantic communications aim to convey only the information relevant to a specific task, thereby reducing the communication overhead by exploiting the meaning of the transmitted data [1]. Recent works have further highlighted the relevance of semantic integration for future practical networks and proposed efficient end-to-end learning architectures that jointly optimize source and channel coding for task-oriented scenarios [2]. As an emerging perspective, semantic communications can be viewed as exchanging information in a form created by

and intended for Artificial Intelligence (AI) models rather than as raw source data. Under this AI-native view, embeddings constitute a natural and particularly suitable data format for enabling semantic system design [3]. Embeddings are high-dimensional, structured vector representations that capture the semantic characteristics of the underlying data. In Natural Language Processing (NLP) in particular, pre-trained sen-tence embedding models have become a standard tool for representing text in a latent semantic space [4]. However, transmitting raw embeddings is prohibitively expensive in bandwidth-constrained scenarios: a single embedding vector of dimension d = 768 , encoded in IEEE 754 single-precision floating-point format, requires 768 × 32 = 24 ,576 bits. This motivates the need for mapping embeddings to compact binary representations that reduce the bit rate while remaining robust to channel noise, so that semantic similarity is sufficiently preserved for the downstream task. Compressing embeddings for digital transmission typically involves two steps: quantizing the continuous embedding space into a finite set of discrete vector realizations, and adapting this set of representations for a communication system by assigning a binary index to each discrete realization. One of the fundamental problems in this second step is the Index Assignment Problem (IAP) [5]. Given a set of possible discrete vectors, the objective is to assign binary codewords to each of them such that a global distortion metric, for example, the distortion induced by channel errors, is minimized. Within this quantization-and-indexing framework, existing approaches largely rely on vector quantization (VQ) tech-niques [6], [7]. These methods first minimize quantization dis-tortion by learning a finite set of representative vectors for the continuous embedding space, and then define a binary index mapping that preserves a form of error locality between the Hamming space and the original embedding space, as in the Binary Switching Algorithm (BSA) [8]. While this approach is well studied and locally optimal within the quantization framework, it suffers from three fundamental limitations. First, it introduces an irreducible quantization distortion for any fixed codebook size K: reconstruction vectors are artificial centroids that may not correspond to real embeddings, and the reconstruction error cannot be reduced without increas-arXiv:2611.21260v1 [eess.SP] 16 Nov 2026 ing K. Second, assigning the nearest centroid index during transmission requires an encoding complexity that grows as 

O(Kd ), making exhaustive nearest centroid search intractable for large K. Third, for performing BSA optimization, the binary mapping refinement incurs a complexity that still grows as O(K2). Scaling K to approach the expressiveness of the full training set is therefore computationally prohibitive. There is therefore a need for binary embedding representations that preserve the robustness objectives of index assignment while remaining scalable to large embedding collections. To address this need, we propose to leverage binary hashing techniques from the information retrieval (IR) literature as a scalable compression framework for semantic communications [9]. Specifically, we investigate Locality-Sensitive Hashing (LSH) [10], a family of methods that generates binary codes by thresholding projections of embeddings so that similar vectors remain close in Hamming space (see Section III-B). We then consider its unsupervised and supervised refinements based on Principal Component Analysis (PCA) [11], Canonical Corre-lation Analysis (CCA) [12], and Iterative Quantization (ITQ) [13] as binary encoding schemes for embedding transmission over a Binary Symmetric Channel (BSC), where embeddings are encoded directly into binary codes at lower complexity and reconstructed at the receiver by Hamming retrieval from a shared table instead of nearest-centroid quantization. The main contributions of this paper are threefold: 1) we propose, to the best of our knowledge, the first application of IR binary hashing techniques as dedicated compression schemes for semantic communications, providing a scalable alternative to vector quantization approaches; 2) we bench-mark these techniques against uncompressed embeddings and a BSA baseline in a practical semantic communication pipeline over a BSC, across a wide range of bit rates and channel crossover probabilities; 3) we show that PCA- and CCA-based hyperplane selection, refined by ITQ, consistently improves over random LSH, with CCA exploiting label information to better encode the embedding semantic structure that retains classification-relevant information. A. General Framework 

All schemes share the affine projection structure

W ∈ Rb×d is the projection matrix and μ is the training mean vector used to center the observed data as a preprocessing step. Geometrically, each row of W defines a hyperplane in the centered embedding space, and the binary code records on which side of each hyperplane the embedding lies. The methods differ in how W is obtained: from no data (LSH), from the unlabeled embedding distribution (PCA), or from the joint embedding-label distribution (CCA). The ITQ variants additionally refine the projection with an orthogonal rotation to balance and calibrate bit assignments. 

B. Classical LSH 

The classical random-projection LSH [10] encodes an em-bedding by testing its position relative to a set of random hyperplanes, as illustrated in Fig. 2. Each row of W is drawn i.i.d. from the Gaussian distribution N (0 , I d), and the sign of the corresponding projection determines one bit of the code. The final binary representation is obtained by concatenating these b sign bits. Because nearby vectors are more likely to fall on the same side of many hyperplanes, they tend to produce similar binary strings and remain close in Hamming space. Since the projection directions are sampled at random and do not depend on the dataset, random LSH is defined as a data-independent hashing scheme.

A. Experimental Setup 

Dataset and embedding model. Experiments are conducted on Banking77 1, a banking intent classification dataset with 

Ntrain =10 ,003 training samples, Ntest =3 ,080 test samples, and 77 intent classes. All dataset splits are embedded using the tomaarsen/mpnet-base-nli 2 model, which produces d=768 -dimensional sentence representations and is used as a general-purpose semantic encoder pre-trained on a larger set of NLP tasks without task-specific fine-tuning [14]. 

Evaluation protocol. Embeddings are standardized using a zero-mean, unit-variance scaler fitted on the training split. Fol-lowing the MTEB protocol [4], each run samples s=8 training examples per class, yielding 616 balanced training samples for Banking77. A logistic regression classifier is trained on this subset using uncompressed embeddings and then evaluated at the receiver on reconstructed test embeddings. The procedure is repeated over n=10 random seeds, and reported accuracies are averaged across runs. 

Baselines. We compare against three baselines: (i) Uncom-pressed (IEEE 754), which transmits the raw d=768 float32 

embedding ( 24 576 bits per embedding); (ii) K-means+BSA ,which quantizes embeddings into K centroids and assigns 

b= log 2 K binary indices with BSA; (iii) PQ+BSA , follow-ing [7], which applies Product Quantization (PQ) with M sub-spaces and K centroids per subspace, yielding b=M log 2 K

bits. Since BSA scales as O(K2) per iteration, large K-means codebooks are impractical; in this work, we identify and define 

K=256 (b=8 ) as the practical ceiling on Banking77. PQ+BSA extends VQ to larger bit budgets with encoding complexity 

O(Kd ), at the cost of subspace quantization distortion that ignores cross-subspace correlations. For all BSA-based schemes, we run up to 200 iterations per round and keep the best mapping among 5 initializations. The current best assignment is then perturbed by 8 random permu-tations, each followed by BSA refinement, and the procedure stops after 4 consecutive rounds without improvement. BSA distortion evaluation uses a fixed error probability p=0 .05 .

Bit budgets and channel parameters. For accuracy evaluation, hashing schemes are tested with b ∈ {16 , 32 , 48 , 64 , 80 , 96 , 112 , 128 } and 

p ∈ {0, 10 −3, 10 −2, 0.1}. Across the same p-range, K-means+BSA is evaluated over b = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8}, and PQ+BSA uses M =16 subspaces with b = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8}

bits per subspace, yielding comparable total bit budgets. Reconstruction MSE is evaluated only for p=0 to isolate compression-induced distortion.

V. C OMPLEXITY AND SCALABILITY DISCUSSION 

To clarify the practical scalability trade-offs underlying the performance trends observed in Section IV, we analyze the computational complexity of the compression schemes across the two main stages of the studied pipeline: offline construction and online operation. 

A. Offline Construction Complexity 

For random LSH, building the binary encoder only requires sampling b random projection vectors in Rd, yielding a com-plexity of O(bd ) [10]. PCA-based hashing requires estimating principal projection directions from the training embeddings. In a standard dense implementation, the dominant cost comes from covariance formation and eigendecomposition, yielding a complexity on the order of O(N d 2 + d3) [11], [15]. CCA-based hashing similarly relies on second-order statistics and a generalized eigenvalue problem involving the label space, leading to a comparable but typically heavier offline cost [12]. ITQ further adds an alternating orthogonal refinement on the projected N × b codes, whose complexity scales with the number of refinement iterations [13]. For the VQ-based baselines, classic K-means training over 

K centroids costs O(IkN Kd ) over Ik iterations. The sub-sequent index refinement by BSA or related code-swapping procedures is substantially more demanding, since it explores pairwise code reassignment operations over the codebook and leads to a complexity that grows as O(K2) per distortion-evaluation round [8]. More generally, optimal index assign-ment is known to be an NP-hard quadratic assignment problem [5]. PQ reduces the dimensionality handled by each sub-quantizer and affords more scalable bit rates, but still requires codebook learning in all M subspaces with index refinement. 

B. Online Operation Complexity 

All hashing-based schemes share the same online structure: a d-dimensional embedding is projected onto b hyperplanes and thresholded componentwise. Their online encoding com-plexity is therefore O(bd ), independently of the training set size N . This applies to random LSH, PCA-Direct, PCA-ITQ, CCA-Direct, and CCA-ITQ. By contrast, K-means+BSA requires finding the nearest cen-troid among K = 2 b representatives, which under exhaustive search yields O(Kd ). For PQ+BSA, each of the M sub-vectors is matched against a sub-codebook of size K, resulting in an overall complexity of order O(M K (d/M )) = O(Kd ).For the proposed hashing pipeline, decoding consists of finding the nearest populated binary cell in Hamming space among the entries of the shared table T . Since computing one Hamming distance between two b-bit strings costs O(b), brute-force retrieval over |T | populated cells requires O(|T | b), with 

|T | ≤ N . The reconstruction is then a simple table lookup. For K-means+BSA, decoding is a direct centroid lookup from the received index and therefore costs O(1) . PQ+BSA similarly reconstructs the embedding by concatenating the retrieved sub-centroids from the M shared subcodebooks, yielding then a decoding cost of order O(M ).

Discussion. Regarding the computational trade-off, hashing-based schemes shift complexity toward receiver-side retrieval, while keeping the transmitter complexity low and scalable, with online encoding growing only linearly in the bit budget 

b. In contrast, VQ-based schemes offer very simple decoding once the binary index is received, while incurring heavier offline construction and online encoding costs due to codebook learning, nearest-centroid assignment, and index refinement. In the Banking77 setting, this difference is reflected by the practical operating points observed in Fig. 3. With d = 768 and 

b = 128 , hashing-based encoding scales as bd = 128 × 768 ≈

9.8 × 10 4, whereas K-means encoding at the practical BSA limit K = 256 already scales as Kd = 256 ×768 ≈ 2.0×10 5,and would rise to about 3.1 × 10 6 for K = 4096 (b = 12 ). Hashing therefore provides a substantially lighter transmitter at large bit budgets, at the cost of a receiver-side Hamming search over at most |T | ≤ Ntrain = 10 ,003 populated cells. These orders of magnitude are consistent with the experimental trends: hashing methods remain scalable as b increases and achieve the best overall accuracy–scalability trade-off in our evaluation, especially with CCA-ITQ. VI. C ONCLUSION 

This paper investigated binary hashing as a semantic com-pression strategy for transmitting sentence embeddings over a BSC. Experiments on Banking77 showed that data-dependent hashing improves upon random LSH, that ITQ refinement increases robustness under channel noise and that CCA-ITQ achieves the best task results across the tested settings. The complexity analysis further highlighted the favorable scaling of hashing-based encoding compared with VQ-based strate-gies, despite a more demanding receiver-side retrieval stage. These findings are limited to a single embedding model and a single downstream task, and broader experiments are needed to assess their generality across a wider range of settings. Future work will therefore consider additional embedding models, larger and more diverse datasets, and other down-stream tasks, including multi-task scenarios. Further directions include accelerated Hamming retrieval at the receiver for large or continuously evolving embedding collections and extensions beyond text classification to other modalities, such as image or multimodal embeddings.
