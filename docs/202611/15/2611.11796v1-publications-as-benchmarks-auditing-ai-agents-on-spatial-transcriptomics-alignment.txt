Abstract 

AI agents are increasingly automating data analysis, but responsible adoption requires reli-able performance evaluation, which is costly in specialized scientific domains. We introduce a framework that translates scientific publications into auditable benchmarks for AI agents and demonstrate its use on spatial transcriptomics alignment tasks. Across 121 tasks, 16 baseline methods, and six AI agents, the best agents (GPT-6-Astra and Gemini-4-Argon) matched or outperformed all baselines on 80% of tasks, often using surprisingly simple heuristics. Agent performance was robust to prompting and access to pre-installed packages. Benchmark con-struction also uncovered errors in prior work and shortcuts in current evaluation metrics. Finally, we show that agent-generated solutions can be distilled into a reusable program that ranks in the top 10% of baseline methods on unseen data. Our framework offers a scalable way to evaluate AI agents in specialized scientific domains by leveraging and stress-testing existing papers and metrics. Advances in technology across scientific disciplines have generated “big data” and increasing de-mands for sophisticated computational tools that can make sense of and uncover insights from these data [1, 2]. For instance, in the biological sciences, high-throughput omics technologies have revolution-ized the field over the past decades and catalyzed numerous computational innovations [3, 4]. Recent development of large language models (LLMs) is rapidly changing the potential solution space: artificial intelligence (AI) agents such as Claude Code, Codex and Gemini CLI can read and modify a codebase, install packages, run experiments, make plots and even write a complete manuscript from a short natu-ral language instruction [5–10]. The appeal is direct for computational scientists, as agents could turn a complex request such as “integrate these heterogeneous data sources to build a performant model” into executable code in minutes rather than weeks. However, responsible adoption requires careful evaluation of the solutions of agents, and increasing automation comes with more hidden risks, from hallucinated priors and facts and misinterpreted user intents to more nuanced selective reporting and test data leakage [15]. Adoption is further burdened by the simple fact that evaluating AI agents on scientific tasks is hard. On the agent side, unlike static supervised learning methods, agents interact with tools, the local environment and very often the open internet, and they can see and change their working environment so that they can explore and automate. If an answer key or a scorer is reachable, the agent may find and use it [16–18]. On the content side, broad scientific tasks have been assembled into suites of test cases, referred to as “benchmarks”, that are increasingly used to evaluate frontier AI agents [19–24]. Many existing ef-forts, however, focus on code generation and bug fixing and rely on automated tests as the evaluation metric [25–27], whereas scientific work is often more iterative and open-ended than most existing bench-marks: tasks are rarely precisely defined, success is multi-dimensional, and ground truth is often am-biguous and expensive to obtain. Moreover, there is an increasing demand to interpret and synthesize outputs from black-box models such as AI agents, so that future analysis and modeling of scientific data do not depend critically on proprietary AI models [28, 29], and the insights can be reused in other scientific domains [30–32]. Motivated by these challenges, we propose a framework that leverages academic publications, especially benchmarking studies, to build auditable scientific benchmarks for AI agents. Academic publications, espe-cially those featuring computational methods, are an underused source for evaluating agent performance. Good computational papers routinely identify public datasets, a clear input and output for each task, the scores used to rank existing and proposed methods and the code that computes those scores, which are many of the key ingredients of a scalable agent benchmark. Recent work has treated papers as executable objects in other ways: PaperBench asks agents to replicate machine learning papers from scratch [33], Paper2Agent converts a publication and its codebase into an agent that exposes the paper’s methods as callable tools [34], NatureBench distills 90 tasks from Nature-family publications to test whether agents surpass reported state-of-the-art performance [35], and SpatialBench introduces 146 verifiable problems drawn from spatial biology workflows, focusing on normalization, differential expression, and spatial domain clustering [36]. However, existing benchmarks typically grade outputs against static reference values reported in the original papers rather than re-evaluating published baselines and agents under a unified scoring harness, examining whether common evaluation metrics faithfully reflect ground truth, 

or auditing how agents arrive at their solutions. Importantly, we use publications not as replication targets, but as blueprints for executable, domain-specific evaluation suites: agents receive sanitized inputs while ground truth remains strictly withheld, and published methods are re-executed on the exact same data to provide a rigorous baseline reference. We demonstrate the use of our framework in spatial transcriptomics alignment, an increasingly com-mon step in understanding tissue biology in three dimensions. Spatial transcriptomics measures gene expression in biological samples with two-dimensional spatial resolution [37, 38]. There is great inter-est in aligning samples collected from adjacent tissue sections, or even from the same tissue measured with a different technology [39, 40]: integrating these samples allows a deeper understanding of com-plementary modalities, such as RNA, protein and chromatin, and of spatial organization, variation and other cellular phenotypes in three dimensions [13, 41]. Alignment, often called registration, also has wide applications in other domains, including functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) and com-puted tomography (CT) [42–44]. Although numerous alignment methods have been proposed [45–56], a comprehensive benchmark to guide their application was until recently absent. SABench addressed this by systematically evaluating a diverse suite of leading methods on 295 alignment tasks across diverse datasets and technologies, measuring accuracy, efficiency, usability and robustness, and the downstream impact of alignment quality [11], and the GEASO study compared methods for alignment, stitching and three-dimensional reconstruction of large sets of sections [14]. Spatial transcriptomics alignment provides an ideal testbed for auditing scientific AI agents. Nu-merous domain-specific methods have been benchmarked on standardized datasets such as the human DLPFC [12] and the whole-mouse-brain MERFISH atlas [13], providing well-characterized human base-lines. Moreover, these datasets span tasks with ground-truth coordinates alongside tasks evaluated indi-rectly via cell-type labels or gene expression similarity, enabling a direct test of how metric choice shapes evaluation outcomes. Here we translate these two alignment studies into 121 auditable tasks and evaluate six frontier AI models operating as coding agents across basic and domain-enriched prompting conditions (Figure 1). Rebuilding tasks and re-executing 16 baseline methods under a single scoring harness also audits the source benchmarks—uncovering a 20-fold unit mismatch in a published evaluation—and demonstrates the necessity of strict container isolation to prevent filesystem leakage. Across tasks with known posi-tions, the best agent run matched or outperformed all published baselines on 22 of 28 tasks (79%) ,whereas single runs did so on 21–64% (Figure 2). Providing pre-installed alignment packages and lit-3erature recommendations shifted agent workflows and increased cost without consistently improving accuracy (Figure 3). Crucially, evaluating alignments without ground truth remains a major bottleneck :label-based metrics tracked true spatial error far better than expression similarity metrics (Figure 4), and the algorithmic steps associated with top scores depended on the evaluation metric (Figure 5). Finally, distilling agent runs into a standalone deterministic program outperformed 87% of baselines on held-out tasks at zero LLM cost, while transferring less effectively to unseen task families (Figure 6). 

## Methods 

Task construction 

Our benchmark comprises 121 tasks, each requiring an agent to register two or more spatial transcrip-tomics sections into a common coordinate system. Tasks were compiled using Paper2Bench, an auto-mated pipeline that translates published benchmarks and methods papers into executable task packages. The pipeline extracts experimental units from figures, tables, methods, code, and public repositories; at-taches deterministic scoring profiles; generates task prompts without exposing held-out evaluation data; and constructs isolated agent workspaces. Candidate tasks must pass a suite of deterministic validation checks before inclusion (Supplementary Note 1). The 121 tasks span five task families derived from three sources: serial tissue sections, a primary motor cortex (MOp) series, and cross-platform section pairs from SABench [11]; rotation, cropping, and three-dimensional reconstruction tasks from GEASO [14] and the whole-mouse-brain MERFISH atlas; and synthetic tissues generated with the Albis simulator [57] (Supplementary Tables 1, 2 and 3). Ground-truth spatial coordinates are available for 32 tasks and cell-type or anatomical region annotations for 69 tasks, while the remaining tasks are evaluated using gene expression similarity. For each task, the agent receives sanitized h5ad files containing only the expression matrix, feature metadata, anonymized observation identifiers, and spatial coordinates. The agent outputs one CSV table of aligned (x, y) coordinates per section, with the reference section retaining its original positions. 

Published methods and baselines 

We re-executed 13 published spatial alignment packages—CAST [53], GEASO [14], GPSA [47], moscot [55], PASTE [45], PASTE2 [46], SANTO [54], SLAT [48], SPACEL [51], Spateo [52], STAIR [56], STalign [49], and STAligner [50]—across 41 parameter configurations on the same task inputs [11, 14] and the simulated tis-sue suite [57]. The execution was assisted by coding agents and verified by human inspection and checks to make sure the reproduced values are close to prior reported values whenever possible [11, 14]. On each task, the best published baseline is defined as the top-scoring configuration across all packages. We also evaluated three geometric baselines across all 121 tasks: an identity baseline (unaligned coordinates) and similarity transformations estimated by principal component analysis (PCA) and iterative closest point (ICP) registration. Software versions, parameter configurations, and comparisons with originally published values are detailed in Supplementary Note 2 and Supplementary Figure 1. 

Agents, prompting conditions, and execution harness 

We evaluated six frontier LLMs through their respective command-line coding interfaces: Claude Code (claude-opus-5.5 and claude-sonnet-5.5 ), Codex CLI ( gpt-6-astra and gpt-5.5 ), and Gemini CLI (gemini-4-argon and gemini-3.5-flash ) (Supplementary Table 4). All agents were executed in non-interactive mode with permission prompts bypassed, no custom system instructions, and a clean envi-ronment per run. Our primary analysis mimics a low-overhead setting for scientists, referred to as the basic prompt condition: the workspace contained only the input data, task specification, and a standard 4scientific Python environment ( numpy , scipy , pandas , scikit-learn , and h5py ), without any domain-specific alignment packages installed or referenced. We assessed the sensitivity of agent performance under the rich-context condition, where published alignment packages were pre-installed in isolated en-vironments and the prompt was augmented with usage guidelines, API examples, package documenta-tion, and literature-derived recommendations summarizing which tool families performed best for each data modality in prior benchmarks (Supplementary Note 3). Every run executed inside an isolated Singularity container [58] with read-only software and data mounts, a single writable workspace directory, and no access to held-out evaluation files. Primary anal-yses use the first completed, scored run for each model, task, and prompting condition. Run cost was recorded as the API-equivalent expenditure reported by Claude Code and calculated from token usage at standard API list prices for OpenAI and Google models. 

Leakage prevention and auditing 

Workspace inputs were constructed using a strict allowlist that stripped cell-type annotations, ground-truth coordinates, and auxiliary metadata, while anonymizing cell identifiers and randomly permuting row order. Following each run, an automated audit scanned the execution trace, workspace files, and sub-mitted outputs for references to restricted filesystem paths, withheld metadata field names, or credentials (Supplementary Note 4, Supplementary Figure 2 and Supplementary Table 5). 

Evaluation metrics and scoring 

Scoring was executed outside the Singularity container after agent termination across all designated sec-tion pairs in each task, as preliminary pilots showed that exposing the scorer inside the workspace led agents to inspect or modify the scoring function. Coordinate error was quantified as the mean absolute error (MAE) between submitted and ground-truth coordinates. Label agreement was evaluated on spa-tially matched cells across aligned sections using nearest-neighbor label matching accuracy, anatomical region overlap (alpha-shape intersection over union), and mutual nearest-neighbor (MNN) label con-cordance [11, 14]. Expression agreement was quantified on a spatial grid over the overlap region using gene-wise Pearson correlation, cosine similarity, and structural similarity (SSIM). Each task’s primary metric was coordinate error when ground-truth positions were available and the composite agreement score (mean of expression and label metrics) otherwise. Formal metric definitions and numerical parity checks against the original SABench codebase are provided in Supplementary Note 5. 

Strategy distillation and cross-validation 

To test whether agent-discovered workflows could be consolidated into a standalone algorithm, we per-formed five-fold cross-validation under both random task splits and task-family holdouts. For each fold, an isolated instance of Claude Code ( claude-opus-5.5 ) was provided with the final scripts and training scores of claude-opus-5.5 and gpt-6-astra from the four training folds and instructed to synthesize a single, general-purpose Python alignment program. The resulting program was then executed on the held-out fold without any LLM calls and graded by the benchmark scorer, with runtime crashes or invalid outputs scored as failures (Supplementary Note 3). 

Statistical analysis 

We partitioned variance in alignment accuracy, runtime, and cost across task, model, prompt condition, and replicate runs using factorial analysis of variance (ANOVA). A run was considered to match or out-perform the best published baseline if its primary score equaled or exceeded the highest score achieved by any published package configuration on that task. Confidence intervals and hypothesis tests were computed via cluster bootstrap resampling (Supplementary Note 6). 5Execution trace analysis 

Agent execution logs were parsed into sequential tool-call steps and classified into eight operational phases (such as data inspection, method exploration, candidate evaluation, and output validation), along-side 48 fine-grained algorithmic tags applied to both intermediate scripts and final submitted code (Sup-plementary Note 7 and Supplementary Figures 3 and 4). 

## Results 

Translating published benchmarks into auditable agent tasks 

We compiled executable agent tasks from two spatial transcriptomics alignment studies: SABench [11] and GEASO [14] (Figure 1a). Each component of a source paper maps to a machine-readable task card: dataset tables specify input sections, experimental protocols define section pairings and geometric per-turbations, evaluation code defines the scorer, and benchmarked tools provide the reference baselines (Figure 1b). During execution, an agent observes only sanitized gene expression matrices, spatial co-ordinates, task instructions, and the required output schema, while ground-truth coordinates, cell-type annotations, and scoring scripts remain hidden. We reconstructed 98% of the tasks used in SABench (source data are not directly available for down-load for the remainder) and verified that our scorer matched the authors’ evaluation results to within 10 −7

(Supplementary Note 1). After grouping simulated tissues from the same source section and serial align-ments within the same dataset, our benchmark spans 121 tasks across 19 datasets and five task families (Figure 1c): cross-platform section pairs, serial sections, simulated tissues with known positions, geomet-ric stress tests (rotations, crops, and partial overlaps), and three-dimensional reconstructions of up to 129 sections. Rebuilding the published evaluations from raw inputs also uncovered a 20-fold coordinate unit mismatch between ground-truth and input positions in the published evaluation of the Drosophila embryo stack (Supplementary Note 1). Figure 1d illustrates a representative three-dimensional reconstruction task requiring registration of 129 scrambled MERFISH sections of a mouse hemibrain across six evaluated frontier coding agents. The top published baseline on this task, SPACEL, left a mean coordinate error of 846 spatial units, whereas 

gpt-6-astra reduced the error to 0.13 in 30 tool calls (32 minutes; $2.91). Trace inspection shows that the agent chained pairwise rigid alignments across adjacent sections, detected cumulative rotational drift along the stack, and enforced a global constraint along the straight medial boundary of the hemibrain. Notably, however, gene-expression-based evaluation metrics ranked SPACEL equal to this near-exact re-construction, illustrating a potential discrepancy between proxy scores and coordinate-based scores. 

Preventing benchmark leakage through container isolation 

In an initial pilot where evaluation scripts and ground-truth files resided on the shared filesystem outside the workspace—even with prompts explicitly instructing agents not to search parent directories—trace audits revealed four modes of test leakage (Supplementary Table 5). Agents traversed the filesystem to copy ground-truth coordinate files, inspected or modified the scoring script, reused outputs from concur-rent runs, or extracted precomputed results bundled with cloned method repositories. We therefore isolated every run inside the Singularity container (Figure 1a, step 3) with read-only mounts and no access to held-out data or scoring code, followed by an automated post-run trace and workspace audit (Supplementary Figure 2). The best AI agents consistently matched or outperformed state-of-the-art spatial alignment methods, achieving lower error on 79% of tasks with ground-truth positions (Figure 2a). Across all 118 tasks evalu-ated by expression agreement, the best agent matched or beat the top published method on 57% of tasks, driven by strong gains on cross-platform pairs (41 of 45; Figure 2b), with robust win rates across other expression and label metrics (Supplementary Figure 1e). Among AI agents, however, performance varied substantially by model version, falling roughly into three tiers: gpt-6-astra and gemini-4-argon ; followed by claude-opus-5.5 , gpt-5.5 , and 

claude-sonnet-5.5 ; and finally gemini-3.5-flash . Stronger models also produced more reproducible results across replications , as they were more likely to converge on the same solution strategies (Fig-ure 2c,d), with rank correlations across runs of ρ = 0.63, 0.71–0.74 (0.13–0.14), and 0.93 (0.10) for 

gemini-3.5-flash , the Claude models, and gpt-6-astra , respectively (Supplementary Figure 5 and Supplementary Note 8). 

Reproduction of published baselines 

Benchmarking the 16 published baselines showed that no single baseline method dominated across every tissue regime (Supplementary Note 2 and Supplementary Figure 1a–d). Pooled across all task families, SLAT led overall, followed by Spateo and CAST. This largely agrees with previous benchmark results [11], with the caveat that the best method varied by regime: SLAT led on serial sections and on simulated mis-alignment due to rotation and cropping, Spateo performed best on the motor cortex series, CAST on cross-platform pairs, and STalign on simulated tissues. Interestingly, custom agent solutions often dra-matically reduced coordinate error in large-scale three-dimensional serial alignment, on both simulated data and a 129-section mouse brain reconstruction, frequently by applying global refinement after serial alignment (Figure 2e). 

Domain-specific context makes agents work longer and explore more without improving ac-curacy 

With minimal prompting, 94% of agent runs solved alignments from first principles, writing custom code to solve the optimization problem rather than calling existing bioinformatics packages (Figure 3). Under the rich-context prompt, where we provided the problem context and commonly used packages, 89% of runs executed a published package and 54% retained one in their final submission. Despite this shift in tool adoption, accuracy did not consistently improve. Mean changes in within-task percentile ranged from +11.5 (95% CI: 2.0 to 18.3) to −6.4 points (95% CI: −13.3 to 0.6) for 

claude-opus-5.5 and gemini-4-argon , respectively. This is largely because package-based submis-sions did not consistently outperform custom code. Token usage and cost, on the other hand, consistently rose, averaging 1.5 × higher overall (Figure 3c). Models also differed in how they adopted external tools: 

claude-opus-5.5 and claude-sonnet-5.5 were much more likely than other models to retain custom code even after trying bioinformatics packages (Figure 3d,f). 

Label-based metrics track spatial coordinate accuracy more reliably than expression similarity 

In most experimental applications, ground-truth positions are unavailable, and alignments must be eval-uated using proxy metrics based on cell-type labels or gene expression similarity [11] (Figure 4a). Across the 32 tasks with ground-truth coordinates, label-based metrics correlated substantially better with true coordinate error (median within-task Spearman ρ = 0.66–0.71) than metrics built from gene expression alone ( ρ = 0.41–0.54; Figure 4b). Agreement also varied by task family. On densely sampled, overlapping mouse brain sections, expression agreement closely tracked coordinate error ( ρ = 0.97; Figure 4d). Distilling agent trajectories into a standalone alignment program 

To test whether agent-discovered heuristics could be consolidated into a reusable, model-free algo-rithm, we tasked an isolated synthesis agent (Claude Code with claude-opus-5.5 ) with distilling the basic-prompt solutions and training scores of the two top-performing agents ( claude-opus-5.5 and 

gpt-6-astra ) into a single Python program under a five-fold cross-validation scheme (Figure 6a). Each synthesized program can then be applied and adapted to other alignment problems without re-querying proprietary models. We considered two splitting schemes: random splits across all tasks and splits by task family. Broadly, the synthesized programs combined rules such as per-section gene-wise z-scoring and reliability-weighted spatial smoothing with coarse-to-fine registration. Aggregated across held-out test folds, the random-split program outperformed 87% of the 16 reference baselines on average, matching interactive frontier agents and exceeding the median published package (54%), whereas the family-split program outperformed 78% overall but dropped to 30% on unseen simulated tissues (Figure 6b). Executing the program takes a median of 47 s per task with zero inference cost, so it amortizes its one-time synthesis cost after 11 tasks compared with querying an average agent in our result set. 12 Discussion 

We translated published spatial transcriptomics benchmarks into an auditable suite of agent tasks and systematically evaluated six frontier coding agents alongside 16 baseline methods on aligning spatial transcriptomics data. Our work highlights several core principles for evaluating AI agents in scientific workflows. First, strict environment isolation is essential: when executed on a shared filesystem, agents routinely located and exploited ground-truth files, evaluation scripts, or precomputed outputs despite explicit prompt instructions forbidding such access. Second, top-performing agents frequently matched or outperformed specialized alignment packages. Remarkably, agents achieved these results primarily by writing compact, custom numerical optimization routines rather than invoking domain-specific libraries. Moreover, equipping agents with pre-installed alignment packages and literature-derived recommenda-tions increased computational cost without consistently improving accuracy. These findings point to broader methodological lessons for benchmarking scientific AI agents. Unlike software engineering tasks, where the desired output can often be specified by unit tests, a scientific coding task admits a broad distribution of exploratory workflows and candidate solutions. Published datasets and results offer a potentially scalable foundation for rigorous evaluation. Additionally, as in many other scientific tasks, metric choice plays a major role in our results, as widely used proxy scores can lead to different model rankings. We emphasize the importance of calibrating against ground truth to guard against metric hacking, such as over-warping in the spatial alignment example. Moreover, because execution traces record every exploratory check, failed attempt, and refinement, they enable systematic program distillation—compiling effective heuristics discovered by frontier models into fast, deterministic, and open-source scientific software. Several limitations should be considered when interpreting our results. First, we focused on spatial transcriptomics alignment; extending our publication-to-benchmark pipeline to more problems, espe-cially open-ended biological analyses, will require domain-specific data contracts and validation checks. Second, because frontier models and command-line harnesses evolve rapidly, point-in-time rankings re-flect current default vendor configurations and might change as new models are released and older ones are deprecated. This is in fact a primary motivation for our exploration of program synthesis as a path to-ward reproducible, vendor-independent tools. Third, although more exhaustive hyperparameter tuning of baseline packages or prompt engineering could further improve individual scores, our evaluation in-tentionally reflects the practical scenario in which scientists apply these tools with a limited tuning budget (or, as in our case, with agent-assisted tuning). To support transparent auditing and community benchmark development, we publicly release the Paper2Bench pipeline, all tasks, the containerized evaluation harness, and an interactive trace explorer containing all agent logs. More broadly, rather than viewing AI agents solely as black-box replacements for human analysts, we envision a collaborative paradigm in which auditable benchmarks and trace dis-tillation harness agent exploration to build more rigorous, interpretable, and reusable tools.

## Supplementary Note 1: Task construction details 

From publication to executable task. Paper2Bench compiles a source publication into benchmark tasks across ten automated stages: source ingestion; extraction of experimental task units from figures, tables, methods, code, and datasets; normalization of inputs into typed data contracts; inference or specifica-tion of output schemas; attachment of deterministic scoring profiles; generation of blind task prompts; construction of isolated workspaces; automated validation; task prioritization by discriminative power, scientific relevance, and computational cost; and emission of runner metadata. Large language models assist in drafting candidate task extractions, output contracts, prompts, and rankings, while determin-istic checks govern acceptance across six lifecycle states ( draft , executable , validated , calibrated , sealed , and 

published ). Automated validators written by AI agents verify schema compliance, file checksums, data licens-ing and provenance, workspace reproducibility, submission format validation (including negative test fixtures), baseline execution (identity transform, geometric fit, and at least one published alignment method), metric determinism and perturbation sensitivity, absence of leaked metadata, and scorer in-tegrity. 

Dataset curation. All tasks are generated programmatically from a declarative registry. Domain-specific curation was performed across task families to ensure exact fidelity to the source publications: matching author notebook scoring modes and marker gene lists (DLPFC); recompiling Bregma slice sets, filtering ambiguous cells, and harmonizing anatomical region labels (MHPR); reconstructing slice assignments 21 (MOp); applying published cell quality filters (whole-brain MERFISH); standardizing input coordinate frames ( Drosophila embryo); regenerating expression matrices with per-section centering and updated marker lists (Open-ST); restoring raw count matrices and linking perturbation metadata (MBSA and MBSP); anonymizing cell identifiers and permuting row order (STARmap and BARISTA-seq); and con-structing lattice-corrected variants for simulated spot tasks. 

Coordinate unit mismatch in the published Drosophila embryo evaluation. Rebuilding the 16-section 

Drosophila E14–16 embryo Stereo-seq stack ( sa-019 ) [41] from raw SABench inputs uncovered a 20-fold scale discrepancy between the input coordinates and the ground-truth coordinates used in the published evaluation [11]. In SABench, input sections are centered raw Stereo-seq pixel coordinates ( raw_x , raw_y ;spatial span ∼ 600 units), whereas the reference ground-truth positions ( new_x , new_y ) are stored in Fly-sta3D 3D-atlas units that are exactly 20-fold smaller (scale = 0.05, plus a small residual rigid rotation per section). Because SABench computed mean absolute error (MAE) directly between unscaled out-put coordinates and 0.05 ×-scaled ground-truth coordinates (SABench Fig. 5f and Supplementary Table 

Flysta3D_MAE ), even the stationary reference slice ( s0 , which no alignment method transforms) incurred an identical floor error of 102.29 across all ten evaluated methods (reproduced to six decimal places by our scorer prior to rescaling). Consequently, under the unscaled evaluation, nearly all rigid and optimal-transport baselines and agent runs compressed tightly around the unaligned identity error, whereas methods that shrank coor-dinates toward the origin appeared artificially accurate ( moscot_warp , 39.8; cast_rescale_affine , 88.5). Rescaling the ground-truth coordinates by 20 × into native input pixel units resolved this artifact: the sta-tionary reference slice has an MAE of 0, unaligned inputs have an MAE of 166.3, and alignment methods separate cleanly—led by PASTE (42.3, a 75% error reduction), moscot (48.0, 71%), claude-sonnet-5.5 

(55.4, 67%), SPACEL (78.4, 53%), claude-opus-5.5 (118.7, 29%), and gpt-6-astra (122.4, 26%), while shrinkage configurations ( cast_rescale_affine , 217.3) and over-warped nonrigid runs score worse than unaligned inputs. 

Sources and task families. The five task families and their data sources are summarized in Supplemen-tary Table 1, with tissue types, spatial platforms, and available evaluation metrics detailed in Supplemen-tary Table 2. Of the 240 tissue sections in SABench, 236 were reconstructed exactly or near-exactly (100 exact, 136 near-exact, 2 with modified quality filters, and 2 from alternative public releases), covering all 295 evaluated section pairs (260 experimental pairs and 35 synthetic rotation and cropping perturbations). Four whole-brain MERFISH tasks use raw imaging coordinates registered to the Allen Common Coordinate Framework (CCF) by the original authors; each section shows an isotropic scaling factor of 1.17 ± 0.05 and about 250 μm of local nonlinear deformation, making them the most challenging geo-metric reconstructions in the benchmark. Ground-truth coordinates derive from simulation (12 tasks), known synthetic transformations of experimental sections (11), author-curated registrations (5), and anatomical atlas coordinates (4). All underlying datasets are publicly available: SABench data (Zenodo

Validation on these synthetic datasets revealed two notable failure modes of proxy alignment metrics. First, grid-based expression correlations exhibit a near-zero upper bound under high Poisson sparsity (split-half correlation of 0.037 on a 10 × 10 spatial grid). Second, nearest-neighbor label metrics can favor imprecise alignments: a coarse centroid fit with a 371 μm coordinate error achieved a label matching accuracy of 0.837, exceeding the score of the exact ground-truth registration (0.815). Coordinate error is therefore used as the primary evaluation metric across all simulated tasks. 

Task package structure. Each task package consists of a machine-readable task card and natural-language task instructions. Agents are instructed to output one CSV file per section containing columns obs_id , x, and y and submissions are validated to ensure exact observation ID matching, row ordering, and finite numerical values.

Label agreement. Label concordance metrics are evaluated within the spatial overlap window (the in-tersection of section bounding boxes) and assigned a score of 0 when sections do not overlap. Matching accuracy (also exported as landmark accuracy) measures the fraction of cells in the aligned section whose nearest spatial neighbor in the reference section shares the same annotation label. Region overlap (ex-ported as region-grid IoU) is the mean intersection over union of alpha shapes ( α = 12) across annotated anatomical regions (scored as 0 for regions containing fewer than 4 cells). 

Expression agreement. Within the spatial overlap window, a 10 × 10 regular grid is constructed, and we retain grid bins where both sections contain at least half of the smaller section’s mean cell count. For each marker gene, spatial expression profiles across retained bins are compared between sections using Pearson correlation (assigned 0 if either profile is constant), cosine similarity, structural similarity (SSIM, computed on maximum-scaled vectors), and mutual information (MI), averaged across marker genes. Main text figures report expression agreement as the mean of gene-wise Pearson correlation, cosine similarity, and SSIM. 

Composite score and within-task percentile. For tasks without ground-truth coordinates, the compos-ite overall score is the unweighted mean of the four gene expression metrics plus label matching accuracy and region overlap when annotations are present. Within-task percentile ranks each run on the task’s primary metric (coordinate error where available, and composite score otherwise) among the 12 primary model-by-prompt conditions, assigning mid-ranks to ties. Failed runs are assigned zero agreement and the coordinate error of the unaligned identity baseline. 

## Supplementary Note 6: Statistical details 

Confidence intervals were estimated via cluster bootstrap resampling (10,000 iterations) over the 19 source datasets, with binomial Wilson intervals reported as a secondary reference. Prompt-condition effects (change in within-task percentile across all 121 tasks and change in log 10 MAE across the 32 coordinate-truth tasks) and pairwise model comparisons against gpt-6-astra were evaluated at the dataset level using two-sided sign-flip permutation tests (20,000 permutations), with Wilcoxon signed-rank and Fried-man tests as secondary checks. gpt-6-astra (mean within-task percentile 79, 95% CI: 67–85) significantly outperformed the four models ranked below gemini-4-argon by 31–51 percentile points ( P ≤ 0.009) and exceeded gemini-4-argon by 7.9 points ( P = 0.12); single-run win rates for gpt-6-astra against the top baseline were 48% (95% CI: 10–72%) on expression agreement and 64% (95% CI: 46–83%) on MAE. On cross-platform tasks where agents outperformed published baselines on expression agreement, agent alignments exhibited lower MNN cell pairing coverage than the top published methods (median 33 0.18 versus 0.25), indicating that higher grid-level expression correlations can be achieved in part by reducing partial tissue overlap; only 1 of 192 final agent scripts on these tasks explicitly computed expres-sion correlations on a 10 × 10 spatial grid. Across the 32 tasks with ground-truth coordinates, the divergence between label-based and expression-based metrics was most pronounced on the 12 simulated tissues (median correlation with coordinate error of −0.10 to 0.30 for expression metrics versus 0.69 for MNN label agreement), whereas on the 15 real-tissue tasks supporting both metric families, expression agreement ( ρ = 0.65) approached label matching accuracy ( ρ = 0.71). Reported P values are unadjusted unless otherwise noted. All statistical analyses were conducted in Python 3.11 ( numpy , pandas , scipy ). 

## Supplementary Note 7: Trace processing, step associations and trace content 

Execution logs were parsed by retaining up to 200,000 characters per step and 60 lines per command out-put after stripping ANSI terminal escape sequences, progress bars, binary blobs, and credential strings. Each run’s final submission script was identified as the last successful execution step that wrote the output coordinate files. Each tool call was classified into one of eight operational phases ( inspecting data , setting up , preprocessing , trying a method , evaluating , writing submission , validating output , and waiting ), and inter-mediate and final scripts were annotated with 48 algorithmic step tags via rule-based AST and regular-expression matching. Because Codex CLI does not log intermediate file-edit payloads or step timestamps and Gemini CLI streams text fragments, exploratory step counts for OpenAI models represent lower bounds. Across basic-prompt runs, exploratory steps were executed substantially more often than they were retained in the final submission: rotational grid searches were explored in 66% of runs and retained in 28%, diagnostic plotting was performed in 53% and retained in 21%, and gradient-based optimization was explored in 12% and never retained (Supplementary Figure 3). Under rich context, PASTE, PASTE2, Spateo, and SPACEL each appeared in 25–38% of final submissions. To quantify the association between individual algorithmic steps and alignment performance, we computed the within-model, within-prompt difference in within-task percentile between runs retaining a given step in their final script and runs without it, pooled across conditions by inverse-variance weighting. Across all 121 tasks, nearest-neighbor matching ( +6.6 percentile points, 95% CI: 3.8–9.7), nonrigid warping ( +6.0, 2.7–10.2), affine registration ( +4.8, 1.6–7.6), and shared-gene intersection ( +3.4, 0.4–6.9) were positively associated with primary scores, whereas invoking a published package, generating diag-nostic plots, or computing custom internal validation scores showed no consistent association. Restricted to the 32 tasks with ground-truth coordinates, however, nonrigid warping was associated with substan-tially worse spatial accuracy ( −17.4 percentile points, 95% CI: −26.5 to −6.2), whereas Procrustes registra-tion ( +13.1, 2.8–21.7) and numerical optimization ( +7.7, 0.3–14.6) were associated with lower coordinate error. ## Supplementary Note 8: Run-to-run variation 

To quantify stochastic run-to-run variation, we compared two independent basic-prompt replicates per model and task (where replicate 1 corresponds to the primary analysis set; gemini-4-argon was run once under the basic prompt and gpt-5.5 completed 69 second-replicate tasks). Cross-replicate consistency was highest for gpt-6-astra (Spearman ρ = 0.93 on coordinate error and 0.98 on composite score) and lowest for gemini-3.5-flash (ρ = 0.63 and 0.57; Supplementary Figure 5a). Replicate runs disagreed on whether the agent matched or beat the best published baseline on 12–18% of tasks; taking the better of two runs increased win rates by 6–10 percentage points (for example, from 61% to 67% for gpt-6-astra and from 32% to 42% for claude-opus-5.5 ; Supplementary Figure 5b). Overall model rankings were identical whether evaluated on replicate 1 or replicate 2 alone (Supplementary Figure 5c), with the median absolute difference between replicates equal to 10–14% of the cross-model range per task for top models (and 49% for gemini-3.5-flash ; Supplementary Figure 5d). 

Variance decomposition. We fitted a saturated factorial linear model (including task, model, prompt condition, and all two- and three-way interactions) across all 2,521 scored runs, partitioning total sums of squares sequentially in the order task, model, prompt, model × prompt, task × model, task × prompt, task × model × prompt, and replicate residual (Supplementary Figure 6a). Outcomes were analyzed as log 10 MAE on the 28 primary coordinate-truth tasks, composite score on the 89 tasks without ground-truth positions, and log 10 -transformed cost and runtime across all 121 tasks.
