Abstract
Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) show strong visual perception
capabilities, but existing evaluations largely focus on single-view or multi-view
settings with shared camera parameters, leaving the challenge of multi-agent
collaborative scenarios underexplored. In such settings, multiple agents observe a
shared environment from independent viewpoints, and reliable decisions require
reasoning across these independent observations of the same environment. To
study this problem, we introduce MaRiO, a benchmark for evaluating multi-
agent collaborative reasoning, and evaluate 23 state-of-the-art MLLMs on it. Our
analysis reveals three key findings: (1) tasks governed by static perception are
generally easier than tasks that require transformational event sequences; (2) most
models struggle to resolve cross-agent correspondences, with errors increasing
with scene complexity; and (3) models show limited ability to leverage geometric
cues present in the scene. As an initial step toward addressing these challenges, we
explore geometry-guided synthetic view augmentation that generates intermediate
transition frames between agent observations, providing auxiliary spatial context
that improves performance in certain categories. Overall, our results highlight that
multi-agent collaborative reasoning remains an open challenge and an important
direction for multimodal embodied systems. The code is available on the project
page: https://omarftt.github.io/MaRiO-page/.

Introduction
Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) achieve strong performance across diverse visual
tasks [1–5]. To assess whether this reflects genuine spatial comprehension, recent works evaluate
spatial and geometric perception abilities like layout, position, orientation, viewpoint and depth
relations [6–11]. These capabilities are essential for embodied applications, like robotic manipulation
[12–14], autonomous navigation [15, 16], and collaborative augmented reality [17], where agents
must reason about space to plan and act safely. In collaborative environments, multiple agents observe
the same scene from different viewpoints [18, 19] and must coordinate their actions (Figure 1).
Prior work on spatial understanding primarily focus on single-view settings [7, 20–23] limited to one
perspective and cannot resolve cross-view ambiguities. Multi-view approaches [14, 24–28] extend
this by evaluating spatial consistency across multiple images, typically captured along a shared
trajectory with known or recoverable geometric relationships.
Therefore, they do not require joint reasoning over distances, motion, or navigational constraints.
In contrast, multi-agent settings [25] introduce an additional challenge: each agent observes the
environment from its own egocentric frame without shared geometric metadata. Such settings can
arise in multi-robot teams without known relative camera poses, drone teams operating in GPS-denied

environments, or collaborative AR/VR systems without a common spatial anchor. As a result, models
must first establish cross-agent correspondences between independent viewpoints and align them
before spatial and geometric relationships can be inferred, a capability necessary for navigation,
collision avoidance, and coordinated movement in collaborative environments.
To study multi-agent collaborative reasoning capabilities of MLLMs, we introduce MaRiO, a bench-
mark based on Multiple agents Reasoning from Independent Observations of a shared environment.
Unlike prior single-view or multi-view setups, MaRiO simulates scenarios where multiple agents
independently observe the same environment from different viewpoints without access to others’
camera poses, coordinate frames, or scene geometry. The benchmark is built around 5 tasks: Distance
Awareness, Occlusion, Relative Positioning, Egocentric Motion, and Navigation, collectively requir-
ing both static spatial perception and compositional reasoning over geometric transformations. By
requiring models to infer relationships purely from independently observed views, MaRiO introduces
a two-stage challenge: establishing cross-agent correspondences and reasoning over the aligned
spatial relationships.
We evaluate 23 recent open-source and proprietary MLLMs on MaRiO and find that all models
perform substantially below human level, highlighting the difficulty of multi-agent collaborative
reasoning. Our analysis shows that many failures arise because models struggle to reliably establish
cross-agent correspondences between independent viewpoints. Importantly, this challenge stems not
simply from the number of observations but from the need to infer relationships between viewpoints
observed from different perspectives. To mitigate this issue, we explore geometry-guided synthetic
intermediate observations that bridge available agent views. These transition views are generated from
reconstructed geometry and incorporated as auxiliary visual context for the MLLM. This approach
helps reveal spatial transitions between viewpoints and improves performance in several multi-agent
collaborative reasoning tasks.
Our contributions are as follows:
• We introduce MaRiO, a multi-agent benchmark for studying multi-agent collaborative
reasoning across independently observed views that requires models to establish cross-agent
correspondence before reasoning.
• We conduct a large-scale evaluation of 23 state-of-the-art open-source and proprietary
MLLMs, revealing substantial performance gaps compared to human reasoning and provid-
ing detailed insights into their limitations.
• We further explore diffusion-guided synthetic view augmentation to generate transition
views between agent observations for improving multi-agent collaborative reasoning.

Related Works
Spatial Reasoning Benchmarks. Recent MLLMs have shown expanded capabilities across visual
reasoning tasks [1, 29–34]. Recent efforts further improve spatial understanding through temporally
enriched inputs, spatial encoders, and 3D-aware representations [28, 35–39]. However, it remains
unclear whether these models can reason across independently oriented agents observing the same
environment. Existing spatial benchmarks primarily evaluate single-view compositional, relational,
3D grounding, and embodied reasoning tasks [10, 15, 16, 22, 40–53], often with geometric metadata
such as scene graphs, camera intrinsics, or 3D annotations. Multi-view and egocentric benchmarks
[8, 17, 24, 25, 27, 28, 54] extend evaluation to cross-view consistency, but typically assume shared
trajectories, recoverable geometry, or observations drawn from a common 3D context. Meanwhile,
existing multi-agent benchmarks [55–57] mainly study strategic decision-making in language-
only settings. In contrast, MaRiO evaluates multimodal multi-agent collaborative reasoning across
independently observed views, where models must establish cross-agent correspondence without
shared geometric metadata.
View Synthesis and Geometry-Guided Generation. Prior work recovers missing viewpoints
using 3D reconstruction methods such as point clouds [37, 58, 59] and Gaussian splatting [60, 61];
but most MLLMs cannot directly process these modalities without costly architectural changes.
Inspired by diffusion-based novel view synthesis from reconstructed geometry [62–64], we generate
intermediate viewpoints that reveal spatial transitions between agent observations that can aid multi-
agent collaborative reasoning without modifying the underlying MLLM.
3
Benchmarking Multi-agent Collaborative Reasoning
We introduce MaRiO, a multiple-choice VQA benchmark for collaborative multi-agent reasoning
over independently oriented viewpoints without shared geometric metadata (Table 1). It contains
3,255 QA samples across 1,680 unique observation sets, each comprising views from multiple agents
within a shared environment (Figure 2).
3.1
MaRiO Construction
MaRiO contains 1,680 unique collaborative multi-agent observation sets, distributed as 1,371 pairs
and 309 triplets of views, forming a total of 3,425 unique observations. Each set captures synchronized
views of a shared scene from independently oriented agents.
Task Definition. MaRiO targets five reasoning dimensions across two categories: Static Perception fo-
cuses on spatial relationships and visibility, covering (1) Occlusion, which tests viewpoint-dependent
visibility and object enumeration under partial occlusion; (2) Relative Position, which requires infer-

depth and proximity estimation from an egocentric viewpoint. Compositional Reasoning focuses on
sequencing geometric transformations and includes (4) Egocentric Motion, which tests orientation
under reference-frame transformations and (5) Navigation, which requires sequential state updates
through compositions of translational and rotational actions.
Image Generation. MaRiO is constructed using both real-world images and synthetic simulators
to balance realism and controllability. For real-world images, we incorporate 200 scenes from
RealEstate10K [65], which provides unconstrained house-tour videos with natural viewpoint variation,
lighting, and object layouts, and 35 scenes from NAVI [66], offering controlled multi-perspective
object observations. These sources contribute a total of 913 real-world image sets. To ensure coverage
of targeted spatial phenomena, we further generate 39 scenes using VirtualHome [67], Habitat-Sim
[68], and AI2-THOR [69] simulators, yielding 767 synthetic image sets.
Some synthetic images are augmented with lighting variations, minor object perturbations, or lens
artifacts to increase visual diversity.
QA Generation. QA pairs were manually generated through a structured annotation process involving
three domain-expert annotators. Initial questions were jointly drafted, reviewed for clarity and spatial
validity, and cross-verified by an additional annotator. Each reasoning category followed predefined
specifications defining the spatial primitives, reference-frame assumptions, and reasoning constraints
to ensure consistency. Questions were designed so that integrating information across views was
necessary to determine the correct answer. All instances follow a four-choice multiple-choice format
with plausible distractors and randomized answer-option order to avoid any inherent position bias.
Exact prompts and additional examples are provided in the Supplementary. An LLM was used as a
writing assistant during this process — specifically, to polish question phrasing and to draft initial
candidate distractors; all outputs were reviewed, edited, and accepted or rejected by the human
annotators, and ground-truth labels were authored by humans only.
3.2
Evaluation Setup
We evaluate 23 state-of-the-art MLLMs, spanning 19 open-source models and 4 proprietary models,
as listed in Table 2. The open-source models include both general-purpose and spatial MLLMs.
Open-source models are evaluated using their official implementations, while proprietary models are
accessed through their APIs. All models are evaluated zero-shot on the multiple-choice VQA task.
We report Overall accuracy, computed as the total number of correct predictions over all questions,
and Average accuracy, computed as the mean of task-wise accuracies across the five tasks.

Results
Table 2 reveals a substantial gap between human and model performance, highlighting limitations of
current MLLMs in collaborative multi-agent reasoning. Proprietary models outperform open-source
ones, with Gemini-2.5-Pro achieving the highest accuracy, while the strongest open-source model,
Ovis2.5-9B, trails by about 14%, and most others cluster between 30–42%.
While models struggle across all reasoning categories, the largest difficulties appear in tasks requiring
compositional reasoning over geometric transformations (i.e., Navigation and Egocentric Motion),
where many models approach near-random accuracy. In contrast, models perform relatively better
on Occlusion and Relative Position, indicating stronger capability in coarse spatial perception than
in dynamic, viewpoint-dependent geometric reasoning. Overall, these results suggest that current
MLLMs rely more on orientation-specific cues than on robust geometric reasoning for collaborative
multi-agent reasoning.

Effect of Removal of Observations.
To further probe the source of this gap, we analyze whether models integrate information across
multiple viewpoints through a viewpoint removal ablation where one randomly selected observation
or all observations are withheld while the question remains unchanged. Results reveal a clear contrast
between proprietary and open-source models (Figure 4). Proprietary models exhibit consistent
performance degradation across reasoning categories when viewpoint(s) is removed, indicating that
their predictions depend on information distributed across multiple observations. In contrast, open-
source models show only minor performance changes, with some categories exhibiting negligible
degradation. Notably, in compositional tasks involving geometric primitives, open-source models
often maintain above or near random accuracy even with no visual input, suggesting reliance on
answer priors or bias rather than cross-agent visual evidence.
Takeaway: Strong static perception does not translate into reliable geometric reasoning in MLLMs
because they fail to integrate distributed multi-agent collaborative observations into single unified
representations.

4.2
Cross-Agent Correspondence under Relational Complexity
Impact of Increasing Relational Complexity. To analyze how relational complexity affects multi-
agent collaborative reasoning, we compare performance between two-agent and three-agent obser-
vations. This setup directly evaluates the models’ ability to maintain consistent spatial alignment
as the number of cross-agent relationships increases. This increase in complexity arises because
models must integrate information from more agent viewpoints and resolve a larger set of cross-agent
correspondences simultaneously. As shown in Figure 5, accuracy declines across nearly all models
and reasoning categories when transitioning from two-agent to three-agent scenes. The largest drops
occur in Navigation and Egocentric Motion, which already exhibit lower baseline performance. These
tasks require maintaining spatial relationships across viewpoints, and the additional agent introduces
more potential cross-view correspondences that must be resolved simultaneously. The consistent
degradation indicates that many MLLMs struggle to maintain coherent cross-agent spatial alignment
as relational complexity increases, particularly in geometric tasks.
Effect of Shared Environmental Landmarks. To further examine the source of this difficulty, we
analyze how the density of shared environmental landmarks affects reasoning. Anchors are static
environmental elements visible across multiple agents’ observation that can serve as reference points
for cross-agent alignment. We group observations by anchor count ([0–2], [3–5], [6–9], [10+])
and report results averaged across both two-agent and three-agent observation settings. Results
show a non-monotonic pattern (Figure 14 in the Supplementary): accuracy improves as anchor
density increases from very sparse scenes, but degrades in more cluttered environments. Navigation
and Egocentric Motion tasks are particularly sensitive to this effect. While a few shared landmarks
provide useful reference points for cross-view alignment, higher landmark density introduces multiple
plausible correspondences across viewpoints, increasing ambiguity in matching spatial references
across agents and leading to performance degradation. These results suggest that as relational
structure grows, models struggle to consistently resolve cross-agent correspondence.
Takeaway: As independent observations from multiple agents and scene clutter increase, MLLMs
struggle to resolve cross-view correspondences, leading to unstable reasoning.
4.3
Geometric Consistency and Structural Cues
Orientation Consistency under Symmetry-Preserving Transformation. Following the observed
degradation under increasing relational complexity, we examine whether these failures stem from
unstable spatial representations. We hypothesize that if models maintain stable spatial representations,
their predictions should remain invariant under symmetry-preserving transformations; failure indicates
orientation-sensitive reasoning. To assess this hypothesis, we perform a horizontal reflection test
on Navigation and Egocentric Motion tasks, where images are flipped and directional language
is correspondingly swapped (e.g., “turn left” →“turn right”) to preserve geometric equivalence.
Despite this symmetry, many predictions change after reflection, and only a minority remain correct
in both orientations (Figure 6, left), indicating orientation instability under geometrically equivalent
transformations. To further probe the source of this instability, we divide Navigation into directional
questions, where both image and text are transformed, and obstacle-based questions, where only
the image is flipped. Directional questions exhibit a larger accuracy drop than obstacle-based ones,
while Egocentric Motion shows a moderate decline (Figure 6, right). This suggests that orientation
sensitivity is more influenced by directional language cues than stable geometric representations.

Impact of Depth Cue Complexity. We next examine whether models can leverage geometric scene
structure when it is naturally present in the image by analyzing whether a higher density of depth
discontinuities, one type of monocular depth cue, correlates with improved multi-agent reasoning.
Relative depth maps are extracted using Depth Anything V2 [76], and depth edge density is computed
as the fraction of pixels lying on detected depth boundaries. Importantly, models are evaluated
only with the original RGB images; depth edges are computed post-hoc to analyze whether scenes
with richer geometric structure correspond to performance change. To control for scene complexity,
samples are grouped by the number of distinct depth layers (Simple: 1–2, Moderate: 3, Complex:
4+), and performance is compared between high- and low-edge-density scenes within each group. As
shown in Figure 7, stronger depth boundary structure does not improve performance; most models
perform worse at higher edge density, with the largest degradation in simple scenes, while effects
remain small or negligible in moderate and complex scenes. This suggests that models fail to exploit
geometric scene structure and often treat additional depth cues as visual complexity rather than useful
spatial information.
Takeaway: MLLMs exhibit weak use of geometric cues, with predictions sensitive to orientation
changes and largely unaffected by scene geometry, leading to inconsistent reasoning and poor
cross-agent alignment.

4.4
Human Reasoning Difficulty
Types of Human Errors. Human evaluators reach 73.2% overall accuracy on MaRiO (Table 2), with
the lowest scores on Egocentric Motion and Navigation. To understand the nature of this difficulty,
we examine every question answered incorrectly by at least one evaluator (21 of 81 questions) and
categorize 23 incorrect responses by error type (Table 3a). The dominant error type (13 of 23)
is direction confusion, where the evaluator rotated, mirrored, reversed, or reordered the correct
direction, consistent with spatial-transformation mistakes. This is expected for three-agent Egocentric
Motion questions, which require composing multiple sequential viewpoint transformations and
place substantial cognitive demand on the evaluator. Prior work in cognitive science shows that
reasoning about a different viewpoint involves a mental-rotation-like process whose time and effort
scale with the degree of perspective change, and that judgments become more error-prone as the
number of required transformations increases. Visibility-type judgments, which do not require such
transformations, do not show the same cost [77, 78].
Human-Model Comparison. We further compare human and Gemini-2.5-Pro outcomes on the same
81 questions (Table 3b). Among the 60 questions every evaluator answered correctly, Gemini-2.5-Pro
failed on 19, indicating that human success does not simply track the questions a strong model also
answers correctly. Conversely, among the 21 questions with at least one human error, Gemini-2.5-Pro
answered 11 correctly. Human and model errors therefore fall on largely different subsets of questions,
rather than on a shared set of questions that are difficult for any answerer.
Takeaway: Human errors reflect the cognitive cost of composing viewpoint transformations and do
not coincide with model errors, indicating that MaRiO’s questions are answerable while remaining
demanding for both humans and MLLMs.
5
View Augmentation for Collaborative Reasoning
Our analysis reveals that many failures stem from models being unable to reliably establish corre-
spondences across agents’ independent observations, as these tasks require jointly reasoning over all
observations to reach a single decision. To mitigate this, we test whether providing additional inter-
mediate viewpoints between observations can help bridge the spatial gap across agents. Intuitively,
if transitions between viewpoints are available, they can reveal how observations relate spatially,
allowing the model to use these frames as auxiliary visual context rather than inferring cross-view
relationships purely through reasoning over independent observations.
9


5.1
Geometry-Guided Synthetic View Augmentation
We generate synthetic intermediate viewpoints to bridge the spatial gap between agents’ observations
and provide additional context. First, we reconstruct a 3D point-cloud representation [58] of the
scene from the multiple agents’ observation. Camera poses are then interpolated and intermediate
views are rendered from the reconstructed geometry to approximate the spatial transitions between
observations. These rendered views are used to guide a diffusion-based video generation process
[62] that produces a sequence of synthetic frames containing novel intermediate viewpoints. The
resulting video provides additional visual context that reveals how the independent observations relate
spatially. For models that support video input, the generated video is provided alongside the original
images; for image-only models, sampled frames from the generated sequence are appended to the
input images, with the textual prompt modified accordingly. Figure 8 shows examples from each
stage of the geometry-guided view synthesis process, from reconstructed point clouds to interpolated
videos and the resulting novel views.
Effect of Synthetic View Augmentation. Synthetic view augmentation yields category-specific
improvements rather than uniform gains (Table 4). Spatial reasoning models show limited benefit
from the generated views, with performance remaining unchanged or degrading slightly in some
cases. These models already attempt to infer geometric structure from the input images, and
diffusion-generated intermediate views may introduce small geometric inconsistencies that affect
precise metric reasoning. In contrast, general MLLMs benefit more noticeably from synthetic views,
particularly in Navigation and Relative Position tasks, which require aligning spatial relationships
across viewpoints. The generated transitions likely provide useful intermediate cues that help models
connect observations from different agents. Ablations on the number of sampled frames, interpolation
order, and a cropped-frame baseline confirm that gains arise from the synthesized novel viewpoints
rather than from additional frames alone (see Supplementary).
Takeaway: Synthetic augmentation selectively improves multi-agent reasoning by aiding cross-view
relational understanding, while offering limited benefit for precise geometric estimation tasks.
6
Conclusion
In this work, we introduced MaRiO, a benchmark for evaluating multi-agent collaborative reasoning,
and studied the behavior of 23 state-of-the-art open-source and proprietary MLLMs on this task. Our
analysis reveals a substantial gap between human and model performance, with models struggling
particularly on geometric tasks such as Navigation and Egocentric Motion. These failures largely stem
from difficulty in establishing reliable cross-agent correspondences across independent viewpoints.
As an initial step toward addressing these challenges, we explore geometry-guided synthetic view
augmentation that introduces intermediate viewpoints between observations, providing additional
spatial context that improves performance in certain reasoning categories. Overall, our results
highlight the need for stronger cross-agent alignment and geometry-aware perception in future
multimodal embodied systems for collaborative environments.
Limitations.
MaRiO focuses on static, snapshot-based observations and a multiple-choice QA
setup, which does not capture temporal dynamics or interactive decision-making. Scores in this
format can also depend on the number of answer options and do not directly measure a model’s
ability to answer without them. Extending the benchmark to sequential observations and embodied
interactions would provide a fuller evaluation of multi-agent reasoning in realistic settings.
Broader Impact.
Our findings highlight gaps in current MLLMs for multi-agent spatial reason-
ing, with implications for applications such as robotics, autonomous navigation, and collaborative
augmented reality, where reliable cross-agent understanding is essential. However, improving such
capabilities raises considerations around deployment in safety-critical and privacy-sensitive envi-
ronments, including multi-camera surveillance, emphasizing the need for robust, interpretable, and
responsible use.

A
Model Bias Analysis
To better understand the internal reasoning tendencies of MLLMs, we conduct a series of diagnostic
analyses aimed at identifying systematic biases in spatial reasoning behavior. Rather than evaluating
overall accuracy, these analyses probe how models respond to different structural formulations of
spatial tasks. Our findings reveal consistent biases in how models represent navigation primitives and
directional semantics.
Bias Towards Navigational Primitives. To analyze how models reason over and utilize navigation
primitives, we evaluate performance across subsets of navigation questions that isolate translation
and rotation motion primitives. If models internally represent navigation as a sequence of grounded
spatial operations, their performance should remain relatively stable across primitive types. Con-
versely, systematic differences may reveal biases in how models interpret or reason about movement
instructions.
We partition the navigation QA subset according to the primitives present in answer choices: Transla-
tion Only, Rotation Only, and Compositions, where the latter contains action sequences consisting of
both translation and rotation. For each model, we compute Top-1 Accuracy separately for each of
these subsets. As shown in Figure 9, models exhibit clear asymmetries across primitive types. Several
models perform substantially better on translation-only questions, while others show higher accuracy
for rotation-only scenarios. Interestingly, some models achieve higher accuracy on compositional
actions than on either primitive subset, suggesting that structured multi-step instructions may provide
contextual cues that facilitate reasoning. In contrast, other models show the opposite behavior, where
performance degrades when primitives must be combined.
Takeaway: MLLMs differ in how they represent navigational primitives. While some appear to
benefit from compositional structure, others struggle to integrate multiple spatial operations into a
coherent navigation plan.
Directional Bias under Cardinal Remapping. We next examine whether models bind spatial
semantics to egocentric spatial frames in a geometry-consistent manner. Specifically, we analyze
directional predictions under two binding conditions: (1) the original binding between cardinal tokens
and egocentric spatial relations (forward, left, right, backward), and (2) a remapped binding in which
these associations are permuted. For each model, we compute predicted cardinal token frequencies
normalized by the ground-truth prior. Results are shown in Figure 10.
Under remapped bindings, models exhibit heterogeneous behavior.
Some architectures (e.g.,
LLaVA-Video-7B, Ovis2.5-9B) display rotation-consistent shifts in their directional predictions
(e.g., North→West), suggesting that directional reasoning is partially grounded in an egocentric
spatial representation rather than arbitrary token-level association. In contrast, other models (e.g.,
InternVL3-8B, mPLUG-Owl3) maintain persistent token-level directional preferences that do not
rotate coherently after remapping.
Takeaway: Directional reasoning mechanisms vary across MLLMs, with some exhibiting partial
geometric grounding and others demonstrating stronger token-level associations.

B.3
Effect of Shared Environmental Landmarks
Figure 13 illustrates representative examples of the shared anchor density bins used throughout our
analysis. Shared anchors correspond to objects that are simultaneously observable across agent
viewpoints and thus serve as cross-view reference landmarks that enable spatial reasoning and
geometric correspondence across perspectives.
We further analyze performance across different shared anchor densities for both 2-agent and 3-agent

make cross-view correspondence more difficult. This trend appears in both 2-agent and 3-agent
configurations, with the effect more pronounced in the 3-agent case, where additional viewpoints
increase the relational load required to reconcile spatial correspondences across agents.

B.6
Explicit Depth Input and a Specialized Spatial Model
We complement the RGB-only analysis by testing explicit depth input in general-purpose MLLMs
and a specialized spatial model.
Depth Maps as Input. We append metric depth maps from Depth-Anything-V2-Metric-Indoor-
Large [76] after the RGB views of the 472 two-agent Distance Awareness questions, keeping the
prompt unchanged. Adding depth provides no clear benefit across the tested models (Table 5).
Gemini-2.5-Pro and Qwen2.5-VL-7B drop by about 13 percentage points, while the two LLaVA
models change little. Replacing the depth maps with duplicated RGB views reduces Qwen2.5-VL-7B
accuracy by only 1.8 points. This suggests that its larger drop with depth cannot be explained by the
number of additional images alone.
For Qwen2.5-VL-7B, the drop from adding depth is comparable to the 17.2-point drop from removing
one agent’s view in our observation-removal ablation on the same questions. In contrast, LLaVA-
NeXT-Video-7B changes little under either condition (+0.6 and +0.4 points, respectively). In neither
case does depth information provide a clear improvement, supporting our finding that additional
per-view depth cues do not address the core bottleneck in multi-agent reasoning.
Specialized Spatial Model. We evaluate SpatialBot [79], a model designed for spatial reasoning with
RGB and depth input, on the two-agent subset (2,590 questions). Using its original implementation,
we provide both agent views with depth maps from ZoeDepth [80]. We compare it with LLaVA-
OneVision-7B and Gemini-2.5-Flash, which receive RGB views only (Table 6). Despite its spatial
specialization and access to explicit depth, SpatialBot performs below both general-purpose models in
every task and overall (33.2% vs. 40.5% and 44.1%). These results suggest that spatial specialization
and access to depth do not, on their own, ensure strong performance in MaRiO’s multi-agent setting.
They also reinforce our central finding that the primary bottleneck is reasoning about cross-agent
viewpoint correspondence.

C.2
System Prompts
Here we present the prompts used for each task category in the benchmark. All evaluations follow
a shared master prompt that enforces consistent answer formatting and instructs the model to base
its decision strictly on the visual evidence across agents’ observations. In addition, each reasoning
category is paired with a specialized prompt that defines the spatial semantics required for that
task, including assumptions about camera placement, agent motion, egocentric coordinate systems,
occlusion definitions, and relative agent positioning. These category-specific prompts ensure that
models interpret spatial relations, navigation primitives, and visibility conditions under a consistent
and well-defined set of rules during evaluation.
Master Prompt
You are an AI assistant performing an academic benchmark evaluation. The question has exactly
four answer options presented in alphabetical order (A, B, C, D). You MUST respond with
exactly ONE alphabet character in the set A, B, C, D. Select the character that corresponds to
the most plausible answer. Do NOT provide explanations, reasoning, analysis, or any additional
text. Do NOT include multiple answers. Your response must consist of a single line only. The
answer MUST be based strictly on the visual evidence in the images. If more than one option
appears plausible, select the single most consistent option with the given views.
There are N agents operating within the same scene. The first image was captured by agent 1,
and the second image was captured by agent 2, ..., and the N th image was captured by agent N.
The images were captured by a camera with exactly the same intrinsic settings, and the images
were captured at exactly the same time.
Distance Awareness
Both agents are at the same height unless mentioned otherwise. The only difference between the
two images is the agent’s 3D position.
Egocentric/Allocentric Motion
In this scene, cardinal directions will be bound to the local coordinate systems defined by agents’
egocentric frames of reference. The camera is positioned on and oriented along the agent’s
forward axis. The agent’s forward-facing direction is North. The opposite direction (extending
outward from the back of the agent) is South. Extending outward from the agent’s right side is
East. Extending outward from the agent’s left side is west.
28


Navigation
Assume that the camera is fixed to the front of the agent and faces outwards along the agent’s
forward axis. Thus, changes to the agent’s position and orientation by way of locomotion are
equivalently applied to the camera’s position and orientation. If the agent is tasked with turning
left or turning right, assume that these commands refer to rotating in place 90 degrees towards
left and rotating in place 90 degrees towards right respectively. Assume that turning around
refers to rotating in place 180 degrees. Assume that prompts such as ’turn slightly left’ and ’turn
slightly right’ refer to rotating in place less than 90 degrees to the left and rotating in place less
than 90 degrees to the right respectively.
Occlusion/Visibility/Counting
’Fully occluded’ means the object cannot be seen at all. ’Partially occluded’ means the object
can be partially hidden, but still detectable. Generally speaking, the use of ’occluded’ can be
interpreted as either ’fully occluded’ OR ’partially occluded’.
Relative Agents
Position Semantics are defined as follows: ’Ahead of’ = Along the same direction as the agent’s
camera is facing; ’Behind’ = Along the opposite direction as the agent’s camera is facing;
’Left-side’ = Perpendicular to the agent’s viewing direction on the agent’s left side; ’Right-side’
= Perpendicular to the agent’s viewing direction on the agent’s right side.
Agent–Image Association.
The association between images and agents is communicated to the
model through the text prompt. The “Agent x” labels shown in the figures are illustrative and are
not embedded in the images. As specified in the master prompt, the ith image is stated to have been
captured by agent i, so the mapping is always given explicitly rather than inferred from image order
alone. For interfaces that support interleaved image–text inputs, we reinforce this mapping with
adjacent textual references (e.g., “Image 1: <image>”). For interfaces that do not, images are passed
in order with the same prompt-based mapping, as the preprocessor preserves image order.
For the synthetic view augmentation experiments, the generated intermediate views are not associated
with any agent. They are provided as auxiliary visual context showing the relationship between
agents’ viewpoints, and the prompt states explicitly that they are supporting context rather than an
additional agent observation.
Agent Visibility.
Agents are not directly visible to one another, reflecting scenarios in which they
operate beyond visual line of sight. The model must therefore infer their relative positions and
orientations from correspondences across their views.
C.3
Annotation Protocol
Question generation was done by three expert annotators drawn from the author team. Each annotator
was assigned primary responsibility for one or more image sources (RealEstate10K, NAVI, Virtual-

types were not partitioned across annotators; instead, all annotators contributed questions across the
five reasoning categories.
The annotation pipeline proceeded in three stages. Annotators first drafted candidate questions

across multiple agents’ views. Multiple-choice options were then drafted with LLM assistance, it
is an LLM was prompted to propose three plausible distractors per question, which an annotator

no LLM output was accepted as a correct answer.
Each draft question was reviewed by at least one additional annotator, and occasionally by two, for
spatial validity, ambiguity, and consistency with the category-level specification. When a reviewer
flagged a question as ambiguous, the team first tried to repair it by reformulating the prompt over

C.4
Human Evaluation Protocol
The human-baseline accuracy reported in Table 2 was collected on a stratified subset of MaRiO. We
sampled 97 questions in total: 17 from Distance Awareness, 21 from Egocentric Motion, 16 from

inputs was given.
C.5
Distractor Construction Rules
To maintain consistent distractor quality across reasoning categories, we followed a category-specific
construction scheme.
Distance Awareness.
The answer space includes the agent identifiers, along with edge-case options
such as all agents are equally close or none of the agents observe the target. Distractors are selected
from the remaining valid agents or edge-case options.
Egocentric Motion.
Options are generally restricted to the four cardinal directions, e.g., North,
East, South, and West. Distractors are selected from the remaining directions that do not correspond
to the ground-truth direction.
Navigation.
The answer space consists of motion sequences over a fixed primitive set {move
forward, move backward, turn left, turn right, turn around}. Distractors are constructed by applying
minimal directional perturbations to the ground-truth sequence, that is done by inverting one or
more direction primitives or reordering steps, so that each distractor remains a syntactically valid
navigation plan but fails to satisfy the goal.

C.6
Number of Answer Options
To analyze how model performance on MaRiO scales with the size of the answer space, we use
GPT-5.2 to expand 600 existing QA samples from four to six and eight answer options, keeping the
original questions and correct answers unchanged. The results show a consistent decline in overall
accuracy as the number of answer options increases across all three evaluated models (Tables 10
to 12). The magnitude of this decline varies across tasks. Distance Awareness has fewer samples in
this analysis because its more constrained answer space limits how far it can be expanded. These
results highlight sensitivity to the number of distractors, a limitation of the multiple-choice evaluation
format.
C.7
Task Taxonomy
To understand how the five tasks of MaRiO relate to one another, we computed pairwise Spearman
rank correlations of task-wise accuracy across the 19 open-source models on the full benchmark
(Table 13). Distance Awareness, Occlusion, and Relative Position are strongly correlated with one
another (0.855–0.933), whereas Egocentric Motion and Navigation correlate more weakly with these
tasks (0.399–0.634) and with each other (0.407).
This pattern led us to group the tasks in relevant analyses as Static Perception (Distance Awareness,
Occlusion, and Relative Position) and Compositional (Egocentric Motion and Navigation). The
grouping reflects the type of reasoning each task requires rather than its content. Static Perception
tasks are answered from perceptual cues identified across the agents’ views, such as relative proximity,
visibility, and relative direction. Since MaRiO provides only RGB observations without camera
poses or coordinates, this includes Relative Position, which must be inferred from perceptual cues
rather than computed from known viewpoint relationships. Compositional tasks additionally require
composing sequential viewpoint transformations or motions across reference frames.

Real vs. Synthetic Data
To examine how image source affects performance, we evaluate each model separately on real-world
and synthetic image sets. The real-world subset contains 913 image sets drawn from RealEstate10K
and NAVI, and the synthetic subset contains 767 image sets generated with VirtualHome, Habitat-Sim,

D
Qualitative Analysis
Figure 22 presents representative examples across the five reasoning categories, highlighting common
success and failure patterns among models. Closed-source models generally demonstrate stronger
performance in tasks requiring coordinated spatial reasoning across multiple views, particularly in
distance awareness and navigation where integrating observations from different agents is critical. In
contrast, many open-source models exhibit inconsistent predictions, often relying on partial visual
cues from a single viewpoint rather than reconciling information across agents. This behavior
becomes more pronounced in the 3-agent setting, where the additional viewpoint increases the need
for coherent cross-view alignment. For instance, tasks involving occlusion reasoning and relative
positioning frequently require identifying objects that are only partially visible across views; while
some models correctly aggregate these cues, others fail to maintain consistent correspondences
between observations. Conversely, egocentric motion questions show mixed behavior across both
model types, suggesting that directional reasoning remains challenging even when visual ambiguity
is limited. Overall, these examples illustrate that while some models can leverage multi-agent
observations effectively, many still struggle to consolidate distributed visual evidence into a unified
spatial representation, particularly as the number of agents increases.

for future work is to explore time-asynchronous observations, where agents may observe the same

16. Declaration of LLM usage
Question: Does the paper describe the usage of LLMs if it is an important, original, or
non-standard component of the core methods in this research? Note that if the LLM is used
only for writing, editing, or formatting purposes and does not impact the core methodology,
scientific rigor, or originality of the research, declaration is not required.
Answer: [Yes]
Justification: An LLM was used during the QA construction process described in Section 3
in two limited ways: (i) to polish the grammar and phrasing of human-authored questions,
and (ii) to draft initial candidates for the three incorrect answer options (distractors). All
LLM outputs were subsequently reviewed, edited, and accepted or rejected by the three
domain-expert annotators; the correct answer for every sample was authored by a human
annotator and cross-verified by another. The LLM was not used to author questions from
scratch, evaluate models, score responses, or determine ground-truth labels.
