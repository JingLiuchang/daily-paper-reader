## Abstract 

As large language models power autonomous agents, understand-ing how they collaborate and where they fail becomes critical. We introduce PARLEY, an extensible platform for studying LLM agent collaboration through four asymmetric puzzle tasks grounded in cognitive psychology (referential communication, joint coordina-tion, perspective-taking, and spatial reasoning). GPT-4o agent pairs succeeded in 60.5% of conversations ( 𝑁 =400, 100 per task), with task-level variation (34–80%) reflecting distinct collaborative de-mands. From these conversations, we derived the data-driven PAC taxonomy : six failure types organized into Perception (visual inter-pretation and spatial reasoning), Adaptation (progress tracking and feedback integration), and Coordination (verification and ground-ing). Failure to incorporate external feedback was a critical indica-tor of unsuccessful collaboration, with agents repeatedly applying unsuccessful strategies. We also show that a taxonomy-guided in-tervention ( 𝑁 =200) improved average task success to 70.5%. These results demonstrate how PARLEY can be used to derive meaning-ful, data-driven insights that can in turn lead to improved agentic collaboration.

## 1 Introduction 

Do you remember the last time you collaborated on a problem without having all the information? Perhaps it was co-writing a research paper with a colleague in a different time zone, debugging an issue where only your teammate could reproduce the crash, or planning a study where you handled the design and someone else ran the participants. In each case, success depended not just on individual competence, but on something harder: building shared understanding across different perspectives, knowledge, and ca-pabilities [34]. Decades of cognitive science have shown that this kind of asymmetric collaboration relies on grounding [9, 10], col-laborative repair [32, 35], and perspective-taking [12, 18] among others. As large language models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed as autonomous agents that collaborate with other agents and hu-mans [30, 38], a pressing question emerges: how do LLMs build shared understanding and recover from collaboration failures? Current evaluations offer limited answers. For instance, strategic games [1] test reasoning under competition but not cooperative communication, and open-ended evaluation suites [25] report ag-gregate success rates without diagnosing how collaboration breaks down. However, neither approach is grounded in the cognitive mechanisms that make human collaboration work [10, 13]. ## 2 Related Work 

Research on multi-agent interaction has explored a range of settings, from competitive environments where agents pursue opposing goals, to collaborative tasks that require shared understanding, to mixed settings where both dynamics coexist. For instance, strategic games [1] and social dilemmas [8] test reasoning under competition but do not require building shared understanding cooperatively. On the collaborative side, Carroll et al. [6] introduced Overcooked, a game that evaluates joint coordination under partial observability, and Peng et al. [31] designed information-asymmetric puzzles that require agents to share private knowledge to reach a shared goal. In a different setting, Park et al. [30] introduced generative agents that inhabit a simulated town, producing emergent social behav-iors (spreading invitations, coordinating attendance at events) in a setting that naturally blends competitive and collaborative dy-namics. In the context of collaborative tasks, the information structure be-tween agents shapes the cognitive demands of the interaction. Sym-metric tasks give both agents the same information and tools [16, 36], testing communication efficiency but not perspective-taking, be-cause an agent can succeed by assuming its partner’s view is iden-tical to its own. Asymmetric tasks introduce information gaps that force genuine communication [5, 24], requiring three cognitive demands drawn from established constructs in cognitive psychol-ogy: information sharing under uncertainty, perspective-taking (modeling a partner’s knowledge state to decide what to communi-cate [18]), and collaborative repair (reconciling misaligned views through language [10, 32]). Asymmetric tasks thus represent the most demanding test of collaborative ability, yet no unified platform exists to systematically compare how different types of asymmetry shape the collaborative process and its failures. Several studies have explored tasks and phenomena related to those in PARLEY, revealing both progress and persistent gaps. For instance, Davidson et al. [11] documented a “collaboration gap” where individually capable models degrade significantly in collabo-rative settings, even when tasks should benefit from multiple per-spectives. In a separate work, Wu et al. [37] characterized LLMs as “passive responders” that wait for explicit prompts rather than proac-tively contributing to shared goals. Related studies examine com-munication and coordination across different settings. CAMEL [22] studies communicative role-playing agents, while SOTOPIA [39] evaluates open-ended social interactions with private goals. In collaborative programming, CooperBench [20] examines agents implementing separately assigned features, including integration and communication failures, while Mieczkowski et al. [27] ana-lyze coordination costs and scaling through a distributed-systems framework and programming experiments. Complementing these interaction-based studies, Kosinski [21] examines whether models track another actor’s knowledge through controlled false-belief stories and matched controls. Bringing information asymmetry into joint problem-solving, Cadet et al. [5] introduced AsymPuzl, a two-agent symbolic puzzle that varies puzzle size and feedback and includes a full-information single-agent comparison. PARLEY builds on these directions across four visual, tool-mediated asymmetric tasks with environment-evaluated outcomes, a shared taxonomy-building pipeline, and an intervention. We characterize recurring collaboration failures and interpret them in relation to prior human-collaboration research. A few studies have explored the idea of creating taxonomies for agentic collaboration failures. For instance, Cemri et al. [7] pro-posed a taxonomy of system-level failure modes in multi-agent LLM systems, categorizing what fails (e.g., verification errors, in-struction drift). In a separate effort, Bisconti et al. [4] developed a taxonomy of risks in LLM-to-LLM interactions focused on safety and adversarial concerns, and Song et al. [33] introduced Aegis, which categorizes agent-environment failures and proposes opti-mizations to mitigate them. In contrast, our work leverages prior research in cognitive psychology and human collaboration to de-rive a data-driven failure taxonomy from 1,621 failure instances extracted from conversation traces, focusing not just on what fails but on why it fails in terms of the underlying collaborative pro-cesses. We further validate the taxonomy through an intervention study, demonstrating that targeted modifications informed by the identified failure patterns lead to improved collaboration outcomes. 

## 3 Research Platform 

PARLEY is an extensible web-based platform for evaluating LLM agent collaboration through asymmetric puzzle tasks, designed around three principles: (1) cognitive grounding in validated hu-man collaboration constructs, (2) information asymmetry requiring genuine communication, and (3) measurable outcomes with binary success criteria. 

## 3.1 Tasks 

The four tasks are adapted from cognitive psychology paradigms for agent-agent collaboration, each capturing a distinct asymmetry type (information, visual, tool, and role). Each task (Table 1) presents two agents with asymmetric information and complementary tools. 

Tangram Reference (Figure 2, top-left) tests referential com-munication [10, 14]. Agent A sees a target sequence of four abstract symbols but has no manipulation tools. Agent B can place and submit symbols but cannot see the target. The goal is for the pair Javier Hernandez and Andrew D. Wilson 

to reproduce the exact 4-symbol target sequence. Each conversa-tion randomly selects 8 symbols from a pool of 32 tangram figures adapted from the KiloGram dataset [17], then draws 4 to form the target. Visually similar variants require fine-grained descriptions, creating information and tool asymmetry. 

Grid Coordination (Figure 2, top-right) tests joint action coor-dination [13]. Both agents must reproduce a target pattern on a 3 ×3grid within 30 turns, but Agent A can only place X and Agent B can only place O. Each conversation generates a new random starting grid and a new random target, both with balanced symbol counts (4 of one symbol, 5 of the other), yielding a large number of unique configurations. Both agents see the same grid, creating tool asym-metry with shared visual information. 

Scene Comparison (Figure 2, bottom-left) tests visual perspec-tive-taking [18, 19]. Agent A and Agent B each see a different version of the same scene, with two objects removed from each version (four total differences). The goal is for the pair to identify all differences between their two scenes. Each conversation randomly selects from a library of 11 validated scene configurations spanning diverse environments (kitchens, living rooms, offices, gardens, etc.), each with pre-defined asymmetric object removals. Both agents share the same reporting tools, creating visual and information asymmetry with symmetric tool access. 

Map Task (Figure 2, bottom-right) adapts the director–matcher structure of the HCRC Map Task [2]. Agent A sees the full map with obstacles and destination but cannot move. Agent B can move in cardinal directions but cannot see the map. The goal is for Agent B to reach the destination using Agent A’s verbal directions. Each conversation generates a random map on a 5 ×5 grid with 4 to 7 procedurally placed obstacles, validated via breadth-first search to guarantee a solvable path from origin to destination. This creates visual, information, and tool asymmetry simultaneously. 

## 3.2 Architecture 

PARLEY uses a turn-based orchestrator built on the OpenAI Re-sponses API that constructs each agent’s prompt from conversa-tion history, role-specific system prompt, private information, and tool definitions (Table 1). Agents perceive the task by invoking a view_interface tool that captures a Playwright screenshot of their current view, and communicate via a shared text channel, forcing verbalization of spatial and perceptual content. Tool asym-metry is enforced at the schema level: each agent receives only its designated function tools. All interactions are logged in structured JSON with automatic success evaluation and failure classification (Section 4). 

Turn management. The orchestrator alternates between Agent A and Agent B. On each turn, an agent may freely invoke up to 10 tool calls (view the interface, perform task actions, and communicate with its partner) in any order. This unconstrained tool access is more realistic than a rigid phase structure: agents must decide when to observe, when to act, and when to communicate, mirroring the self-directed nature of real collaborative work. The per-turn tool call limit prevents runaway loops while allowing agents sufficient freedom to observe, act, and communicate within a single turn. 

Logging and reproducibility. Each conversation produces a struc-tured JSON log containing the full prompt history, tool call argu-ments and results, screenshots at every turn, and the final task out-come. Logs include timing metadata (API latency, total conversation duration) and model configuration (model version, temperature, system prompt). This enables post-hoc analysis at any granularity, from aggregate statistics to individual turn-level inspection. 

Extensibility. All task configurations are randomized across con-versations (target sequences, grid patterns, scene selections, and map layouts) to ensure findings generalize beyond specific instances. The platform is designed for community extension: new tasks re-quire only a task definition file (specifying roles, tools, success criteria, and difficulty parameters) and a web interface, with no changes to the orchestrator or analysis pipeline. 

## 4 Method 

To establish baseline performance, we conducted 400 conversations using GPT-4o across all four tasks (100 per task) with random-ized configurations. Each conversation is evaluated on binary task completion (success/failure) and number of turns for successful completion. 

## 4.1 Experimental Setup 

Each conversation pairs two GPT-4o instances with role-specific system prompts (Agent A and Agent B) and proceeds turn-by-turn until either the task is completed successfully or the maximum turn budget of 30 turns is reached. Agents can capture screenshots of the task interface by invoking the view_interface tool, providing visual context alongside the text conversation history. Temperature is set to 0.7 to allow variability across conversations while maintain-ing coherent behavior. The 30-turn budget was selected through pilot sessions. We selected GPT-4o [29] as our study model because it supports both multimodal input (enabling visual task perception) and function calling (enabling tool-mediated actions), capabilities essential for studying collaboration across diverse asymmetry types. It is important to note that the goal of this study is not to compare models but to use a single capable model to systematically study collaboration dynamics. Future work may consider more thorough evaluation of different models and how collaborative performance evolves over time. 

## 4.2 Collaboration Analysis 

To analyze failure patterns in the conversations, we followed an LLM-as-a-judge approach using GPT-4o as the judge. For each of the 400 conversations, we prompted the model to extract all distinct cognitive errors or problematic behaviors as structured JSON (Figure 3). Extraction was applied to both successful and unsuccessful conversations to ensure methodological consistency. To prevent task-specific vocabulary from driving cluster formation, the task-agnostic abstraction instructs the model to describe failures in general language (e.g., “The agents repeated a failed approach without variation” rather than “kept trying the same grid pattern”). To derive the taxonomy, we embedded each instance’s task-agnostic description using text-embedding-3-large (3,072 di-mensions) through the Azure OpenAI API. ## 4.3 Intervention Design 

To test whether the taxonomy enables actionable interventions, we designed a set of collaboration repair instructions appended to both agents’ system prompts, with one directive per failure pattern identified in the taxonomy (Section 5, Figure 6). No other changes were made to the infrastructure, model, or task configurations. We ran 200 intervention conversations (50 per task). 

## 4.4 Evaluation 

We use the Wilcoxon rank-sum test on pattern counts to assess each failure pattern’s association with outcome, with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons across all six patterns. To model the joint relationship between failure patterns and conversation outcomes while controlling for task effects, we fit a linear mixed-effects model (LMM) with conversation outcome (successful or unsuccessful) as the dependent variable, the presence of each of the failure pattern types as fixed effects, and task as a random intercept. For the intervention experiment, we use Fisher’s exact test to compare success rates between baseline and intervention conditions.

## 6 Results 6.1 How Do Agents Perform Across Collaborative Tasks? 

Across 400 controlled GPT-4o conversations (100 per task), overall success is 60.5%. Task-level analysis reveals meaningful variation in difficulty. Scene Comparison achieves the highest success (80.0%), where the primary challenge lies in comparing two different visual scenes. Grid Coordination follows at 73.0%, where both agents share the same visual information and the challenge is tool coordination (who places X vs. O) rather than information sharing. Map Task succeeds at 55.0%, reflecting the compounding difficulty of simultaneous visual, information, and tool asymmetry: the director must translate spatial knowledge into step-by-step navigation commands for a partner who cannot see the map. Tangram Reference achieves only 34.0%, requiring agents to describe abstract visual symbols with sufficient precision for a partner who cannot see them. Performance degrades sharply with conversation length: all con-versations completed in ≤6 turns succeed, but conversations still ongoing at turn 12 have only a 37% chance of eventual success, and those still ongoing at turn 20 succeed just 19% of the time. Conver-sations that reach the 30-turn limit never succeed, establishing a practical “point of no return” well before the cap. This pattern sug-gests that early turns are critical: if agents cannot establish effective grounding quickly, subsequent turns tend to compound rather than resolve misalignment. Figure 7 shows the distribution of turns to completion for suc-cessful conversations across the four tasks. Grid Coordination re-solves fastest when it succeeds (Mdn = 4, 𝑀 = 6.0, 𝑆𝐷 = 4.6), with a tight distribution suggesting that successful pairs quickly con-verge on a coordination strategy. Scene Comparison also resolves quickly (Mdn = 5 turns, 𝑀 = 8.0, 𝑆𝐷 = 6.3), likely reflecting both the relative simplicity of visual comparison and agents’ ability to con-verge once they establish what to look for. Tangram Reference shows the widest spread (Mdn = 13, 𝑀 = 14.7, 𝑆𝐷 = 8.4), reflecting the variable difficulty of describing abstract shapes to a partner who cannot see them. Map Task demands the most turns when it does succeed (Mdn = 19, 𝑀 = 20.1, 𝑆𝐷 = 3.2), consistent with its compounding asymmetry requiring sustained coordination across many navigation steps. Overall, these distributions reflect a joint function of task difficulty and model capability: the tasks are chal-lenging enough that a substantial fraction of conversations reach the 30-turn limit without completing, yet sufficiently tractable that successful strategies can be observed and characterized.

## 7 Discussion 

Motivated by cognitive science research on human collaboration, we evaluated GPT-4o as an agent collaboratively solving four asym-metric tasks, achieving 60.5% overall success. Through systematic analysis of these conversations, the PAC taxonomy revealed six failure patterns that differentially characterize collaboration out-comes, and a taxonomy-guided intervention improved average task success to 70.5%, illustrating the diagnostic utility of the framework. Here we discuss the implications of these findings. Agent failures can be interpreted in relation to prior human-collaboration research. In human collaboration, a well-documented challenge is egocentric bias: people consistently privilege their own perspective during joint tasks, even when they know their part-ner holds different information [12, 18, 19]. In PARLEY’s excerpts, agents sometimes show excessive compliance, capitulating rather than defending their (sometimes correct) position when partners disagree. This compliance tendency may contribute to several of the PAC failure patterns, particularly Feedback Integration (A2), where agents accept partner suggestions without critically eval-uating them, and Grounding (C2), where agents confirm shared understanding prematurely rather than probing for misalignment. A related consideration concerns the expression of uncertainty. Human collaborators naturally signal confusion, hesitation, and doubt through hedging language, questions, and explicit statements of uncertainty, which serve as critical coordination cues for their partners [9, 32]. Some PARLEY excerpts instead show confident-sounding responses even when agents’ capabilities (such as visual interpretation) may not be reliable or their environment represen-tation is ambiguous. This is not simply a stylistic difference: the absence of uncertainty signals deprives the partner of information needed to calibrate certainty, offer help, or adjust strategy. Several of our failure patterns, particularly Grounding (C2) and Feedback Integration (A2), may be amplified by failures to surface confusion as a communicative act. Human collaborators, when a strategy fails, naturally explore alternatives: trying different descriptions, reframing the problem, or switching roles [32]. In our excerpts, agents exhibit Feedback Integration failures (A2), reapplying the same failed strategy with minor surface-level variations. This pattern is consistent with the “passive responder” characterization of Wu et al. [37] and suggests opportunities to support explicit monitoring of strategy effective-ness. While grounding theory [9] documents how humans naturally adapt when communication fails, some PARLEY agents persist with failed strategies even when tool feedback explicitly indicates failure, a rigidity consistent with the Einstellung effect [26]. These observations suggest that interventions designed for hu-man collaboration failures (e.g., perspective-taking prompts to counter egocentric bias) may need adaptation for LLM agents [3]. The PAC taxonomy provides concrete intervention points for collab-orative behavior: from how agents perceive their environment, to how they adapt their strategies, to how they coordinate with their partner, and the results of our intervention experiment support the diagnostic value of this framework. Despite the encouraging findings, this work has several limita-tions. For instance, we evaluate agent-agent interactions on four controlled tasks that may offer limited generalization to noisier real-life collaborative settings. While this controlled setup allowed us to study failure patterns of agentic collaboration under controlled conditions, future work may consider more realistic scenarios. Also, it is important to highlight that the PAC taxonomy has been de-rived from a single model (GPT-4o), which means different model families may exhibit additional failure patterns not captured by our six-pattern scheme. While we believe some of the patterns will translate to other models, we envision the same methodology can be readily applied to characterize their specific failure patterns and enable systematic cross-architecture comparison. The platform and analysis pipeline are reusable, while measured failure frequencies and intervention gains characterize GPT-4o in these tasks. Looking ahead, this work illustrates a cycle of simulating, analyz-ing, and improving collaborative behavior through system-prompt updates. We explore one researcher-guided iteration, but future work could extend this process into longer, agent-directed cycles as a step toward recursive self-improvement. Agents could syn-thesize tasks that reflect collaboration difficulties encountered in real-world settings, use PARLEY to compare alternative strategies or instructions, and revise their own collaboration prompts based on the resulting evidence. Evaluating revisions on held-out tasks would help establish whether these cycles produce improvements beyond the situations used to generate them. Ultimately, a broader vision for PARLEY is to draw on human collaboration research to inspire better communication and coor-dination among people and agents. This raises a central design question: how do the strategies that support effective agent–agent collaboration differ from those that help people understand, guide, and collaborate with an agent? Future studies could use and extend PARLEY to compare human–human, agent–agent, and human– agent interactions, examining which practices transfer across these settings and which benefit from adaptation. By connecting these forms of collaboration, PARLEY could help turn advances in agent coordination into more effective and understandable human–AI partnerships.
