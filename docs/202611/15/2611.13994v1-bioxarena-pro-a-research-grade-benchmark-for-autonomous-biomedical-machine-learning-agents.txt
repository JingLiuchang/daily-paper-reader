Abstract 

Coding agents are increasingly proposed as autonomous builders of biomedical models, but existing benchmarks are small, near saturation or open to leaked information. We present BioXArena-Pro, 64 research-level modelling tasks from eight biomedical domains, each rebuilt from a published study; most hold out whole biological units such as species or cohorts. Across 22 agent configurations, the best scores 55.1 of 100, and the scaffold matters almost as much as the model. We re-ran a published method for every task: where the published recipe applies unchanged, the best agent and the methods tie; on rebuilt tasks the methods trail by 8.2 points, suggesting that agents gain by adapting to new data. Pretrained models (including biological foundation models) matter where the strongest agent uses them: forbidding them decreases its score on those tasks by 9 points. Every task was audited in several AI-assisted rounds and a final human review. 

Keywords: AI agents, biomedical machine learning, benchmark, foundation models, distribution shift, evaluation 

Large language model (LLM) agents can now inspect data, write and debug code, train models and iterate on results with little human input 1–3 , and in biomedicine they are being proposed as research assistants and even autonomous scientists 4–6 . Whether this 1arXiv:2611.13994v1 [cs.LG] 10 Nov 2026 promise holds depends on measurement: not whether an agent produces a plausible-looking model, but whether its held-out performance reflects real modelling ability on the data that biomedical researchers actually face. Current evaluations fall short in two ways. First, most scientific and biomedical agent benchmarks test knowledge, tool use or analysis traces rather than end-to-end model building 7–9 , and the existing model-building benchmarks are small, drawn from competitions, or limited to clean tabular inputs and random splits 10–12 . NatureBench tests whether coding agents can match the published state of the art of Nature-family papers across the sciences 13 ; it inspired our published-method references, which focus on biomedical machine learning. Our earlier benchmark, BioXArena (76 tasks in nine domains) 14 , broadened the coverage, but it is approaching saturation: on the common scale used here, the two strongest configurations already average 66 of 100, and on 12 of its 76 tasks the best of four configurations comes within 10 points of the maximum. Second, a coding agent does more than fit a model. It reads the task files, searches the data for regularities and tests what the grader rewards, so it will find and use any leaked label, metadata artefact or grader loophole left in a benchmark, and the result-ing score will overstate its ability 15–17 . Biological data are especially prone to such leaks, through homology or similarity between training and test sequences and struc-tures, entities shared across splits and artefacts of data generation 18–20 . A benchmark for these agents must therefore be harder than current ones, and it must be checked for shortcuts before release. Here we present BioXArena-Pro (Fig. 1). Each of its 64 tasks reproduces a research-grade modelling problem from a published study or database and keeps the distribution shift, data volume and native formats of the original work; examples are predicting perturbation responses in unseen cell lines and transferring protein-interaction predic-tors across species. Compared with BioXArena, the median task carries about twice as much training data, and most tasks hold out an entire biological unit, such as a species, cohort, donor or cell line, rather than random rows 21 . Every task is scored on a common 0–100 scale on which higher is better, and all capability results come from the final release. We report three results. First, we evaluated 22 configurations: Codex CLI 22 ,Claude Code 23 and Gemini CLI 24 , each with two of its vendor’s models, and four model-agnostic scaffolds crossed with four models. The best configuration reaches 55.1 of 100 (54.5 on average over three runs), no task is solved to saturation, and the scaf-fold around a model matters almost as much as the model itself. Second, we re-ran a published method for every task under our protocol. For 33 tasks this is a method published for the task’s dataset; for the other 31 it is the strongest published method for the same problem class (Methods). These references match the strongest agent where our test set stays close to the original study, and they trail it by 8.2 points on the tasks that we rebuilt against contamination. A published recipe is tuned to the data for which it was designed, whereas the agents tailor validation, normalization and ensembling to each new split. Third, agents load pretrained models, including bio-logical foundation models 25,26 , in 18% of their solutions. Where the strongest agent uses them, they carry real signal: forbidding them decreases its score on those tasks by 9 points. # 1 Results 

## 1.1 A research-grade benchmark for biomedical modelling 

BioXArena-Pro comprises 64 tasks, eight per domain, across chemical biology, imag-ing, network biology, perturbation biology, phenotype–disease modelling, sequence and genomics, single-cell and spatial omics, and structural biology (Fig. 1a, Supplemen-tary Table 1). Each task is a self-contained capsule: a public directory with training data, unlabelled test inputs, native-format assets and a description; test labels are kept private. An agent receives the capsule, one GPU and a two-hour budget, and must return predictions for the test set (Methods). ## 1.2 Capability of 22 agent configurations on the final release 

On the final release we evaluated 22 agent configurations in two designs (Fig. 2; Methods). The first design pairs each of three vendor coding command-line interfaces (CLIs), namely Codex CLI, Claude Code and Gemini CLI, with two backbones from its own vendor. The second design crosses four model-agnostic scaffolds with four back-bones. The scaffolds are an open-source coding CLI (OpenCode 27 ), a biomedical agent (Biomni 4), a search-based agent for machine-learning engineering (MLEvolve 28 ) and a minimal scaffold that sends a single prompt; the backbones are the newer model of each vendor CLI and an open-weight model, DeepSeek V4.1 Flash. Codex CLI with GPT-6 Astra reaches 55.1 (95% confidence interval, CI, 50.0–59.8), followed by Claude Code with Opus 5.5 and by OpenCode with GPT-6 Astra or Opus 5.5 (all 53.2); the minimal scaffold with DeepSeek V4.1 Flash is last at 33.6 (Fig. 2a). Domain matters about as much as configuration: domain means range from 36.4 for perturbation biol-ogy to 56.4 for imaging (Fig. 2b). Cost varies more than score: agent time ranges from 11 to 127 hours and input tokens from 0.3 to 279 million, with little relation to rank (Fig. 2a,e; Supplementary Table 4). In the four-by-four design, OpenCode is the best and the minimal scaffold the worst scaffold for every backbone (Fig. 2c); across the 16 cells, the backbone accounts for 53% of the variance in mean score and the scaffold for 37%. A full coding scaffold adds more as the backbone weakens, from 6.7 points with GPT-6 Astra and Opus 5.5 to 14.2 with DeepSeek V4.1 Flash. As a result, the open-weight backbone in OpenCode (47.8) is level with both frontier backbones in the minimal scaffold (46.5; the difference is within the CIs). On their own backbone, vendor CLIs hold little advantage over the open-source CLI (Fig. 2d): Codex CLI exceeds OpenCode by 1.9 points (95% CI 1.1–2.7), and Claude Code and Gemini CLI differ from it by 0.0 and −1.1. Within a vendor CLI, the newer backbone scores 9.9 points higher for GPT-6 Astra over GPT-5.6 Sol and 5.3 points higher for Gemini 3.8 Flash over 3.1 Pro (a comparison that also crosses model tiers), but only 0.3 points higher for Opus 5.5 over Opus 5. More search did not help within the two-hour budget. MLEvolve iteratively mutates and re-evaluates candidate solutions. With every backbone it spent about 127 agent-hours, four times as much as Codex CLI with GPT-6 Astra (Fig. 2e), yet it never beat the best coding CLI on the same backbone: it trailed by 8–9 points with GPT-6 Astra and DeepSeek V4.1 Flash and did not differ significantly with Gemini 3.8 Flash ( −3.4) or Opus 5.5 ( −0.5). Picking the best agent for each task would add little: the per-task best of all 22 configurations averages 57.0, only 1.9 points above the best single configuration, and the per-task winners are spread over 12 configurations. ## 1.3 Agents against reproduced published-method references 

To interpret absolute scores, we compared the agents with a reproduced published-method reference for every task (Fig. 3). For 33 tasks (tier 1) we re-ran a method published for the task’s dataset. For the other 31 (tier 2) we re-ran the strongest published method for the same problem class, because no dataset-specific method exists, none could be run legitimately or a same-class method validated better. Each method used the authors’ code and training recipe, was trained only on the public training data and used no checkpoint whose training data overlap the test samples; the primary method was chosen by validation on training data and scored with the official grader (Methods). These references (median publication year 2024) reach 48.6 on average, below Codex CLI with GPT-6 Astra (55.1) and Claude Code with Opus 5.5 (53.2); the agent is higher on 53 of 64 tasks (Fig. 3a). The gap is concentrated in the tasks that we rebuilt against contamination (Fig. 3b). Nine tasks combine a method published for the dataset with a test set close to the original study, so the published recipe applies unchanged; we treat them as the published-method baseline. ## 1.4 Pretrained models help where agents already use them 

We classified the final code of the 1,401 runs that left code (Fig. 4a). Deep learning appears in 52% of runs, gradient-boosted trees in 45% and linear, forest or kernel models in 39%, but only 18% of runs load pretrained weights, including biological 8foundation models. How often a configuration uses pretrained models depends on both the backbone and the scaffold: Opus 5.5 loads them in 22–27% of runs in every scaffold, whereas GPT-6 Astra does so in 19% of runs in Codex CLI but in 2% in Biomni and in the minimal scaffold. To measure what they are worth, we reran Codex CLI with GPT-6 Astra on all 64 tasks with two modified prompts: one forbidding all pretrained weights and embed-dings, and one pointing the agent to a local zoo of 99 pretrained models (Methods). The two arms average 52.7 and 54.6 against 55.1 for the default prompt (54.5 on average over three runs), and the effect is asymmetric. On the 14 tasks whose default solution already relied on a pretrained model, forbidding it cost 9.3 points on average (lower on 13 of 14, median −4.9, Wilcoxon P = 0 .0002), and up to 41.5 points for image–caption retrieval without BiomedCLIP 31 (Fig. 4b). On the eight tasks where the default solution used none and the encouraging prompt made the agent adopt one, scores changed by +2 .0 on average, four up and four down ( P = 0 .31); only cross-species interaction prediction on the PRING-derived task gained clearly (+13 .3 with ESM2 and ProtT5 32 ; Fig. 4c). On the 42 tasks with no pretrained model in either arm the differences had an s.d. of 3.6 points, so single-task changes of a few points are noise. For this agent, pretrained models thus carry real signal on about a fifth of the tasks, and it already finds them there; telling it to use more does not help on aver-age. The grouping is post hoc and each arm is a single run, so the per-task values are indicative. 

## 1.5 Frontier agents find and exploit integrity defects 

Before release we audited every task in four rounds, one manual and three AI-assisted, each followed by expert verification of the flagged problems, and closed with a final expert review of every task and fix (Fig. 5a; Methods). The rounds confirmed 168 defects in 62 of the 64 tasks (82 serious), most often identifiers or public files that let a networked agent retrieve the answer, train–test overlap through homology or shared entities, and tasks that a trivial baseline saturated (Fig. 5b). Agents exploited such defects without instruction: of 37 runs examined because of an anomalous score or submission, 12 clearly exploited a defect and five in part (Fig. 5d shows the 13 of these 17 with a comparable rerun); of the AI auditor’s 34 serious claims, verification confirmed nine (Fig. 5c). Claude Code with Opus 5.5 reached a perfect 100 on a pose-classification task by learning a bond-length artefact that separated decoy from native poses, and Codex CLI with GPT-6 Astra scored 72.8 on a network-completion task by reading gaps in element numbering (56.4 after the fix). Across the 13 clear or partial cases with a comparable rerun, the fix lowered the same configuration’s score by 13.1 points on average (range 0.5–34.9). These scores after a fix come from the task version of that round, which later rounds changed further. Across all tasks fixed without changing the metric, scores fell by 3.9–7.1 points (Fig. 5e), a shift that also includes other hardening of the rebuilt tasks. Because only runs with anomalous scores were examined, these counts do not estimate how often exploitation occurs; they show that it occurs without instruction and is hard to detect from aggregate scores. This is why all capability results use the final release. 92 Discussion 

Coding agents can already build credible models for many biomedical problems: on the final release they produce valid submissions in 97% of the 1,408 runs (22 configurations on 64 tasks), and the strongest configurations exceed reproduced published methods on most tasks. But the same competence makes them dangerous to evaluate naively: agents found and used label leakage, metadata artefacts and grader loopholes that had passed expert curation. We draw four lessons. First, a benchmark for data-reading agents needs opaque identifiers, controlled metadata, group-aware splits, shortcut baselines and graders that cannot be gamed, and it needs an adversarial audit before release; AI auditors are valuable red-teamers, but they over-report severity, so their claims need verification against the real grading path. Second, what the benchmark measures is adaptation rather than reuse: published methods hold their own where our test sets stay close to the original studies and fall behind where we introduced new splits, inputs or metrics, which is also where biomedical models matter most, in new cohorts, species and cell lines. Third, a score belongs to a scaffold–backbone pair: a weaker backbone in a strong scaffold can match a stronger backbone in a weak one. Fourth, pretrained models matter on about a fifth of the tasks and add little elsewhere; the strongest agent already uses them where they help, so the frontier is better judgement about when and how to use them, not more encouragement. The results also show where the frontier lies. The per-task best of all 22 configura-tions is only 1.9 points above the best single configuration, so the remaining 43 points are not recoverable by picking among these agents. Perturbation biology and network biology stay below 41 on average, and three tasks remain under 20 for every configu-ration. These include problems that remain open in the field, such as predicting the effects of unseen genetic perturbations 33 or responses in unseen cell lines. Without noise ceilings, however, we cannot separate unsolved biology from label noise or from difficulty added by removing identifiers. More search within the same per-task limit does not close this gap either: the search-based scaffold spent four times the agent-hours of the best coding CLI for no gain. The lead of Codex CLI with GPT-6 Astra persists on three-run means (54.5 against 53.5), whereas the next five configurations are indistinguishable. We expect progress to come from agents that reason about the data-generating process, choose or adapt domain models deliberately and verify their own validation design. Our study has limitations. Lookup of public source data cannot be fully prevented when agents have network access: opaque identifiers and explicit prohibitions limit it, but inputs such as sequences or structures can themselves be searched, and network traffic was not captured. An audit of the logs of all 1,408 runs found no download of a source dataset and no database query with test inputs, but 40 runs used public checkpoints whose training data may overlap our test samples, and this overlap was not measured (Methods). The 0–100 scale is an index rather than a fraction solved. An uninformed submission scores zero on 36 tasks, 50 on the six AUC-type tasks and 8.5 on average over the 63 tasks where chance is defined (Supplementary Table 6), and no task has a measured noise ceiling; means across tasks and domains should therefore be read comparatively. De-identification removes prior knowledge that a domain method 10 or a foundation model could legitimately use, which may lower both the references and the value of pretrained models. Backbones were served through different API routes. Most configurations were run once per task, and the differences among the top configurations are smaller than their bootstrap intervals. We did not measure human experts directly. The references are, however, expert-designed: each is a method that domain experts devised, tuned and published for that dataset or problem class, and where a published number on the same test set exists our reproductions match it (Methods). What a reference lacks is the adaptation an expert would make on meeting our rebuilt split, so it is best read as a lower bound on expert performance. The nine baseline tasks, where the published recipe needs no adaptation, give the most direct comparison: there the strongest agent and the published methods tie (54.9 against 54.7). The evidence therefore supports agents reaching the level of published methods on these problems, not exceeding what experts could achieve with the same freedom to adapt. The audits used models of the same families as the evaluated agents, and tasks were revised after agent runs had been inspected, so the final tasks may be harder for these families than for others. The prompt ablation covers one agent, and the 64 tasks sample eight domains. BioXArena-Pro turns these lessons into a benchmark on which a score reflects modelling rather than lookup. As agents improve, new shortcuts will appear, so we release it as a versioned, living benchmark with a public audit log, an evaluation server that keeps test labels private and the tooling needed to audit new tasks. 

# 3 Methods 

## 3.1 Task selection and construction 

We first compiled a candidate list of datasets from recent primary studies in Nature, Science, Cell and their sister journals and from the benchmarks of recent machine-learning venues, keeping those that release a dataset together with a method and an evaluation. Three authors (L.L., D.Z. and X.D.) downloaded, processed and screened the candidates against five criteria. The source had to be a primary study or curated database, preferably from the last few years (median source year 2024) and with a peer-reviewed publication where one exists. Its licence had to permit academic non-commercial benchmark use. The prediction target had to be scientifically meaningful, with a real downstream use. The task had to be difficult: we preferred tasks that combine several input modalities or data sources and whose held-out unit (species, cohort, donor, cell line, scaffold or time window) matches the scientific claim of the original study. Finally, the task had to fit one GPU and two hours. Candidates that failed a criterion, duplicated a problem class already covered or saturated under a trivial baseline were dropped or replaced during the integrity rounds described below. The resulting 64 tasks, eight per domain, were reviewed at a high level by the two senior authors (K.Z. and Le Song) for relevance, balance and challenge. BioXArena-Pro is not disjoint from BioXArena. Of the 64 tasks, 45 share no source dataset with it, 8 reuse a BioXArena dataset for a different prediction task, and 11 revisit a BioXArena dataset and target with a new split, inputs and metric (six as whole tasks and five as one component of a multi-source task). In five of these 11 tasks, 11 some test samples appear with labels, under other identifiers, in the public training data of BioXArena: about 55% of the test items of the sci-Plex task and of the Tox21 instance, and 7–14% of those for Swiss-Prot function, two ClinVar instances and the ProteinGym stability assays. Each task keeps the native data formats of its source and exposes a fixed primary metric chosen to match the scientific output. Per-task provenance, licence, split rule, modalities and published-method reference are listed in Supplementary Tables 1–3. 

## 3.2 Data sources, licences and redistribution 

Every task is built from a public primary study or database, listed with its licence in Supplementary Table 2 and, except for the ten tasks noted below, cited in its descrip-tion. We followed the policy of BioXArena: the packaging, splits, opaque identifiers, descriptions and graders are released under CC BY-NC 4.0 for academic and other non-commercial use; sources must permit academic non-commercial benchmark use; and sources whose terms restrict redistribution of the original records are released only as processed, de-identified task files, with the original resource cited and left to be obtained from its provider. Each task’s data remain subject to the terms of its source. Three sources need a specific note. The MSK-MET task is reproduced value for value from the authors’ Zenodo deposit of the cohort (CC BY 4.0); because the same study is distributed through cBioPortal, whose DataHub requires derived databases to be shared alike, the files of this task and the cBioPortal-derived files of the paediatric leukaemia task are offered under ODbL 1.0. The leukaemia task uses only open-access data of the TARGET programme, for which NCI requires no data-use certification; the TARGET purpose limitations apply to controlled-access data, and use follows the NIH Genomic Data Sharing Policy. The ionic-liquid conductivity values of one task derive from NIST Standard Reference Database 147 (ILThermo); they are shipped as pro-cessed values for non-commercial research with the acknowledgement NIST requests, and permission for redistribution has been requested from the NIST Standard Ref-erence Data Program. Ten task descriptions deliberately withhold the name of their source to limit answer lookup; their sources are disclosed in Supplementary Table 2. 

## 3.3 Ethics and data governance 

This study analysed only previously published, de-identified data and involved no human participants: we recruited no participants, generated no new human or animal data and had no contact with any data subject. The 27 tasks that include human-derived data (clinical cohorts, tumour profiles, medical images, tissue sections and cell atlases) use only resources that their providers distribute openly in de-identified form, collected under the ethical approvals and consent procedures reported in the original studies (Supplementary Table 2); no controlled-access data were used. We made no attempt to re-identify individuals or to link records across sources. The released task files go further than the sources: sample, patient and donor identifiers are replaced by opaque codes, free-text and date fields are removed, and clinical variables are kept only where the task needs them and in the coarsened form in which the source released them. Users of the benchmark must comply with the terms of each source, and we will 12 remove or replace any task on request of a data provider. Two tasks on oligonucleotide knockdown efficacy triggered a model’s biosafety refusal (see Agent configurations and execution environment); no task involves pathogen enhancement, toxin design or other dual-use content. 

## 3.4 Task capsules, submission contract and scoring 

Every task is a self-contained capsule with a public and a private part. The pub-lic directory holds description.md , train.csv , test.csv , sample submission.csv 

and the task’s native-format assets (images, volumes, structures, expression matrices, graphs or embeddings). For 43 tasks the prediction is a value per test row; the other 21 use a file manifest, in which each row of the submission names a prediction file in the agent’s output directory so that predictions keep their natural granularity (for example a mask per image or an expression matrix per tissue section). The private directory holds answers.csv , any private label files and the grader. A submission must reproduce the sample submission’s rows, columns and identifier order; the sub-mission is invalid, and the run a failure rather than a valid submission with a low score, if a file is missing or malformed, the schema or identifier set is wrong, a path is unsafe or a required value is not finite. With a public pre-flight check, an agent can catch packaging errors before the deadline. Each grader computes one fixed primary metric; task instances that share a grader are averaged with equal weight unless the task description states other weights; the metric, its aggregation and the measured chance level of every task are given in Supplementary Table 6. Scores are normal-ized to a common 0–100 scale on which higher is better. Correlation coefficients and Matthews correlation are clipped to [0 , 1], so that chance scores zero, as it does for the skill metrics. AUC, C-index, average precision, F1, accuracy and mean reciprocal rank keep their native [0 , 1] scale. Two perturbation tasks are expressed relative to the all-zero prediction, and the hospitalization forecast uses a ranked probability skill score. All scores are multiplied by 100. A failed run scores 0. For Fig. 1e the 28 correlation-scored tasks of BioXArena were rescored from the raw metric with the same clip so that both benchmarks share one scale. 

## 3.5 Integrity hardening protocol 

Every task was audited in four rounds before release (Fig. 5a). AI agents did the search-ing in three of them; an author verified every AI finding and approved every fix. Round 0 was a manual review of the first version by the authors who constructed the tasks. Round 1 was a systematic scan with Claude Code and Opus 5.5 for identifier leakage, train–test overlap and metadata–label correlations. Round 2 was a second, broader systematic scan with Claude Code and Fable 5.1, extended to lookup of identifiers in public databases, file-order and file-metadata leakage, homology and scaffold over-lap, target-derived features and grader loopholes. Round 3 was an adversarial audit by a different agent, Codex CLI with GPT-6 Astra, which attacked the real grader of each task. In addition, anomalous agent scores and submissions from the evaluation runs were traced back to their cause. After the last round, the authors manually re-examined all 64 tasks — data files, splits, descriptions and graders — and signed off each one before the final release. 13 The rounds confirmed 168 defects in 62 of the 64 tasks (82 serious, 69 moderate, 17 minor), counted once per mechanism per task. The largest classes were identifiers or public files that let a networked agent retrieve the answer (30), train–test overlap through homology or shared entities (30), tasks that a trivial baseline saturated (22), metadata or target-derived features correlated with labels (16) and grader loopholes (16). Most defects were fixed by rebuilding the task, the others by changing the grader or rewriting the description; four tasks were retired and replaced, and one instance was withdrawn from another task. The 168 defects include eight in the four retired tasks; the 62 of 64 refers to the 64 tasks of the first version, and a defect found through agent behaviour is assigned to the round in which it was found. The fixes follow fixed rules. Identifiers are replaced by opaque salted codes, and lookup keys (gene symbols, variant strings, compound names and structures, accession numbers) are removed from test items. File order is shuffled and file metadata made uniform. Test sets are re-split so that an entire group of related samples is held out, with bounded sequence identity or disjoint scaffolds between training and test sets where applicable (Supplementary Table 1). Graders require exact coverage of the test identifiers and reject non-finite values and abstention, and descriptions state which external data and lookups are not allowed. The 37 examined runs were selected because a score or a submission was anomalous; each was classified from its transcript and code, and the comparison in Fig. 5d uses the same configuration rerun on the fixed task. The round-3 auditor ran once per task in a sandbox with read-only access to the task’s public and private directories and its grader. It was asked to find ways of obtaining the labels or of scoring well without learning the biology, and to measure each against the real grader. Of its 34 serious claims, re-execution by an author through the real grading path confirmed nine, downgraded 23 whose gain was within noise and rejected two (Fig. 5c). Because defects are counted per affected task, round 3 contributes 14 serious defects to Fig. 5a: the nine confirmed claims, two further tasks that share a confirmed grader defect, one defect found by direct review of the task that the auditor’s safety filter declined, and two found by the authors while fixing. 

## 3.6 Agent configurations and execution environment 

We report 22 configurations in two designs. In the vendor-CLI design, each coding CLI runs with two backbones from its own vendor: Codex CLI 0.163.0 with GPT-6 Astra and GPT-5.6 Sol, Claude Code 2.1.302 with Opus 5.5 and Opus 5, and Gemini CLI with Gemini 3.8 Flash and Gemini 3.1 Pro (preview). In the scaffold–backbone design, four model-agnostic scaffolds — OpenCode 1.18.5, Biomni, MLEvolve and a minimal scaffold — each run with GPT-6 Astra, Opus 5.5, Gemini 3.8 Flash and DeepSeek V4.1 Flash, that is, with the newer backbone of each vendor CLI and one open-weight model. The minimal scaffold sends the task once and executes the single code block it returns, with no tools and no feedback, so it measures what a backbone achieves without an agent loop. Three further configurations on the public leaderboard (Codex CLI with GPT-6.1 Sol and the minimal scaffold with DeepSeek V4 Pro and with Gemini 3.1 Pro) fit neither design and are not analysed here. All configurations receive the same task prompt, one NVIDIA A100 GPU (80 GB), 16 cores of an AMD EPYC 7713P processor, 96 GB RAM and a 7,200 s wall-clock limit 14 per task. Each run is one Slurm job inside a filesystem sandbox, in which the task’s public directory is read-only and only the run’s own output directory is writable; labels and answer files are never on the agent’s filesystem. Network access is not blocked, because agents must reach the model API and may install packages and download pretrained weights; leakage of answers is instead prevented by three safeguards. First, the Landlock filesystem sandbox restricts an agent to reading its task’s public directory and the runtime libraries and to writing its own output directory, so private answers, graders, other tasks and earlier runs are outside its view. Second, the data are de-identified as described under Integrity hardening protocol, so that test items cannot be matched to their public source records; some tasks also rotate spatial coordinates or project embeddings at random. Third, the shared task prompt forbids access to private data, labels, answer files, graders and any non-public artefact and states that original data releases are out of bounds, and 62 of the 64 task descriptions also state which external data and lookups are not allowed for that task. A run that exceeds the time limit, returns a missing or invalid submission or is killed for exceeding memory scores 0. Infrastructure failures — gateway errors, empty completions from the serving API, network drops and runs killed by another job — are rerun, and the replaced attempts are archived with the release. Every API response is logged together with the model that served it, and a run is rejected if fewer than 95% of its responses come from the requested model. The main evaluation of the 22 configurations used 1,023 hours of agent wall-clock time, each run with one GPU allocated, and 2.54 billion input and 0.26 billion output tokens; the published-method references, the prompt ablation and the repeat runs are not included in these totals. GPT and Claude backbones were served through an institutional API proxy, and DeepSeek V4.1 Flash and Biomni with Gemini 3.8 Flash through OpenRouter. The other five Gemini configurations ran with the same harness, prompt, tasks, limits and hardware class through a gateway for the native Gemini protocol; ten of their runs failed on gateway errors or external process kills and were rerun, two of them (OpenCode) through OpenRouter. For MLEvolve with Opus 5.5, the proxy returned empty completions for two tasks (siRNA and antisense-oligonucleotide knockdown efficacy), so these two scores are taken from MLEvolve with Opus 5; without them the configuration’s mean is 51.3 instead of 52.7. On the same two tasks, Claude Code with Opus 5.5 declined on biosafety grounds, and the CLI’s built-in refusal fallback completed them with Opus 5; these are the only runs exempted from the 95% rule. 

## 3.7 Audit of network use 

Because agents had network access, we audited all 1,408 runs of the final release for network use, from the recorded transcripts, the executed code, the scripts that the agents wrote and the per-run download caches. Pattern matching produced candidate events, and every candidate that involved a data repository, a database, a web search or a code repository was read manually. In total 362 runs (26%) used the network: 84 installed packages and 313 requested pretrained weights (35 did both). No run down-loaded the source dataset of its task, accessed any public data repository (including that of BioXArena) or queried a public database with test inputs. The 313 runs include downloads that the final solution did not use, which is why the number exceeds the 15 18% of final solutions that load pretrained weights. Seven runs searched for the source study by name; six retrieved nothing, and one read the code repository and paper of the source benchmark without retrieving data and scored below the median of the other configurations on that task. Forty runs used a public checkpoint whose training data may overlap the test samples: inverse-folding models on the CATH test chains, generalist Cellpose models 34 on one of two test image sources, pathology encoders pre-trained on TCGA images (inputs only, no survival labels) and one peptide-sequencing checkpoint of unverified provenance. The task descriptions permit these model classes, the overlap was not measured, and the runs are reported without exclusion. The audit covers what the logs record; network traffic itself was not captured. 

## 3.8 Reproduced published-method references 

For each task we reproduced a published method. Tier 1 is a method published for the task’s dataset (33 tasks); tier 2 is the strongest published method for the same problem class, with only the input and output layers adapted (31 tasks). A task is tier 2 when no dataset-specific method exists (11 tasks), when none could be run legit-imately, or when a same-class method validated better. Methods were run with the authors’ code, version and training recipe and were trained only on the task’s public training data. Pretrained weights were allowed only when their training data contain none of our test samples; otherwise the model was retrained from our data. Excluded checkpoints include GNINA 35 , BepiPred-3.0 36 , the released PLM-interact models 37 ,STNeuroNet 38 , LocalTransform 39 and the Tahoe-specific perturbation models. Meth-ods without available training code, or whose released model had been trained on a resource that contains our labels, were not used; the code repository lists them per task. The authors implemented the references, partly with the help of AI coding agents, and every method directory records the paper, code commit, weights and their prove-nance, hyperparameters, deviations, validation score and runtime. One to six methods were run per task (median two). The primary method was chosen solely by validation on a split of the training data that mimics the shift between training and test data, and test labels were never used to select or tune a method. For the mutation-effect instance of the cross-species interaction task, fine-tuning was shortened from 50 to 23 epochs for compute reasons, with the epoch selected by development-set AUPR as in the original recipe. All 167 submissions were scored with the official grader. After a first scoring pass, we re-examined the nine tasks with the lowest reference scores for implementation errors; this is the only use made of test scores. We found and fixed configuration errors in five of them (a merge step that never ran, a tree depth tuned for a random split, a splice model chosen for the wrong organism, an unsuited classifier and an undertrained model) and confirmed the other four as genuine; the primary method was then re-selected by validation. We report the primary method; the best method per task by test score averages 49.0. Comparability with the original study was graded per task as close (same data and metric family; 9 tasks), rebuilt (new split, removed inputs or new metric; 48 tasks) or mixed (the grade differs between instances or applies to part of the task; 7 tasks). All nine close tasks have tier-1 methods. We call them the published-method baseline, because the published recipe applies to them without adaptation 16 (Supplementary Table 7), and we treat the other 55 references as lower bounds on expert performance. Publication years come from the method papers. Reproduction fidelity was checked for three methods with a published number on the same or nearly the same test set: LegNet (the primary method) reaches 82.1 on the yeast promoter task, whose test set is drawn from that of the DREAM 2022 challenge, against about 82 reported; DeepGO-SE 40 reaches 51.9 on Swiss-Prot function prediction against 52.9; and Suite2p 41 reaches an F1 of 0.504 on the real calcium-imaging instance against the organisers’ 0.499. 

## 3.9 Pretrained-model use and prompt ablation 

Model families were detected by regular expressions over the Python files that each run left in its output directory, excluding vendored packages: deep-learning frameworks, gradient-boosted tree libraries, linear, forest and kernel estimators, and the loading of pretrained weights (library loaders, hub downloads and names of common biological and vision models). Seven runs left no code and are excluded. The prompt ablation reran Codex CLI with GPT-6 Astra on all 64 tasks of the final release with the same harness, budget and hardware as the main evaluation. It used two modified prompts: the forbid prompt bans all pretrained weights and pretrained embeddings, including embeddings shipped with a task; the encourage prompt adds a paragraph recommending pretrained models and gives the path of a local zoo of 99 models, while stating that each task’s own rules take precedence. The default arm is the main run. Whether each solution loaded pretrained weights was decided by reading its code and transcript; the automatic detector missed two task-specific pretrained models (InstaNovo 42 and ProteinMPNN 43 with ESM2), which were corrected by hand. Compliance of the forbid arm was checked for all 64 runs; none loaded, downloaded or referenced pretrained weights, and the task-supplied ESM2 and IgFold embeddings were explicitly discarded. 

## 3.10 Statistical analysis 

Confidence intervals are 95% percentile intervals from 5,000 bootstrap resamples of the 64 tasks; differences between two configurations are bootstrapped paired by task. In the scaffold–backbone design, the shares of variance attributed to scaffold, backbone and interaction come from a two-way decomposition of the 16 configuration means (one value per cell). The per-task best is the mean over tasks of the highest score that any configuration reached on each task. To measure run-to-run variation, we ran Codex CLI with GPT-6 Astra and Claude Code with Opus 5.5 twice more on all 64 tasks, with the same prompt, pinned CLI versions, limits and API route as in the main round. We report the mean and s.d. of the three 64-task means and the median over tasks of the per-task s.d. (1.3 and 1.1 points). In both repeat runs of Claude Code, the refusal fallback completed the same two tasks as in the main round. We tested 15 comparisons between pairs of configurations, which follow from the two designs and were fixed before the repeat runs and the reference reproductions: each vendor CLI against OpenCode on the same backbone, OpenCode against the minimal scaffold for each backbone, the newer against the older backbone in each vendor CLI, MLEvolve 17 against the best coding CLI on each backbone, and the two strongest configurations against each other. For each comparison we report the mean paired difference, the number of tasks won and lost and a two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test over the 64 tasks, with Holm correction across the 15 comparisons (Supplementary Table 5). All differences reported as such in Results remain significant after correction ( PHolm <

0.05); the five comparisons described as small or not significant do not reach it. In the prompt ablation, the per-task score changes of each task group were tested against zero with a two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test. No other hypothesis tests were performed. 

## 3.11 Use of large language models 

Besides being the systems under evaluation, LLM-based coding agents assisted the authors in four ways, always under author supervision. They carried out the searching in the three AI-assisted audit rounds (Claude Code with Opus 5.5 and Fable 5.1, and Codex CLI with GPT-6 Astra; see Integrity hardening protocol). They partially helped implement the published-method references from the authors’ code and recipes. Claude Code with Opus 5.5 and Fable 5.2 assisted with analysis code, figures and reference checking. The same tools were used to edit the manuscript text. The authors checked every output, verified each reported number against the underlying data and take full responsibility for the content of this article.

S16 Supplementary Table 6 | Primary metric, normalization and chance level of each task. 

Definitions follow the grading code of the release. Every task score is finally clipped to [0 , 1] and reported ×100; an invalid submission (schema, identifier or value errors) scores 0 for the task, and 100 is the formal maximum of every metric (no noise ceiling was measured, so it need not be reachable in practice). “Chance” is the score of an uninformed submission on the 0–100 scale, measured with the released graders (constant or reference prediction for regression and ranking scores; uniformly random labels, random rankings or random scores otherwise; mean of 3–5 seeds). The tasks fall into two families. (i) Chance-zero metrics (36 tasks): correlations reported as clip( r, 0, 1), calibrated regression scores, R2, average precision or F1 expressed as a gain over label prevalence, skill scores and scores offset against a public reference prediction, together with exact-match and segmentation scores that have no chance agreement. (ii) Metrics kept on their native scale (28 tasks): ROC-AUC and the concordance index (chance 50), and macro-F1, accuracy, average precision, reciprocal rank, Fmax, sequence recovery and sensitivity at fixed specificity, whose chance level depends on the number of classes or candidates and is listed per task. r, Pearson correlation; ρ, Spearman correlation; AP, average precision; p, positive prevalence in the test set; SD, standard deviation; n.d., not defined. aAn input-independent training term-frequency prior scores 39.6 on this task. bExact counts have no natural chance level; the all-zero template scores 0. cThe training climatology scores 0; a uniform forecast scores 14.8.
