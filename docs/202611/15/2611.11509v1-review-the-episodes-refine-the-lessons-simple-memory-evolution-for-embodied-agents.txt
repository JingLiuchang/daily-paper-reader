ABSTRACT 

Recent studies of embodied agents based on frontier multimodal large language models (MLLMs) develop harness engineering around the MLLM, such as har-nesses with post-trained policies and recursive self-improvement loops. Yet whether these additions exceed the native capability of the MLLM remains un-explored. We introduce SimplEvo to explore how far an MLLM can improve under direct control when it retains its execution experience in self-written text memories. It requires no weight updates, post-trained policies, or privileged in-formation. The MLLM writes a coordinate-free task memory once from a single demonstration. Under a strict train–test protocol, it then creates a bounded work-ing memory on training episodes only and refines it after each episode with lessons on procedure, completion, and recovery. Both memories stay fixed at test time. On EBench, SimplEvo lifts the success rate (SR) of GPT-6-Astra under direct control from 46.7% to 81.7%, and tops the RoboDojo leaderboard with 69.63% SR and a score of 75.32. Compared with memories that also store past visual frames, SimplEvo reaches a similar SR with text memory alone while using fewer tokens. With its memories fixed, SimplEvo also generalizes to unseen layouts and ran-domized scene configurations. Moreover, the working memory transfers across models: written by GPT-6-Astra, it can be reused as is by weaker MLLMs and improve their performance without any adaptation of their own. arXiv:2611.11509v1 [cs.RO] 8 Nov 2026 Preprint 

1 INTRODUCTION 

Generalist robotic manipulation is a central problem of embodied intelligence. Vision-language-action models (VLAs) [2, 3, 25] and world action models (WAMs) [55, 61, 63] align their manipu-lation skills through post-training on benchmark-specific trajectories [5, 30, 38]. In contrast, recent studies show that frontier multimodal large language models (MLLMs) directly operate robots to complete tasks without parameter updates. They command the end effector from camera views and language instructions [21, 24, 31, 32, 40, 66], explore and adapt their behaviors to the observed results [9, 18], and recover from errors within an episode [12, 60]. These findings have motivated a growing range of embodied agents built on frontier MLLMs. These agents fall into two lines of work. The first is code-as-policies , which began with lan-guage models writing robot code [29] and composes perception modules and motion primitives into executable programs. In systems such as Code as Policies [29], COAP [22], and RAX on RoboDojo [23], the synthesized programs run at test time without the MLLM in the loop, so they cover only anticipated scene variations and failure modes. Recent works such as CaP-X [12] and PyRUA-Lean [47] therefore execute code online with the MLLM in the loop. Even so, code-as-policies systems typically rely on privileged information beyond RGB views and proprioception, such as metric depth, camera parameters, and object sizes, or on estimates from calibrated depth sensors and vision foundation models [26, 43, 58, 59], to which the resulting programs are sensitive. Frontier MLLMs, by contrast, already perceive and reason about space from RGB views and pro-prioception, which raises the question of whether routing perception through intermediate models is necessary at all. The second is harness engineering , which extends the harness around the MLLM with post-trained policies or external improvement loops. Some systems build harnesses with policy that expose post-trained policies as callable tools [53, 67], so the MLLM plans while the policy executes low-level actions. Each delegated call returns only its final observation and status, so the actions of the policy never enter the context of the MLLM, and the harness can constrain the capability of the underlying MLLM. Other systems adapt self-evolving language agents into embodied recursive self-improvement (RSI) frameworks that revise context, tools, or control logic through revision loops around the agent [8, 33, 37, 49]. These frameworks iterate on external harnesses and rarely mea-sure the base model under direct control first. Some self-evolving designs adapt on the test scenes themselves [54]. Because the evaluator then returns feedback on the scenes used for evaluation, test-scene information can leak into the evolved system, and its gains no longer show whether the acquired experience generalizes to scenes the agent has never attempted. It thus remains unclear how far a frontier MLLM can go under direct control on its own, and hence how much of this added machinery is necessary. Both lines of work focus on either exploiting the coding ability of the MLLM or wrapping it with post-trained policies and improvement loops, thereby overlooking the rising capability of the MLLM itself to act directly as a general-purpose robot controller. We refer to this setting as direct control ,where the MLLM commands the end effector directly using RGB views and proprioception, with-out external perception modules or a post-trained policy. Direct control returns the result of every executed command to the MLLM, so the closed-loop feedback and within-episode recovery are ex-plicit. After a missed grasp or a failed insertion, the MLLM compares the commanded end-effector coordinates with the resulting camera views, diagnoses the error, and adjusts its next command [60]. These adjustments and the experience of each trial, however, vanish when the episode ends, so each episode starts anew. Can a frontier MLLM unlock strong manipulation under direct control simply by inheriting and evolving the in-episode adjustments and trial experience across episodes, relying only on its native multimodal capabilities? To show that recording and revising its own memory is enough for a frontier MLLM to excel under direct control, we present SimplEvo, illustrated in Figure 2. SimplEvo keeps the native direct-control loop unchanged and introduces only two compact text memories that the MLLM writes itself during adaptation on the training set. From one training demonstration, the MLLM formulates a coordinate-free task memory once, and after each training episode, it reflects on its own trajectory in one turn to rewrite a bounded working memory of transferable lessons. A strict train–test protocol confines adaptation and its outcome feedback to training scenes, none of which appears in the test set. Both memories are then fixed, and every test episode reads them without reflection turns or memory 2Preprint updates. Throughout adaptation and testing, the agent observes only RGB views and proprioception and requires no post-trained policies, weight updates, or privileged information. With its working memory fixed after adaptation, SimplEvo outperforms post-trained policies and other MLLM agents on the test episodes of EBench [15] and the test layouts of RoboDojo [6]. On EBench, SimplEvo raises GPT-6-Astra from 46.7% under direct control to a success rate (SR) of 81.7% across its 510 test scenes while reducing tokens per success, against up to 56.0% SR for harnesses with policy and 61.4% SR for the strongest post-trained policy. On RoboDojo, after adapting on 15 training layouts per task, SimplEvo leads the public leaderboard across 50 test layouts per task with 69.63% SR and a score of 75.32. This paper makes three contributions: • We propose SimplEvo, a two-stage memory framework for direct control, in which the MLLM derives a task memory without coordinates from a single training demonstration and rewrites a bounded working memory through its own reflection on every training episode, without post-trained policies or weight updates. • Under a strict evaluation protocol with train–test separation, native step limits, and no privileged information, SimplEvo ranks first on both the EBench and the RoboDojo leaderboards, ahead of post-trained VLAs, WAMs, and other MLLM agents, while using fewer tokens per success than direct control. • We show that SimplEvo performs well with simple text-based memories and, with these mem-ories fixed, generalizes to unseen layouts and randomized scene configurations. Moreover, the working memory created by a frontier MLLM can be reused by weaker MLLMs to improve their performance. 

2 RELATED WORK 

Harnesses and self-improvement for embodied agents. Beyond the code-as-policies systems and harnesses with policy discussed in Section 1, harnesses also coordinate post-trained policies through memory [57], and direct control can command end-effector targets [24, 32, 66] or semantic action units [7]. Self-improving embodied systems differ in what they change. They revise tools, harnesses, or code policies during development and score the fixed result [22, 37, 49], co-evolve guidance with harness modules [10, 28, 48, 51, 52], or distill trajectories into policy weights [4, 42, 56], and Zhou et al. [71] classify such systems by which component a retained change updates and how broadly its gain holds. Controlled audits find that harness evolution does not consistently outperform test-time scaling at equal budget, that frequent updates can fail to improve held-out performance [54, 69], and that selection on small, noisy evaluations admits spurious gains [16, 45]. SimplEvo keeps tools, harnesses, code, and model weights fixed. It acts under direct control and updates only the text working memory that the MLLM reads across episodes. 

Frontier MLLMs as robot policies. Frontier MLLMs now control robots without being trained on robot data. For example, GPT-6-Astra [40] commands end effectors directly on RoboDojo [6, 66] and learns from demonstration blocks in context [31], while other MLLM agents write robot commands from saved procedures [24] or refine a revisable model of the robot body [32]. Yao et al. [60] profile such an agent on EBench [15] across direct control and harnesses with policy, and they find that its failures concentrate in physical execution. This study measures how far an agent under direct control improves once its execution experience persists across episodes. 

Memory and experience evolution. Agent memory systems differ in how they represent and up-date experience across episodes. Language agents improve without weight updates by accumulating verbal reflections [46], extracting insights from successful and failed trajectories [68], or curating evolving playbooks [65]. In multimodal interactive environments, VISTA [20] preserves a lossless visual history of past observations, so carrying it across manipulation episodes lengthens the context with every executed command. Embodied agents store experience as executable skills [50], vali-dated program repairs [33], policy repositories of prompts, tools, and control code [11], per-task programs [53], or a knowledge package that GPT-6-Astra writes once from training demonstrations and reads unchanged in every test episode [44]. 3 METHOD 

As shown in Figure 2, SimplEvo equips direct control with two memories written by the MLLM, each overcoming a limitation of isolated episodes. The task memory supplies the order of stages and the final state of a task before the first attempt, and the working memory preserves the corrections that would otherwise vanish when an episode ends. 3.1 DIRECT CONTROL UNDER A STRICT INFORMATION PROTOCOL 

Agent under direct control. The agent pairs a fixed-weight MLLM core F with an agent harness that specifies its observation and action space. Under direct control , the MLLM issues every robot command itself instead of calling a post-trained policy. Information protocol. On both benchmarks, we restrict the agent to the information that a phys-ical deployment provides. During execution, the agent observes the scene solely through RGB images and robot proprioception ot = ( It, q t), matching the inputs of a post-trained VLA or WAM. Although recent evaluation guidelines for robot-use agents allow camera intrinsics [41], we ex-clude privileged information such as depth, camera parameters, or object states used by prior sys-tems [22, 23, 29]. On a physical robot, such information would come from external estimation modules that are sensitive to perception errors. Restricting observations to RGB views and proprio-ception therefore reflects more faithfully how the agent adapts to the robot embodiment and refines 4Preprint its execution in the environment. Beyond ot, the MLLM receives only the instruction ℓτ , the two memories, and the training demonstration Dτ of Section 3.2. Finally, like post-trained policies, the agent acts within the native per-task step limits of each benchmark and receives no intermediate progress signals. 3.2 TASK MEMORY FROM A DEMONSTRATION 

The task memory M task records the stage order and the visual completion criterion that a single demonstration conveys reliably. Before the first episode, an annotator instance of the same MLLM reviews the recorded actions and camera frames of the demonstration Dτ and writes M task once. 

M task lists the stages of the task in order, stating for each stage the operation and the arm that performs it, and ends with the visual state of the completed task. It contains no coordinates, so that it applies to layouts other than the demonstrated one. Because the final stage specifies the completed state, M task gives the MLLM an explicit stopping criterion before any execution experience exists, which we term terminal-state anchoring . 4 EXPERIMENTS 

We evaluate SimplEvo on two public manipulation benchmarks against their leaderboard entries and against GPT-6-Astra without working memory, with adaptation confined to training scenes. 4.1 SETUP 

Benchmarks and generalization. The test episodes of EBench [15] and RoboDojo [6] evaluate the working memory under variations in scene conditions. EBench comprises 26 tasks of a simulated ARX Lift 2 dual-arm mobile robot in Isaac Sim [14], and its 510 test scenes perturb the manipulated 5Preprint objects, the background, the instruction, or several of them at once. Its seven single-layout tasks keep one object layout across training and test episodes while varying objects, backgrounds, and instructions. Its 19 multi-layout tasks also vary the layout, so a test layout may be unseen or already seen in training. Fifteen of these 19 tasks include both seen and unseen test layouts. RoboDojo comprises 42 dual-arm tabletop tasks in five capability dimensions, each tested on 50 layouts of initial objects and their poses. On its 12 Generalization tasks, half of the test layouts randomize the background, the lighting, the target objects, and the clutter. Following the official protocols, we report SR and the stage-completion score, both on a 0–100 scale. 

Train–test protocol. Per task, adaptation uses up to 15 EBench training episodes or 15 RoboDojo training episodes, then fixes the working memory for testing. On EBench, adaptation stops early after three consecutive successes, as Appendix B.3 details. On RoboDojo Generalization tasks, training includes both standard and randomized configurations. The task memory M task comes from one training demonstration per task. 

Agent and baselines. We use GPT-6-Astra [40] at high reasoning effort as the MLLM core F in all experiments except the cross-model transfer study. On EBench, we compare against the 12 post-trained VLAs and WAMs on the public leaderboard, as well as GPT-6-Astra under direct control and in harnesses with π0.5 [2] or InternW0-∆ [36] following Yao et al. [60], all reading the task memory but no working memory. On RoboDojo, we compare against the leading post-trained policies and agents on the public leaderboard, the official zero-shot entry of GPT-6-Astra on test layouts, and task memory only on the training layouts used by SimplEvo. We also report tokens per success, the total tokens of an evaluation divided by its successes, and analyze cost in Appendix G. 4.2 RESULTS ON EB ENCH 

With its working memory fixed, SimplEvo reaches 81.7% SR on the 510 test episodes of EBench and ranks first in every task group of Table 1, using the leaderboard snapshot of 29 October 2026, ahead of PICA-WAM [72], the best post-trained policy, at 61.4% and the best harness with policy at 56.0%. The largest gains arise on high-precision, tabletop, and long-horizon tasks. On high-precision tasks, direct control without working memory succeeds in 11.3% of the episodes, and neither harness with policy exceeds 12.5%. With working memory, the same direct control reaches 82.5%, 2.3 times the 36.3% of PICA-WAM, which shows that the waypoints of the MLLM are precise enough for these tasks once the corrections of earlier attempts persist. On long-horizon tasks, direct control drops to 27.3% from 73.2% on short-horizon ones, while SimplEvo reaches 76.3%, above even the short-horizon SR of direct control. The task memory fixes the stage sequence, and the working memory adds to each stage the completion checks and recovery steps verified in earlier episodes. On bottle collection, SimplEvo reaches 45.0% SR, while none of the 12 leaderboard policies succeeds. It also reaches 90.0% on coffee-bean collection and 95.0% on shop checkout, where direct control without working memory has no successes. At test time, SimplEvo spends 2.76M tokens per success, compared with 8.12M for direct control, 3.46M for the harness with π0.5,and 3.99M for the harness with InternW0-∆, as Appendix G reports. 4.3 RESULTS ON ROBO DOJO 

After adapting on the 15 training layouts of each task, SimplEvo ranks first among the RoboDojo leaderboard entries of Table 2 with 69.63% SR and a score of 75.32 on the 50 test layouts per task. It also has the highest SR and score when the Open dimension is excluded. Table 3 shows two effects of the working memory. First, reflection distills experience into working memory on the eight Open tasks, which provide no demonstration or task memory. On the training layouts, SR rises from 32.50% without working memory to 70.00% during adaptation. After adaptation, the fixed working memory reaches 77.50% on the test layouts. Second, the fixed working memory carries training experience into test episodes. Test SR exceeds the 58.53% observed during online adaptation, and tokens per success are 2.23M on test layouts, compared with 4.12M for task memory alone on training layouts. 5 ANALYSIS 

5.1 DESIGN CHOICES FOR MEMORY AND REFLECTION

Each chain starts from an empty working memory, so its SR includes the first attempts of adaptation, unlike the fixed working memory of Table 1. Under direct control, Table 4 shows that the text working memory more than triples the SR of task memory alone, from 15.0% to 47.5%. Adding a visual replay of earlier attempts, which archives their camera frames, to the text working memory leaves the SR unchanged, and the MLLM queries the replay in only 3 of the 40 episodes. Maintaining a single continuous conversation across the chain, following the visual history design of VISTA [20], reaches a similar SR with a slightly higher score. Outcome feedback. The working memory retains most of its gain without terminal outcome feed-back. Eight tasks test this signal: the four high-precision tasks and four multi-layout tasks, namely dishwasher loading, the fruit task, utensil placement, and dish placement. When the terminal out-come rk is withheld during online adaptation along their 20-scene chains, the overall SR decreases from 73.8% to 65.0%. The high-precision chains keep 52.5% SR in both conditions. On the fi-nal five episodes of the four multi-layout chains, where task memory alone also runs on the same scenes, working memory without outcome feedback reaches 80.0% SR, compared with 35.0% for task memory alone and 95.0% with outcome feedback, thereby retaining three quarters of the im-provement through self-assessment of ξk alone, as detailed in Appendix E.2.

6 CONCLUSION 

Although embodied agents increasingly surround frontier MLLMs with perception pipelines, har-nesses with policy, and critic loops, our results show that a frontier MLLM under direct control already achieves strong closed-loop manipulation once its within-episode corrections persist across episodes. SimplEvo retains these corrections in two self-written text memories: a coordinate-free task memory distilled once from one training demonstration, and a bounded working memory rewrit-ten after each training episode. Under the strict train–test protocol, with RGB views and propriocep-tion as its only observations, SimplEvo raises GPT-6-Astra from 46.7% to 81.7% SR on EBench and leads the RoboDojo leaderboard. Our analyses yield four takeaways for embodied agent design. (1) A bounded text memory distilled from the trajectories of the agent itself matches the SR of visual memory at lower cost and retains most of the improvement without outcome feedback. (2) The pro-posed memory mechanism improves direct control more than the compared harness with policy. (3) Lessons on task invariants, applied to the objects that the MLLM locates in the current views, hold on unseen layouts and under randomized configurations. (4) A text memory written by one MLLM also improves other MLLMs that read it without modification, though only for the behaviors that it states explicitly. 

Limitations and future directions. These findings point to three directions for future work. First, 

verified memory updates could validate each candidate rewrite before it enters the working memory and thus filter out spurious lessons. Second, object-centric, behavior-explicit memory could anchor recorded actions to visual features and state the low-level control habits that GPT-6-Astra leaves im-plicit, so that recorded evidence carries across workspace geometries and the memory serves weaker MLLMs. Third, both benchmarks pause the simulator during MLLM deliberation and end episodes automatically upon completion, and each episode costs millions of tokens. These conditions mo-tivate asynchronous, token-efficient deployment on physical robots with autonomous completion detection [41].

E.1 MEMORY DESIGNS IN BOTH AGENT HARNESSES 

Protocol. Each design runs one chain of ten validation scenes per task in the same scene order, and the first episode of each chain reads the task memory alone. The designs differ only in the infor-mation retained across episodes. The first design reads only the task memory in every episode. The second design carries the text working memory of SimplEvo. The third design adds visual replay to the text working memory. The replay archives the frames and robot states of earlier attempts for inspection during execution and reflection. Both agent harnesses run these three designs on the four high-precision tasks. In the harness with π0.5, a fourth design, described in Appendix E.3, adds the executed trajectory of every policy call to the text design on the four high-precision tasks. Under direct control, a continuous design adapted from VISTA [20] replaces the working memory with one conversation across the ten episodes of each chain, together with the task memory, a guide that the MLLM revises, and an archive of observed frames. Time counts the control phase of an episode, from the first observation until the MLLM ends control, as model time plus 0.5 s per physics step.

The successes of the task stay at 0 and 1, and its mean score rises from 0 to 31.4 and 27.9. Under direct control, on gear installation, peg insertion, and nut tightening, the agent without working memory already grasps near the poses that the final working memory records, so the working memory mainly changes the actions after the grasp. As defined in Section 3.1, a policy call reports only the state at its end, so the motion of π0.5 never enters the trajectory ξk on which the reflection operates. The π0.5 trajectory design in Table 5 places this motion into ξk.

Recorded states. π0.5 predicts absolute joint and gripper targets and replans every 30 physics steps. At the end of each replanning interval, the harness reads the proprioception of the robot and records the end-effector pose of each arm, as a position and a unit quaternion in the planning frame of that arm, together with the gripper openings, the base pose, and the physics step. These fields and frames match the measured state that every end-effector command returns, so the MLLM can com-pare a state reached by π0.5 with its own waypoints and command that state directly. The last entry of each policy call records the state at the end of that call, and because a physical robot measures these quantities at every control step, the record satisfies the information protocol of Section 3.1. 

Exposure to the MLLM. The record reaches the MLLM through three channels. First, the result of each policy call lists one entry per replanning interval next to the existing step count, state, and images. Second, the memory tools that list earlier calls and measured robot states return the same entries from the episode record, so the MLLM can query them during the episode and during reflection. Third, the reflection prompt after the episode includes the entries of every policy call, so the record remains available for reflection in long episodes. All other settings, including the tool descriptions, the size budget of the working memory, the step limits, and the π0.5 checkpoint, match the text design. 

Results. With the executed trajectory, the harness succeeds in 17.5% of the episodes, down from 25.0%, while the use of the harness stays nearly unchanged. Every policy call of this design returns its executed trajectory, yet π0.5 executes 41% of the commanded physics steps, against 42% with the text design, the final working memories store poses about as often, and the MLLM commands positions near the states that π0.5 reached about as often as with the text design. These results indicate that visibility of the motion of π0.5 alone changes neither how the MLLM divides control nor what its working memory records. E.4 COMPARISON WITH THE CRITIC -D RIVEN RSI L OOP 

Protocol. The critic-driven RSI loop starts from the harness with π0.5 and an empty playbook. In each of five rounds, ten training tasks run once with and once without their demonstration, 100 episodes in all, and a read-only critic instance of the same model inspects these episodes and accepts revisions to the playbook and the harness. Most failures with the fixed working memory follow two patterns, a stored recipe that reproduces failing executions as well as successful ones, and a lesson drawn from training successes whose cause the camera views leave ambiguous. Of the 93 failures among the 510 test episodes of the train–test protocol, 41 replay stored coordinates, with at least a quarter of their moving waypoints within 1 cm of a coordinate in the fixed working memory, and 27 of these 41 failures occur on 32 Preprint single-layout tasks. On nut tightening, each of the six failures places more than half of its moving waypoints near a stored coordinate, as the 14 successes do. Pen placement shows the second case. In four of its seven training successes, the official success arrived during a late adjustment of a pen that already appeared to stand in the holder, and the working memory states that “a late small intervention can precede success without proving its exact mechanism” while still recommending such adjustments. With this working memory, pen placement succeeds in 5 of 20 test episodes, and each of its 15 failures replays at most 5% of its moving waypoints. Pen placement and utensil placement together account for 23 of the 52 failures without replay.
