## Abstract 

Agentic search demands that a search engine move beyond per-document relevance ranking to assemble document sets optimized for downstream LLM reasoning. Prior work addresses this either by improving individual pipeline stages in isolation or by applying RL-based selection on top of relevance-trained upstream modules, leaving the full stack unaligned to utility for reasoning. We pro-pose rubric-aligned search, a framework that unifies the traditional search pipeline under a single supervision surface. For each query, we derive a rubric from coarse to fine, specifying weighted positive criteria that the document set must satisfy and negative criteria that it must avoid. From this rubric we derive two training sig-nals. First, a lightweight labeler distills rubric-grounded judgments into per-document utility labels, which supervise a contrastive dual-encoder for retrieval and a multi-task ranker trained with four rubric-aligned scoring heads. Second, a rubric-conditioned evaluator produces a set-level reward that drives a dual-branch RL selector, combining GRPO with best-rollout distillation to resolve credit assignment in subset optimization. Deployed in Yuanbao Search, the pipeline improves end-to-end answer quality by 8.52% over the online production baseline.

## 1 Introduction 

For decades, web search has been dominated by learning to rank: given a query, order documents by relevance, authority, freshness, and diversity [2, 43]. The goal is to maximize per-position util-ity for a human user who scans the ranked list and synthesizes information. Recently, agentic search engines have changed this consumption model [19, 31]: instead of browsing a ranked list, the user receives a single answer synthesized by an LLM that reasons over a bounded set of documents, synthesizing a multi-source sum-mary or answering a multi-hop question that no single document covers, so the quality of its answer is entirely gated by the utility of the document set it receives. The retrieval objective therefore shifts from ranked-list quality to set-level utility : a compact, conflict-free document set that maximizes answer accuracy. To improve set-level utility, prior work has pursued two com-plementary directions: characterizing evidence quality for LLM generation, and optimizing evidence utility for downstream an-swer quality. The first direction asks what makes a document set 

useful for LLM synthesis. Three aspects have been studied: task utility, whether the evidence covers the information needed to answer the query (e.g., answer contribution, information cover-age [21, 40]); model usability, whether the model can effectively consume the evidence (e.g., position bias, context noise [23]); and trustworthiness, whether the evidence is faithful and free of hallu-cination (e.g., citation faithfulness [10]). quality into discrete criteria, rubric-based evaluators [8, 11] can score document sets against all three aspects in one pass. The sec-ond direction optimizes evidence utility through two lenses. From the LLM training literature, RaR [30] and Chasing the Tail [45] show that rubric-guided feedback serves as an effective RL reward, yet neither has been applied to search pipelines. From the retrieval literature, generator-aware selectors such as SARA [13] learn to choose document subsets that maximize answer quality, but op-erate exclusively at the selection stage, taking upstream retrieval and ranking as given. However, prior work either defines evidence quality without training it, or optimizes evidence utility without aligning upstream stages (Figure 1(b)). To close this gap, we adopt the rubric as the central optimiza-tion signal for the entire search pipeline (Figure 1(c)). Translating rubric guidance into pipeline action, however, poses three non-trivial challenges. First, there is a granularity mismatch between evaluation and training. Retrieval and ranking stages must score documents independently to enable efficient indexing and caching. A rubric evaluates document sets , and its criteria cannot be applied to a single document in isolation. Second, annotation cost prohibits direct rubric-based labeling at scale. Rubric-based evaluation de-mands costly LLM inference per query–document set. Training retrieval and ranking models requires labeled data across millions of query–document pairs, making per-instance annotation using strong reasoning LLMs prohibitively expensive for production scale. Third, even for the selection model, the only stage that natively op-erates on sets, the rubric reward is inherently sparse: a single scalar score assigned to an entire selected set provides no token-level guidance, making credit assignment and training unstable. To address these challenges, we first define a query-specific rubric with positive criteria carrying importance weights (what the set must contain) and negative criteria carrying deduction magni-tudes (what it must avoid). We design a coarse-to-fine procedure that automates rubric construction at production scale. This formu-lation reduces set-level evaluation to per-criterion scoring, making the rubric actionable for training while the generation pipeline ensures rubrics are both comprehensive and discriminative. From this rubric we derive two training signals. The first is per query– document utility labels. A large model first reasons with the rubric to score individual query–document pairs. These scores are dis-tilled into a lightweight labeler via supervised fine-tuning and refined through RL-based correction. The labeler thereby amor-tizes rubric-grounded set-level evaluation into pointwise scores, resolving the granularity mismatch noted above. Guided by these utility labels, the retrieval stage trains a contrastive dual-encoder (Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B [47]) with utility-aligned fine-tuning, and the ranking stage trains a Qwen3-4B [28] ranker with a utility-oriented focal loss. The second signal is a set-level reward: a rubric-conditioned evaluator scores candidate subsets directly against the rubric. We train the selection stage via a dual-branch RL archi-tecture: GRPO optimizes the sparse set-level rubric reward, while Best-Rollout Distillation densifies credit assignment by treating the highest-reward rollout as a token-level teacher. We evaluate each component offline and in an online A/B test on Yuanbao Search production traffic. Offline evaluation shows consistent gains across pipeline stages, and in the downstream agent system, cumulative answer quality improves by 8.52%. Our contributions are: (1) A rubric-as-reward formulation that defines query-dependent, set-level utility as the single supervision surface for all pipeline stages, together with a lightweight labeler that amortizes this signal into per query–document utility labels at corpus scale; (2) A dual-branch RL training strategy for set selection that com-bines GRPO with best-rollout distillation to ensure stable con-vergence under sparse set-level reward; (3) An industry-deployed system in Yuanbao Search, with offline and online experiments validating the effectiveness of each pipeline component and the full agent search system. 

## 2 Method 

We address the aforementioned three challenges in three steps. First, we define query-specific rubrics with weighted positive and negative criteria, and automate their construction via a coarse-to-fine generation pipeline. Second, we train a labeler to amortize rubric-grounded evaluation into per-document utility labels, super-vising retrieval and ranking over the full corpus. Third, we train the selection stage via dual-branch RL with a rubric-conditioned set-level reward, resolving credit assignment through GRPO and best-rollout distillation. Figure 2 illustrates the overall framework.

## 2.2 Rubric-oriented Utility Label Generation 

2.2.1 Utility Label Generation. The traditional retrieval–ranking funnel scores documents independently to support efficient index-ing and top-𝐾 retrieval, and therefore requires pointwise supervi-sion. For simple queries 

𝑙 =3 is attainable by a single document; for complex queries with many facets, even a partial match at 𝑙 =2 reduces the documents needed to satisfy R𝑞 , so higher grades remain informative. Assigning these labels requires multi-criterion reasoning: the model must check each (𝑞, 𝑑 ) pair against every positive and nega-tive criterion in R𝑞 , weigh their relative importance, and produce both a label and a rationale explaining which criteria are met or violated. A small model lacks the reasoning capacity to do this reliably across diverse queries, while running a strong LLM on every query-document pair is infeasible at corpus scale. We resolve this tension through distillation. A strong LLM serves as an offline teacher, annotating a small subset of query-document pairs with a utility grade ˆ𝑙 and a rationale ˆ e. A lightweight student labeler is then trained to approximate the teacher’s judgment and scales annotation to millions of query-document pairs at negligible cost. 

2.2.2 Labeler Training. We train the lightweight labeler in two stages. In the SFT stage, the student is fine-tuned on teacher-annotated 

(𝑞, 𝑑, R𝑞 ) → ( ˆ𝑙, ˆe) pairs via next-token prediction, learning to fol-low the labeling format and produce grounded rationales. This provides a necessary cold start: without it, RL would explore an unstructured output space with no signal. However, SFT optimizes for surface imitation rather than rubric satisfaction, so its accuracy is bounded by the teacher. We address this gap with an RL stage that directly optimizes rubric alignment. ## 2.4 Multi-Task Ranking 

This section describes the architecture and training objectives of our utility-aligned ranking model. The ranking stage reorders D𝑞 into a ranked list Drank of roughly twenty documents for selection . We use a Qwen3-4B [28] decoder-only ranker, trained with four scoring heads sharing a single encoder: utility, authority, freshness, and quality. The utility head is supervised by the labeler’s rubric-aligned annotations ; the other three are supervised by existing production signals. At inference, only the utility head is used, producing a single scalar ranking score. The auxiliary heads serve as multi-task regularizers that encourage the shared encoder to learn authority-, freshness-, and quality-aware representations, which indirectly benefit utility scoring. Each head is optimized with a pairwise loss. We down-weight the three auxiliary losses by 𝜆 aux = 0.5, giving the utility objective twice the per-head gradient weight of any single auxiliary task. ## 3 Experiments 

To validate the effectiveness of the proposed rubric-aligned pipeline, we conduct extensive offline and online experiments on Yuanbao Search, a large-scale commercial AI search engine.

## 4 Related Work 4.1 Neural Retrieval and Ranking 

Learning to rank has evolved from classical methods using hand-crafted features [2, 43] to neural models that learn semantic rep-resentations end to end. Dense retrieval replaced sparse lexical matching with dual-encoder architectures trained via contrastive learning with in-batch negatives [15, 41], and ColBERT [16, 35] introduced late interaction for token-level matching. Encoder ad-vances such as SimCSE [9], Contriever [12], E5 [39], BGE [4], and Qwen3-Embedding [47] improved dense embeddings and scaled to multilingual settings, deployed with Faiss [14] and Matryoshka representations [18]; ListT5 [44] advanced listwise retrieval. On the ranking side, LLM-based rankers have demonstrated strong zero-shot and fine-tuned performance: RankGPT [38], RankLLaMA [25], PRP [27], and Setwise [51] pioneered LLM listwise reranking, spe-cialized open-weight rankers, pairwise prompting, and setwise selection, respectively. Focal loss [22] has been adopted to address class imbalance in ranking. A common limitation across these meth-ods is that they optimize for per-document relevance, yet coverage, contradiction, and redundancy, which emerge only when docu-ments are combined into a set, lie outside the scope of pointwise scoring. We build on them by training retrieval and ranking with rubric-grounded utility labels that encode set-level requirements (e.g., “covers both pros and cons”) into per-document supervision, aligning early stages with the answer quality they serve. 

## 4.2 Document Set Selection and Agentic Search 

Beyond per-document scoring, explicit document subset com-position has been studied extensively. Classical approaches re-duce redundancy through submodular optimization [21], maxi-mal marginal relevance [3], and determinantal point processes. Information-gain-based methods, including InfoGain-RAG [40] and Uplift-RAG [32], select documents that maximize knowledge gain for the generator. Diversity-oriented approaches including CL4DIV [6] optimize for complementary coverage. Generator-aware selectors directly optimize subsets for downstream answer quality: SARA [13] performs iterative evidence selection through hybrid compression, R3 [49] trains reinforcement-learning-based retrievers, and ReSearch [5] scales deep research through RL-driven multi-turn search. Agentic search further shifts retrieval from one-shot ranking to iterative reasoning-and-retrieval loops: ReAct [42] established the reasoning-and-acting paradigm; Self-RAG [1] in-troduced self-reflection to decide when and what to retrieve; and ALCE [10] provides a systematic framework for evaluating retrieval-augmented pipelines. These methods focus on optimizing the se-lection stage, taking upstream retrieval and ranking as given. Our framework complements this: the same rubric that defines a good document set also trains retrieval and ranking, so the selector re-ceives documents aligned to set-level quality, not topical relevance. 

## 4.3 Rubric-Based Evaluation and Reinforcement Learning 

Rubrics decompose holistic quality judgments into discrete, ver-ifiable criteria. LLM-based evaluation was established by MT-Bench [48] and advanced by G-Eval [24] and Prometheus [17], which improved alignment through chain-of-thought scoring. RUBRIC [8] and LLM-Rubric [11] applied criterion-decomposed evaluation to retrieval and search-augmented generation. Recent work has begun transforming rubrics from evaluation instruments into training signals: RaR [30] uses checklist-style rubrics as GRPO rewards, Chasing the Tail [45] designs rubric reward models for long-tail post-training, RuscaRL [34] breaks exploration bottlenecks via rubric-scaffolded RL, and Search Wisely [33] reduces retrieval uncertainty. On the RL front, PPO [36] and RLHF [26] established preference-based alignment, DPO [29] simplified it through direct preference pairs, GRPO [37] enables efficient policy optimization via within-group advantage estimation, and Scaf-GRPO [46] adds scaffolded exploration. GRPO-based selection methods typically rely on a single-branch design; our dual-branch extension (detailed in §2.5) adds best-rollout distillation for stable convergence under sparse set-level reward. Taken together, prior work has established rubrics as effective evaluation protocols and has applied rubric rewards to individual LLM components. We extend these ideas by using the rubric as a shared supervision surface that connects retrieval, ranking, and selection under the same set-level utility objective, so each stage targets the answer quality it produces. 

## 5 Conclusion and Future Work 

In this work, we present practical solutions to align the search pipeline with rubric-grounded utility in a large-scale agentic search engine. The proposed solutions are successfully implemented and deployed to power Yuanbao Search. To bridge the granularity mis-match between set-level rubric evaluation and per-document scor-ing, we proposed a lightweight labeler that distills rubric-grounded judgments into per-document utility labels, supervising a con-trastive dual-encoder for utility-aligned retrieval and a multi-task ranker with rubric-aligned utility scoring. To manage the sparse reward and credit assignment problem in subset optimization, we design a dual-branch RL selector that combines GRPO with best-rollout distillation, using the best rollout as a self-teacher for token-level correction. Extensive offline and online experiments verify the effectiveness of our proposed solutions. Future work includes dynamically refreshing rubrics from fresh web evidence to handle rapidly evolving topics, and extending the pipeline to multimodal queries where criteria span text, images, and structured data. ## A Selector Training Dynamics 

We compare two selectors that share the same data, training recipe, and PE Judge: GRPO, and GRPO+OPD (ours), which adds best-rollout distillation to GRPO. Both are trained for 108 steps on 180K training queries, with G=8 rollouts per query. We track two quan-tities. Validation reward is the PE Judge set-level score (0–10) on held-out queries, measured at steps 0, 40, 80, and 108. Actor entropy measures how concentrated the policy is, so lower values mean sharper output; we report it at steps 0 and 108. Figure 5 plots both quantities across the 108 training steps. Two observations follow. First, GRPO+OPD keeps improving while GRPO stalls. Its validation reward rises from 8.586 at step 0 to 9.871 at step 108, against 8.536 to 9.597 for GRPO. Between steps 80 and 108, GRPO+OPD gains only 0.014 while GRPO still gains 0.194. Second, actor entropy separates the two branches in the same direction: GRPO’s entropy rises from 0.622 to 0.748 as the policy drifts, while GRPO+OPD’s falls to 0.310. GRPO+OPD also takes 608 s per step against 941 s for GRPO. The final quality gap is small (Table 5, +0.03), so the branch’s value is faster, lower-entropy convergence rather than a large gain.
