# Reliable Evaluation of AI Agents for Investment Research: A Framework for Performance and Validity Assessment 

Dongnuan Tian, Yan Yu, Yangfan Liu, Wenjun Tang, Junting Kan, Xiang Wang, Sicong Liu, Yangyang Zhang, Shuanghong Li ∗

Gildata GAOS Team Shanghai Hundsun JuYuan Data Service Co., Ltd. 

# Abstract 

We consider the problem of evaluating AI agents undertaking investment-research tasks, where agents interact with external information sources and computational tools to produce financial analyses and research reports. An important challenge is that the quality of an agent’s final output does not necessarily reflect the quality of its execution process, while the reliability of the resulting assessment also depends on the validity of the evaluation procedure. Furthermore, investment-research tasks differ considerably in their requirements for numerical accuracy, information retrieval, financial analysis and supporting evidence, making it difficult to assess agent performance using a uniform set of criteria. We propose a domain-oriented evaluation framework that distinguishes outcome quality from execution quality and accommodates these differences through task-specific criteria, deterministic validators and independent large language model judges. We also intro-duce an evaluation-validity mechanism that examines the integrity of evaluation inputs, reference information, supporting evidence and grading outputs, with expert review used to resolve identi-fied deficiencies and significant grading disagreements. Numerical results show that the proposed framework can distinguish differences in agent performance across evaluation dimensions, while identifying potential reliability issues in the evaluation process. The associated implementation and evaluation materials will be made available on GitHub. 

Keywords: AI agent, investment research, performance assessment, evaluation validity

# 1 Introduction 

Large language models (LLMs) have increasingly been used to develop AI agents capable of inter-acting with external tools and information sources to undertake complex tasks (see, e.g., Yao et al. (2022); Wu et al. (2024); Wang et al. (2024a); Chowa et al. (2026)). In contrast to conventional LLM applications in which a response is generated from a given input, tool-augmented agents can obtain additional information, perform intermediate computations, and determine subsequent ac-tions according to the results of previous interactions. These capabilities are particularly relevant to investment research, where the preparation of financial analyses and research reports may involve information retrieval, quantitative calculations, and the interpretation of evidence from multiple sources (Zhou et al., 2024). The resulting research output is therefore associated with an execution process comprising a sequence of decisions and tool interactions. Evaluating such an agent raises the question of how the quality of its final output and the quality of its execution process should be assessed, and whether the resulting assessments provide a reliable basis for comparing different agents. A substantial body of research has investigated the evaluation of LLMs and AI agents, with methods developed for different task settings and assessment requirements (Chang et al., 2024; Yehudai et al., 2026). Conventional benchmark-based approaches typically evaluate task completion or the correctness of generated outputs against predefined criteria. However, these approaches may provide limited information about the intermediate actions undertaken by an agent or the quality of outputs for which a unique reference answer is unavailable. To address different aspects of these problems, researchers have considered execution-based evaluation and LLM-based assessment. For example, Zhuge et al. (2025) proposed Agent-as-a-Judge, in which an evaluating agent examines task requirements and supporting artefacts through an interactive assessment process. Es et al. (2024) developed RAGAS to evaluate retrieval-augmented generation through criteria concerning the retrieved context and generated responses. Other studies have examined the consistency and reliability of LLM-based evaluators (Gu et al., 2026; Tan et al., 2025). These methods provide useful foundations for assessing agents, particularly when the assessment involves both directly verifiable requirements and qualitative judgements supported by external evidence. In the financial domain, earlier benchmarks concentrated primarily on particular capabilities of language models, including numerical reasoning, financial knowledge, and question answering (Lee et al. (2025)). Chen et al. (2021) introduced FinQA to evaluate numerical reasoning over financial reports, while Islam et al. (2023) developed FinanceBench to examine the ability of LLMs to answer financial questions using supporting evidence. Xie et al. (2024) subsequently proposed FinBen, which covers a broader collection of financial tasks. These studies establish useful ap-proaches for evaluating financial reasoning through structured questions, reference information, and task-specific assessment criteria. Nevertheless, the evaluation of an investment-research agent may involve more than determining whether an individual financial question has been answered correctly. In particular, research tasks may require the agent to retrieve and combine information, develop analytical arguments, and produce outputs whose quality cannot be determined solely by Tian et al.: Reliable Evaluation of AI Agents for Investment Research 3comparison with a predefined answer. Recent studies have thus considered more complex financial-research and investment-management settings. Sun et al. (2025) proposed FinResearchBench, a logic-tree-based Agent-as-a-Judge frame-work for evaluating financial research reports. Their approach extracts the structure of arguments and supporting evidence from a report and assesses different aspects of research quality using both rule-based measures and LLM judgements. This provides a methodology for examining the ana-lytical content of financial research beyond the correctness of individual answers. Hu et al. (2026) developed FinSearchComp to evaluate financial information retrieval and reasoning in settings in-volving complex searches and time-sensitive financial information. More recently, Bek et al. (2026) introduced FinSkillBench to assess agents undertaking investment-management tasks, incorporat-ing point-in-time data, task-specific verifiers, and execution trajectories. These contributions are particularly relevant to the present research because they provide complementary approaches to evaluating analytical outputs, financial information retrieval, and the use of domain-specific tools. They also establish the importance of specifying the information and verification procedures asso-ciated with each financial task, for which the framework proposed in this paper (see Section 3) is a natural fit. The development of these evaluation methods raises several methodological questions when assessing agents across different investment-research tasks. First, the requirements of financial tasks differ in the extent to which their outcomes can be verified objectively (Sun et al. (2025)). Numerical results and explicitly specified requirements may be assessed through deterministic calculations or reference-based checks, whereas the quality of an analytical argument and its supporting evidence may require contextual judgement. The use of different grading mechanisms is already supported by existing research, including approaches that combine rule-based assessment and LLM evaluation (Sun et al., 2025; Anthropic, 2026). The relevant question is therefore not whether one grading mechanism is generally preferable to another, but how the mechanism should be selected and applied according to the requirements of a particular criterion. In addition, the correctness of financial information must be considered in relation to its reporting period and availability at the time specified by the task (Hu et al., 2026; Bek et al., 2026). Consequently, a common evaluation procedure must accommodate different forms of verification without treating all financial-research criteria as equivalent. Second, the assessment of an agent’s final output does not necessarily characterise its execution process. Existing agent-evaluation studies have demonstrated the relevance of intermediate actions and execution trajectories for understanding agent behaviour (Ma et al., 2024; Zhuge et al., 2025; Yehudai et al., 2026; Bek et al., 2026). In investment research, the execution trace may provide evidence about information retrieval, tool selection, intermediate calculations, and the sequence in which research activities are undertaken. Such evidence is relevant to evaluating the appropri-ateness and efficiency of the execution process, but it does not replace an assessment of the final research output. Conversely, the correctness of the final output does not establish whether the agent has followed an appropriate execution process. We therefore distinguish outcome quality and Tian et al.: Reliable Evaluation of AI Agents for Investment Research 4execution quality as two components of agent performance. This distinction allows the assessment to consider both the research produced by an agent and the process through which it is obtained, while recognising that the criteria and evidence required for the two components may differ. A third question concerns the reliability of the evaluation procedure used to obtain the per-formance scores. Previous research has examined limitations of LLM-based evaluation, including variation in judgements and the consistency of grading decisions (Gu et al., 2026; Tan et al., 2025; Stureborg et al., 2024). However, the reliability of an assessment also depends on whether the correct task requirements, reference information, and execution evidence have been supplied to the evaluator. An assessment may produce a numerical score even when the reference information does not correspond to the task, the available execution trace is incomplete, or the grading output does not satisfy the prescribed assessment requirements. These problems differ from errors made by the agent being evaluated: they concern whether the evaluation has been conducted using the appropri-ate information and procedures. Thus, the assessment of agent performance and the verification of the assessment procedure should be treated as separate questions. In particular, a low performance score should be distinguished from a score whose interpretation is affected by deficiencies in the evaluation process. To address these methodological challenges, we make the following contributions: 

• We propose a domain-oriented framework for assessing the outcome and execution quality of AI agents undertaking investment-research tasks. The framework accommodates different task requirements and forms of evidence by specifying assessment criteria and selecting the corresponding grading mechanisms. 

• We develop an evaluation-validity mechanism that verifies the association of evaluation in-puts, the integrity of reference information and execution evidence, and the consistency of grading outputs. This enables deficiencies in the evaluation procedure to be distinguished from deficiencies in agent performance. 

• We construct an investment-research evaluation testbed with task-specific criteria, reference information, and verification procedures, and conduct computational experiments to examine agent performance, evaluation-validity issues, and their implications for comparisons across tasks and agents The remainder of the paper is organised as follows. Section 2 introduces the motivating ex-ample and problem formulation. Section 3 presents the proposed evaluation framework. Section 4 reports the numerical results, and Section 5 concludes the paper. # 2 Motivation and problem formulation 

2.1 Motivation 

Consider an agent asked to compare the financial performance of two companies at a specified date. The resulting report provides evidence about the accuracy of the reported figures and the consistency of the financial analysis. However, these properties do not establish how the agent obtained the information or whether its intermediate actions were appropriate. Figure 1 illustrates two possible executions that produce the same final result but differ in their execution traces. An assessment based only on the final report would not distinguish these processes, while an assessment based only on the trace would not establish whether the report adequately satisfies the task requirements. Moreover, the interpretation of either assessment depends on whether the required reference information and execution evidence are available and correspond to the specified task. We therefore distinguish the assessment of agent performance from the verification of the procedure used to obtain that assessment.

Although the report contains financial comparisons, its investment recommendation lacks sufficient risk analysis and supporting evidence. The example motivates two outputs of the evaluation procedure, namely a performance as-sessment and an evaluation-validity indicator. # 4 Numerical results 

In this section, we report the results of an experimental evaluation of three AI agents, namely Codex, DSH and OpenClaw, on 62 investment-research tasks, resulting in 186 agent–task records. The task set consists of 14 factual retrieval and numerical accuracy tasks (T1), 15 multi-step financial analysis and comparison tasks (T2), 27 time-sensitive information and supporting evidence tasks (T3), and six open-ended research and interpretation tasks (T4). The distribution of tasks and corresponding records is provided in Appendix D. Agent performance is assessed using eight evaluation dimensions, comprising five outcome-quality dimensions (correctness, coverage, evidence quality, research analysis quality and deliverable quality) and three execution-quality dimensions (functional path, failure recovery and execution efficiency). The applicable dimensions and their weights are determined by the requirements of each task. When a dimension is not assessable, it is excluded from the calculation, and the weights of the remaining assessable dimensions are renormalised. The scoring perspectives and detailed calculations are provided in Appendices B and C.

# 5 Conclusions 

In this paper, we have developed an evaluation framework for AI agents undertaking investment-research tasks. The framework provides a basis for comparing agent performance across different assessment dimensions and task requirements, while examining whether the resulting scores satisfy the prescribed evaluation requirements. Our numerical results show that Codex achieves the highest overall performance among the three agents considered, although the relative performance of the agents varies across evaluation dimensions and task types. The experiments also identify deficiencies in evaluation integrity and substantial disagreements between independent judges, indicating the importance of verifying the evaluation procedure before accepting the resulting scores. Future research could extend the framework to a wider range of investment-research settings and investigate the sensitivity of the results to different grading criteria and evaluators.

# Online Appendices A Related work 

The evaluation problem considered in this paper is closely related to four areas of research, namely, LLM-as-a-Judge, Agent-as-a-Judge, financial-agent evaluation, and evaluation reliability. We dis-cuss the relevance of these approaches to the proposed framework below. 

LLM-as-a-Judge. LLM-based evaluators have been used to assess generated responses against criteria for which deterministic verification may be difficult. Existing approaches include rubric-guided assessment, reference-based evaluation, and methods that examine the relationship between generated responses and supporting information (Zheng et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2024; Es et al., 2024; Saad-Falcon et al., 2024; Gu et al., 2026). These methods are relevant to investment-research tasks because the quality of analytical arguments and supporting evidence may require contextual judgement. However, other requirements, such as numerical accuracy and compliance with explicitly specified conditions, can be assessed using deterministic rules. In our framework, the grading mechanism is therefore selected according to the requirements of each assessment criterion, rather than applying LLM-based evaluation uniformly. The reliability of the resulting judgements is examined separately from the selection of the grading mechanism. 

Agent-as-a-Judge. The evaluation of AI agents requires consideration of the actions undertaken during task execution, which may not be fully reflected in the final output (Yehudai et al., 2026). AgentBench and AgentBoard considered agent behaviour in interactive environments and the in-formation provided by intermediate progress (Liu et al., 2024; Ma et al., 2024). Zhuge et al. (2025) proposed Agent-as-a-Judge, in which an evaluating agent examines task requirements and support-ing artefacts through a multi-step assessment process. In the financial domain, Sun et al. (2025) proposed FinResearchBench, which extracts a logic tree from the generated research report to ex-amine its analytical structure and supporting evidence. These studies demonstrate different ways of incorporating intermediate information into agent evaluation. In comparison, our framework specifies task-specific assessment criteria and verification requirements independently of the agent’s generated output, and distinguishes evidence used to assess execution quality from evidence used to verify the evaluation procedure. 

Financial-Agent Evaluation. Financial benchmarks have examined numerical reasoning, ques-tion answering, and broader financial capabilities using structured tasks and reference information (Chen et al., 2021; Islam et al., 2023; Xie et al., 2024). More recent studies have extended evalu-ation to financial research reports, information retrieval, and investment-management tasks (Sun et al., 2025; Bigeard et al., 2025; Hu et al., 2026; Bek et al., 2026; Zhu et al., 2026). FinResearch-Bench (Sun et al., 2025) provides a method for examining analytical arguments and supporting evidence, while FinSearchComp (Hu et al., 2026) and FinSkillBench (Bek et al., 2026) consider Tian et al.: Reliable Evaluation of AI Agents for Investment Research 16 requirements associated with time-sensitive information and task-specific verification. Research concerning risks associated with financial agents further highlights the importance of examining the conditions under which their outputs and behaviour are assessed (Chen et al., 2025). These approaches address different aspects of financial-agent evaluation. Our framework considers out-come and execution quality within a common assessment procedure, while specifying the reference information, evidence, and verification requirements applicable to each task. 

Evaluation Reliability. The reliability of model-based grading has been examined in relation to evaluator bias, agreement, consistency, and calibration (Zheng et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024b; Stureborg et al., 2024; Tan et al., 2025; Gu et al., 2026; Saad-Falcon et al., 2024). Related guidance has also considered the complementary roles of deterministic, model-based, and human assessment (Anthropic, 2026). In financial applications, the correctness of reference information must addi-tionally be considered in relation to reporting periods and information availability (Hu et al., 2026; Bek et al., 2026). These considerations concern different sources of uncertainty in an evaluation. In particular, consistency between grading judgements does not establish that the correct task requirements, reference information, and supporting evidence have been used. We therefore in-troduce separate integrity checks for these elements and for the resulting grading outputs. These checks establish compliance with prescribed evaluation requirements, rather than guaranteeing the correctness of every grading judgement.
