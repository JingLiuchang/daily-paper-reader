Abstract 

Multi-agent delegation lets LLM systems dis-tribute evidence collection across workers with separate contexts. This creates a consistency challenge: facts retrieved by workers may be missing from the evidence available to the lead for a joint conclusion. We introduce a bench-mark and evidence-flow profiler for study-ing multi-agent memory inconsistency through controlled factual conflicts in Harvey LAB document-review tasks. Pooled across three models with comparable generation spending, requiring delegation increases retrieval of both conflicting claims from 50.2% to 68.7% rela-tive to single-agent execution, yet conflict de-tection falls from 16.2% to 13.2%. Within this setting, workers report both claims for only 35.2% of retrieved pairs, and pooled matched comparisons show higher detection than single-agent execution when one worker reads both claims but lower detection when the claims are split across workers. Worker-to-lead handoffs are a central target for improving conflict de-tection in multi-agent LLMs. 

1 Introduction 

Multi-agent LLM systems scale complex work-flows by distributing work across agents with sepa-rate contexts. In lead–worker architectures, a lead assigns subtasks to specialized workers and com-bines their findings (Fourney et al., 2024). Re-search systems use this structure to explore multi-ple information sources in parallel and report gains over single-agent execution (Anthropic, 2025c). Controlled evaluations nevertheless show that these gains depend on task structure and coordination choices (Kim et al., 2026). Delegation, despite its parallel exploration gains, reshapes agent working memory. To analyze this gap, we study multi-agent mem-ory consistency : whether task-relevant evidence remains available across local and shared states, where a joint conclusion requires it. As Figure 1 illustrates, a worker can accurately summarize its assigned documents yet omit a claim needed to detect a conflict with another worker’s findings. We examine this problem through factual con-flict detection in document-review tasks, compar-ing single-agent execution ( None ), discretionary 1arXiv:2611.22245v1 [cs.AI] 17 Nov 2026 delegation ( Optional ), and mandatory delegation (Required ). Required reserves source inspection for workers assigned to document partitions. Across three models and two runs per model under com-mon per-task dollar allowances, Required increases retrieval of both conflicting claims ( Source Reads Pair Recall ) from 50.2% to 68.7% relative to None, yet conflict detection ( Judge Pass ) falls from 16.2% to 13.2%. Optional achieves the highest pooled detection rate, at 24.2%. Tracing the conflicting claims leads to the largest drop in evidence preservation at the worker-to-lead handoff. Under Required, Pair Recall falls from 68.7% at Source Reads to 24.4% at Lead Evidence. Workers report both claims for only 35.2% of the pairs they collectively retrieve, leaving the lead without the complete pair its workers retrieved. As the first stage-wise empirical study of multi-agent memory inconsistency, this paper makes three key contributions: • Benchmark and profiler (§3). We construct 73 controlled numerical conflicts across 11 Harvey LAB document-review tasks (Gru-pen et al., 2026), with a profiler tracing both claims through Source Reads, Worker Reports, Lead Evidence, and the Final Report. Pair Re-call measures evidence coverage separately from semantic conflict detection under the three delegation policies. • Evidence loss at the worker-to-lead hand-off (§4.3). Pooled across models and runs under comparable generation spending, Re-quired retrieves more complete evidence pairs than None but detects fewer conflicts. The largest measured loss occurs at the worker-to-lead handoff, where workers omit claims. • Different outcomes for Same and Split ev-idence (§4.4). In pooled cohorts defined by Required’s Source Reads, Required detects more conflicts than None on identical cases when one worker reads both claims (34.2% versus 22.5%), but fewer when the claims are split across workers (10.0% versus 18.9%). 

2 Background and Related Work 

2.1 Scaling and Delegation in Agent Systems 

LLM agents interleave reasoning traces with ac-tions on external environments (Yao et al., 2023) and retain information across steps and trials through memory and reflection (Shinn et al., 2023; Park et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024a). Multi-agent systems divide tasks among communicat-ing agents with assigned roles (Li et al., 2023; Qian et al., 2024; Guo et al., 2024). In the orchestrator–worker pattern, a lead plans and synthesizes while workers execute subtasks (An-thropic, 2024, 2025c); Magentic-One similarly separates orchestration from specialized execution (Fourney et al., 2024). AutoGen supports config-urable conversations among agents and tools (Wu et al., 2024), while MetaGPT structures collabora-tion through specialized roles and standard operat-ing procedures (Hong et al., 2024). Coding agents have followed the same path. SWE-bench evaluates the resolution of real GitHub issues by editing a repository (Jimenez et al., 2024). SWE-agent addresses it through a purpose-built agent–computer interface (Yang et al., 2024), Open-Hands provides an open platform for developer agents that supports coordination among multi-ple agents (Wang et al., 2025), and MAGIS di-vides planning and coding among four role-specific agents (Tao et al., 2024). Current coding har-nesses provide subagents that work in their own context windows or threads and return summaries to the main agent (Anthropic, 2026; OpenAI, 2026). For long-form writing, STORM gathers material through simulated conversations in which writ-ers with different perspectives question a source-grounded expert (Shao et al., 2024), and DeepRe-search Bench evaluates the reports and citations of deep-research agents (Du et al., 2026). Multi-agent debate was proposed to improve factuality and reasoning (Du et al., 2024), yet a single agent with strong prompts nearly matches the best discussion methods (Wang et al., 2024b), and debate systems do not reliably outperform self-consistency or ensembling without careful tuning (Smit et al., 2024). The benefit of multi-agent over single-agent systems diminishes as model ca-pabilities improve (Gao et al., 2025), and single agents match or outperform multi-agent systems on multi-hop reasoning when reasoning tokens are held constant (Tran and Kiela, 2026). Controlled comparisons show that agent-system performance depends on task structure (Kim et al., 2026). Agen-tic systems can be designed automatically (Hu et al., 2025a), although automatically designed multi-agent systems can incur greater cost with-out outperforming strong single-agent baselines (Jwalapuram et al., 2026). 2(a) Benchmark Workflow

Context policies govern which evidence agents receive and retain (Yu et al., 2026b); history edits can also increase execution cost when they disrupt prefix caching, even with fewer prompt tokens (Yu et al., 2026a). For coding agents, masking old ob-servations halves cost relative to an unmanaged history while matching the solve rate of LLM sum-marization (Lindenbauer et al., 2025), and com-paction can silently remove task constraints (Salle et al., 2026). Iterative context rewriting can like-wise erode details over time (Zhang et al., 2026b). For delegated workflows, we track which retrieved facts reach the lead and final report, together with conflict detection and generation spending. 

2.2 Distributed Evidence and Multi-Agent Failures 

MAST categorizes failures in multi-agent traces across frameworks (Cemri et al., 2025), and Agent-Debug diagnoses errors in agent trajectories and supplies corrective feedback (Zhu et al., 2025). Failure attribution methods aim to identify the agent and step responsible for a failed trajectory (Zhang et al., 2025b, 2026a). HiddenBench tests collective reasoning and communication by supply-ing complete information to single agents and dis-tributing facts across multi-agent groups (Li et al., 2026). In our setting, both single-agent and multi-agent systems must first retrieve evidence from document collections and then produce long-form reports, exposing what happens before and after workers select findings to report. A practitioner account attributes conflicting sub-agent outputs to context that the subagents do not share and recommends sharing full agent traces (Yan, 2025), whereas recommended subagent de-signs return condensed summaries to the lead (An-thropic, 2025b,a). An information-theoretic anal-ysis treats a model that compresses context for another model as a noisy channel and finds that the mutual information between a context and its compression predicts downstream performance (He et al., 2026). We measure this handoff for specified claims. 3Tracing tools provide a complementary basis for inspecting these workflows. AgentSight correlates LLM traffic with system events (Zheng et al., 2025), and PROV-AGENT connects agent interactions to workflow provenance (Souza et al., 2025). Our pro-filer uses captured execution records to track spec-ified conflicting claims: which agents read them, which contributors report them, and whether the final report identifies their discrepancy. 

2.3 Agent Memory and Conflicting Evidence 

MemGPT manages memory tiers to extend an agent’s limited context window (Packer et al., 2023), and surveys organize the design and eval-uation of agent memory (Zhang et al., 2025d; Hu et al., 2025b). Benchmarks test long-term mem-ory across conversation sessions (Maharana et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2025). G-Memory organizes multi-agent collaboration trajectories in a three-tier graph hierarchy (Zhang et al., 2025a). An architectural account of multi-agent memory separates update visibility from conflict resolution over shared or distributed state (Yu et al., 2026c). StateFuse de-signs memory interfaces that preserve conflicting claims (Volkov et al., 2026), while Facts Without Rules traces privacy-related usage constraints lost during handoffs (Wang et al., 2026). Inconsistency also arises among the context views served to a sin-gle coding agent: CodeNib compiles lexical, dense, and structural views of one commit so that their results can be cross-checked, and reports that incre-mentally maintained views can diverge from a full rebuild even when every update completes without error (Yu et al., 2026b). We trace known factual comparisons through source inspection and worker reporting in document analysis, measuring whether both claims remain available for a joint conclusion. Knowledge-conflict studies examine how mod-els respond when supplied passages contradict parametric knowledge or one another (Longpre et al., 2021; Chen et al., 2022; Xie et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2024). WikiContradict and ContraDoc evaluate models on contradictory passages and self-contradictory documents (Hou et al., 2024; Li et al., 2024). Model performance also degrades when relevant information sits in the middle of a long context (Liu et al., 2024), and summarizing the in-sights repeated across many documents remains an open challenge for long-context LLMs and RAG systems (Laban et al., 2024). These evaluations give a single system the evidence to be reconciled, whereas our setting requires separate agents to re-trieve and report both claims before any model can compare them. FEVER evaluates supporting-evidence recovery alongside claim verification (Thorne et al., 2018), and HotpotQA evaluates supporting facts alongside multi-hop answers (Yang et al., 2018). Adapting the complete-evidence-group principle to the two claims defining a planted conflict lets us measure evidence recovery at each stage separately from semantic detection. 

3 Benchmark and Measurement 

We evaluate whether agents flag planted numeri-cal conflicts in their reports, using the document edits, delegation policies, and profiling pipeline summarized in Figure 2. 

3.1 Conflict Injection in Harvey LAB 

We build on 11 Harvey LAB due-diligence tasks in which agents review document directories and write red-flags reports (Grupen et al., 2026). Each task retains its original instructions and document collection, with controlled factual edits and conflict-specific grading criteria. To plant conflict i, we replace selected appear-ances of a recurring numerical value Ai with Bi

in designated directories, leaving Ai unchanged elsewhere. Each conflict specification records the values, source locations, permitted equivalent repre-sentations, edits, and grading criteria. Five to seven conflicts appear per task, totaling 73; all conflicts in a task are evaluated against the same report. Agents receive the ordinary task prompt and edited documents. The evaluator keeps the conflict specifications and grading rubrics separate from the agent inputs. A successful report flags the dis-crepancy, states the need for reconciliation, and accurately treats the quantitative comparison.

Under Optional , the lead may inspect sources itself, create workers, and assign overlapping reviews. Under Required , workers in-spect assigned document partitions while the lead coordinates and synthesizes their findings without reading source documents. Workers can also con-tribute to a shared report draft. Optional measures whether models choose to delegate, whereas Required evaluates execution un-der mandatory delegation. It adapts the separation of coordination and execution used by Magentic-One, which routes tool use through specialized agents (Fourney et al., 2024), and the original Agen-tOrchestra planner, which delegates environment interaction to sub-agents (Zhang et al., 2025c). 3.3 Evidence-Flow Profiling 

Our profiler captures model requests and responses through a local proxy and correlates them with SDK events, native traces, tool outputs, and report artifacts. Agent and tool-call identifiers attribute evidence to its reader, sender, or recipient within the trajectory prefix that produced the graded report. Source evidence is counted from returned content, including search excerpts. We follow four measurement stages. Source Reads captures endpoints visible in source-tool out-puts; Worker Reports captures outgoing worker messages and completion reports, including peer-directed messages. Lead Evidence combines in-coming evidence with the lead’s permitted source reads. Final Report contains endpoints in the se-lected, graded deliverable. Figures and tables ab-breviate these as Source, Worker, Lead, and Final. A uniform matcher identifies endpoints through fact-specific anchors and verified equivalent repre-sentations. We track outgoing messages and incom-ing receipts separately, using plaintext collabora-tion traces for GPT-6 delegated executions. Source Reads, Worker Reports, and Lead Evidence accu-mulate observations through the graded checkpoint; Final Report measures the selected deliverable.

4.2 Delegation Behavior and Worker Contributions 

Under Optional, Haiku creates workers in all 22 executions, whereas GPT-5.6 does so in 9/22 and GPT-6 in 10/22 (Figure 3a). Workers also con-tribute different amounts of evidence. Across all 146 conflict observations per model, both GPT models’ Worker Reports achieve Pair Recall of only 0.7% (1/146) under Optional, compared with 24.0% under Required for GPT-5.6 and 18.5% for GPT-6. Haiku’s workers achieve 37.0% under Op-tional and 30.8% under Required. Required lets us compare evidence preservation under mandatory delegation across models with different tendencies to delegate. 

4.3 From Evidence Collection to Conflict Detection 

Pooled across models and runs, Required increases Source Reads Pair Recall to 68.7%, compared with 50.2% under None, but Judge Pass falls from 16.2% to 13.2% (Figure 3b). Optional reaches 62.8% Source Pair Recall and 24.2% detection. Collecting more evidence at the source stage therefore does not guarantee higher conflict detection in the final report. The gap is visible within each model. Relative to None, Required raises Source Reads Pair Re-call by 8.9 percentage points (pp) for GPT-5.6 and 2.1 pp for GPT-6, while detection falls by 5.5 pp for each. Haiku gains 44.5 pp in Source Pair Re-call but only 2.1 pp in detection. Optional has the highest detection rate for all three models. Figure 3c shows the difference between Source Reads and Final Report Pair Recall, divided into the decline before and after Lead Evidence. Required has a larger total decline than None for every model, with most of its decline occurring before the lead. Under None, source reads enter the lead’s evidence, so there is no drop between those two stages. Each bar measures percentage-point changes over all 146 conflict observations for that model and policy. 7Across all 438 Required observations, Source Reads contain both claims in 301 cases (68.7%), compared with 107 (24.4%) at Worker Reports and Lead Evidence. Of the 301 pairs present at Source Reads, 106 also appear in Worker Reports (35.2%). Worker Reports and Lead Evidence have identical labels, locating the observed handoff gap between source inspection and worker reporting. Collective retrieval therefore often fails to provide the lead with both claims needed to detect a conflict.

Figure 4 examines whether conflict detection dif-fers when one agent supplies both claims and when the claims are split across agents. The two groupings there-fore describe evidence contributors at their respec-tive stages. 

5 Conclusion 

Answering where factual conflicts disappear in multi-agent LLM systems requires separating re-trieval coverage from distributed memory consis-tency. While mandatory delegation (Required) broadens collective source retrieval over single-agent execution (None), net conflict detection drops because evidence fractures across two stages. First, at the worker-to-lead handoff, workers summariz-ing isolated partitions lack the cross-document con-text needed to recognize figures that appear un-problematic in isolation as conflicting across doc-uments, omitting one or both claims in 73.2% of split-evidence cases compared with 50.5% when a single worker reads both. Second, even when both conflicting claims reach the lead, conflict res-olution depends heavily on provenance: leads flag 72.2% of conflicts supplied by a single contributor (Same), yet only 17.1% when claims arrive divided across separate worker reports (Split). For system designers, these findings caution against rigid, non-overlapping context partition-ing on cross-document verification tasks; permit-ting overlapping source access and lead source inspection (Optional) consistently outperforms both single-agent and strictly partitioned execu-tion. More broadly, end-to-end task accuracy masks opposing retrieval gains and coordination losses. Our benchmark and evidence-flow profiler allow practitioners to separate source discovery, handoff retention, and lead synthesis, providing a testbed for structured reporting protocols and conflict-preserving multi-agent memory. 

Limitations 

Our evidence comes from 11 Harvey LAB tasks with planted numerical conflicts and two runs per model. Broader document domains, non-numerical conflicts, and more repetitions would test how widely the observed patterns hold, with uncertainty estimates accounting for shared tasks. The profiler measures cumulative evidence presence; tracing when both claims become jointly available would extend this analysis. Manual inspection identified three saved Judge Pass labels that credited neigh-boring comparisons rather than the planted conflict; broader human review could refine endpoint match-ing and semantic grading. Required combines mandatory delegation, par-titioned source assignments, and restricted lead access, while model families use different SDKs. The matched groups also reflect agents’ observed retrieval and reporting choices. Supplying identi-cal evidence while varying which agents receive it would help separate the effect of distribution from retrieval difficulty. Reporting interventions could then test whether preserving paired claims improves detection. Budgets were calibrated from observed spending, and GPT-5.6 Required check-points were selected retrospectively; future evalu-ations should fix budgets and checkpoint rules in advance. 8References
