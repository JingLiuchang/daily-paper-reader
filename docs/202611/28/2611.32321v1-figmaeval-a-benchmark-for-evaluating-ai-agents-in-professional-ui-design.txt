Abstract
Recent advances in AI agents have enabled autonomous UI
design using professional tools such as Figma, yet eval-
uating their design capabilities remains challenging due
to the open-ended nature of design tasks and the absence
of unique ground truths.
We introduce FigmaEval, a
benchmark for evaluating AI agents on professional UI de-
sign tasks, comprising 84 tasks and 783 screens derived
from real-world Figma design files.
FigmaEval adopts
a constrained-specification, open-solution paradigm, stan-
dardizing task requirements and design resources while al-
lowing agents to independently determine layouts, visual hi-
erarchies, component composition, and visual presentation.
To assess design quality beyond reference similarity, we de-
velop 22 expert-grounded evaluation rubrics across six di-
mensions and introduce a human evaluation arena where
professional designers compare agent-generated designs
through blinded pairwise judgments.
Evaluating eleven
models under a unified Figma agent setup, we find sub-
stantial agreement between rubric-based assessments and
human preferences, alongside distinct differences in design
practices and quality–efficiency trade-offs. FigmaEval pro-
vides a framework for systematically assessing professional
UI design capabilities and understanding the strengths and
limitations of current AI agents.
1. Introduction
User Interface (UI) design builds on iterative design prac-
tices in which sketches and prototypes support the explo-
ration and refinement of alternatives [1–3]. Research on
creativity-support tools and professional design practice ex-
amines how tools support exploration and how designers
apply design knowledge [4–6]. In March 2026, Figma en-
abled AI agents to create and modify native design artifacts
directly through its official Model Context Protocol (MCP)
server [7]. This development raises an important question:
how can we evaluate agents’ ability to autonomously pro-
duce professional-quality UI designs in Figma?
Existing benchmarks do not fully address this question.
Code-oriented benchmarks primarily evaluate frontend im-
plementation, such as HTML and CSS generation [8, 9].
Design Arena [10] evaluates human preferences across gen-
erated websites, applications, and presentations, but does
not specifically assess native, editable Figma design ar-
tifacts.
CANVAS [11] evaluates tool-based UI design
through replication and modification tasks, where perfor-
mance is measured against predefined reference designs or
target states.
However, professional UI design often re-
quires translating product requirements into original design
solutions, rather than reproducing existing interfaces. Eval-
uating agents’ autonomous design capabilities in Figma
therefore remains underexplored.
Evaluating this capability presents two key challenges.
First, unconstrained design briefs allow agents to vary the
scope and complexity of their solutions, undermining fair

2. Related Work
2.1. UI Generation Benchmarks.
Early work such as pix2code [12] explored generating inter-
face code from screenshots. Design2Code [8] benchmarks
screenshot-to-code generation, while Sketch2Code [13]
evaluates sketch-to-webpage prototyping, including itera-
tive refinement through simulated user feedback or clar-
ification.
Figma2Code [14] and FigmaBench [9] assess
design-to-code generation from structured Figma inputs.
Beyond code-based UI implementation, ReDesign [15]
reconstructs editable design structures from raster im-
ages, while CANVAS [11] evaluates tool-based UI de-
sign through reference-guided replication and modification
tasks. In contrast, FigmaEval evaluates agents’ ability to
autonomously create native, editable Figma designs from
standardized product requirements without prescribed ref-
erence layouts.
2.2. UI Design Quality and Creativity Assessment.
Beyond visual fidelity, research on web aesthetics distin-
guishes classical and expressive aesthetic dimensions [16].
DesignScape [17] supports graphic design through interac-
tive layout refinement and brainstorming suggestions. For
UI assessment, UIClip [18] predicts design quality and se-
mantic relevance.
Duan et al. [19] use GPT-4 to gen-
erate heuristic-based feedback on Figma mockups, while
UICrit [20] provides professional designers’ critiques and
quality ratings to improve automated UI feedback. AUV-
Bench [21] examines aesthetic scoring, diagnosis, and
repair, whereas Design Creativity Bench [22] evaluates
originality, creative diversity, and requirement satisfaction.
Building on these efforts, FigmaEval extends professional
UI design quality assessment to native, editable Figma ar-
tifacts, introducing expert-grounded rubrics that evaluate
both visual quality and underlying design structure.
2.3. Human Preference-Based Design Evaluation.
Human preferences provide a complementary approach to
evaluating open-ended outputs, as exemplified by Chat-
bot Arena’s pairwise evaluation of language models [23].
In design, UI-Bench [24] collects blinded expert pair-
wise comparisons of generated websites, while Design
Arena [10] evaluates a broad range of generated artifacts,
including websites, mobile apps, presentations, and 3D as-
sets, through pairwise preferences, aggregating them into
Bradley–Terry-based Elo ratings for model ranking. Fig-
maEval incorporates blinded pairwise comparisons by pro-
fessional designers to assess agent-generated Figma arti-
facts and independently validate its fine-grained rubric-
based evaluation.
3. Benchmark
We introduce FigmaEval, a benchmark for evaluating AI
agents’ ability to autonomously create professional-quality,
native, and editable UI designs in Figma. Rather than re-
producing reference interfaces, FigmaEval requires agents
to translate standardized product requirements into design
solutions. The benchmark preserves design freedom while
ensuring task comparability and capturing the complexity
of real-world, multi-screen UI design.
3.1. Task Formulation
Each task provides agents with a standardized product spec-
ification defining the required screens, functional require-
ments, and available design resources. Agents use Figma
MCP to produce all required screens as native, editable
Figma artifacts. The expected output is a coherent design
deliverable rather than an executable application. Original
reference layouts are withheld, requiring agents to indepen-
dently determine how the specified requirements should be
organized and presented. As illustrated in Fig. 2, the same
Foodgo task specification and shared design resources lead
to visibly different design solutions. The agents make dis-
tinct decisions regarding information hierarchy, spatial or-
ganization, component composition, and visual asset place-
ment, despite receiving identical design constraints. This
example highlights the distinction between fixed task re-
quirements and agent-controlled design decisions, which is
central to FigmaEval’s open-ended evaluation setting.
3.2. Task Construction
We develop a structured pipeline to transform existing UI
designs into standardized, design-agnostic task specifica-
tions. The pipeline comprises four stages: source curation
and scope selection, requirement abstraction and data nor-
malization, design resource preparation, and quality valida-
tion.
Source Curation and Scope Selection. We curate 84 pub-
lic design files from Figma Community, prioritizing coher-
ent product scenarios with multiple high-fidelity screens,
meaningful functional workflows, and sufficient design
complexity.
We exclude isolated single-screen concepts,
wireframes, component libraries, and incomplete or redun-
dant designs. For each source file, we establish a consistent
task scope by identifying the target platform, visual theme,
and required screens.
Requirement Abstraction and Data Normalization. We
employ AI-assisted extraction to identify product context,
screen requirements, functional elements, navigation rela-
tionships, and interface content from the source designs.
Professional designers then review and refine the extracted
information into structured task specifications. Rather than
prescribing the visual composition of the original inter-
faces, each screen is described in terms of its intended

Design Resource Preparation. To reduce variation aris-

solution.
Quality Validation. Professional designers review the re-

requirement completeness, factual correctness, and cross-
screen consistency. The validation process further examines
whether agents can identify all required deliverables from
the specifications and whether multiple visually distinct so-
lutions remain feasible.
Task Packaging. Following construction and validation,
each task is packaged as a read-only Figma Starter File
comprising four pages. 00 Start Here contains the prod-
uct brief, required screens, functional requirements, fixed
content and data, and navigation relationships. 01 Foun-
dations provides essential branding constraints, including
primary colors and typography. 02 Components supplies
basic interface elements. 04 Media contains organized vi-
sual assets, including images, logos, illustrations, and icons.
3.3. Benchmark Composition
FigmaEval comprises 84 UI design tasks requiring a total
of 783 screens, averaging 9.3 screens per task. As illus-
trated in Fig. 3, the benchmark spans eight application do-
mains and twelve screen categories, covering diverse prod-
uct contexts and interface functionalities.

ity evaluation and independent human preference evaluation
for holistic design judgments.

Figma Design Arena. Following Design Arena [10], we
establish Figma Design Arena to collect professional de-
signers’ preferences through blinded pairwise comparisons
of agent-generated Figma artifacts. Reviewers inspect the
standardized product specification, aligned screenshots, and
editable Figma files, selecting the preferable overall de-
sign deliverable based on requirement completeness, infor-
mation organization, visual quality, and usability. Model
identities and automated scores are concealed, and left–
right placement is randomized. Direct preference votes are
aggregated using the Bradley–Terry model [28] to derive
human-preference rankings, providing an independent ba-
sis for comparison with rubric-based evaluation.
4. Experiments
4.1. Setup
Evaluated Models. We evaluate eleven models: Claude
Sonnet 5.5, Claude Opus 5.5, DeepSeek V4 Flash, GPT-
6.1 Sol, GPT-6 Astra, Qwen 3.8 Max, Fable 5.1, GLM-5.3
Flash, SenseNova 6.8 Flash, Gemini 3.5 Flash, and Kimi
K3.
Agent Setup. Tool-using language agents can interleave
reasoning with environment actions and observations [29].
All models operate within the same Claude Code agent har-
ness. To isolate native Figma design capabilities from code-
to-design pipelines, we restrict the available MCP tools
to whoami, get_metadata, get_screenshot, and
use_figma.
This configuration supports structural in-
spection, visual feedback, and direct manipulation of Figma
elements through the Plugin API, while excluding auxiliary
code-based workflows.
VLM-as-a-Judge. We evaluate the generated designs us-
ing three VLM judges: Claude Opus 5.5, GPT-6.1 Sol, and
DeepSeek V4.1 Flash.
Judging is repeated across three
seeded passes (42, 102, and 423).
Prior work identifies
systematic biases in model-based judges [30], motivating
validation against independent human judgments. We mea-
sure model-level ranking agreement using Kendall’s τ and
comparison-level agreement using the proportion of pair-
wise preferences consistent with human judgments.
Evaluation Metrics. We assess human–VLM alignment
at two levels. At the model level, Kendall’s τ measures
the agreement between model rankings derived from hu-
man preference win rates and mean rubric scores. At the
design-pair level, we measure the proportion of within-task
comparisons in which rubric-based preferences agree with
human judgments.

4.2. Overall Evaluation
Overall Performance. Table 2 shows that Claude Opus
5.5 and GPT-6 Astra form the leading group under both
human preference and rubric-based evaluation, with Opus
ranking first in both Elo (1219.59) and overall rubric score
(86.70%). However, aggregate rankings mask distinct capa-
bility profiles. GPT-6 Astra slightly surpasses Opus in user
experience and layout quality, but scores lower than even
Claude Sonnet 5.5 in deliverable quality. Across the six
evaluation dimensions, layout quality exhibits the largest
variation among models (55.07%–87.51%), suggesting that
spatial organization remains a key differentiator in profes-
sional UI design.
Alignment with Human Preferences.
Fig. 4 compares
Elo-based human preference rankings with rubric scores av-
eraged across three VLM judges. The two evaluations show
substantial agreement at the model level (Kendall’s τb =
0.764), with Claude Opus 5.5 and GPT-6 Astra occupying
the top two positions under both evaluations. At the design-
pair level, rubric-based preferences agree with blinded pro-
fessional judgments in 76.58% of comparisons. Together,
these results demonstrate substantial alignment between
rubric-based evaluation and professional designer prefer-
ences at both the model-ranking and individual-comparison
levels.

4.4. Design Quality Diagnostics
Design Practices of Leading Agents. To investigate the
design practices associated with stronger performance, we
analyze agents’ Figma interaction trajectories, focusing on
design-system inspection, layout construction, and compo-
nent reuse. Before making modifications, Claude Opus 5.5
inspects the provided design system, including typography,
variables, and reusable components, in 100% of runs, com-
pared with 14–29% for GLM-5.3 Flash, Qwen 3.8 Max,
and Fable 5.1. During construction, Opus performs an aver-
age of 0.95 auto-layout operations per newly created frame,
names 6.6 layers per frame, and creates 20 components per
screen. In contrast, both GPT models rely predominantly
on absolute positioning, with minimal use of auto-layout.
Opus also makes more systematic use of shared styles and
variables across its designs.
These observations reveal a distinction between visual
layout quality and the structural quality of editable design
artifacts. Although GPT-6 Astra slightly outperforms Opus
in layout quality (87.51% vs. 86.58%), Opus achieves sub-
stantially higher deliverable quality (86.42% vs. 69.94%)
and ranks first in human preference. This contrast suggests
that producing visually effective layouts and maintaining
well-structured, reusable Figma artifacts represent related
but distinct capabilities.
Opus’s design-system-oriented
workflow is consistent with its stronger deliverable quality,
highlighting the importance of evaluating not only rendered
interfaces but also the underlying design structures.
Representative Failure Modes. To investigate the limita-
tions of current UI design agents, professional designers in-
spected generated artifacts and identified four representative
failure patterns, as illustrated in Fig. 7. (1) Empty Space:
agents fail to adapt provided images to target screen lay-
outs, resulting in poorly scaled assets and excessive empty
space. (2) Image–text overlap: visual elements obscure
textual content, compromising readability. (3) Data–visual
Inconsistent: visual representations contradict the underly-
ing data, such as progress bars that fail to reflect actual val-
ues. (4) Long-content Overflow: agents compress exten-
sive content into a single viewport rather than organizing it
into scrollable regions. With additional examples provided
in the supplementary material.

isolated cases, we further quantify failure prevalence across

are available, and that the brief can be satisfied by multi-

B. Evaluation Protocols
B.1. Complete Rubric Definitions
Table 1 summarizes the criterion counts and maximum
scores by dimension.
We evaluate each generated Figma design using version
4 of a fixed rubric with 22 scored criteria. The criteria cover
two perspectives: usability and ease of use (A, B, G; 27
points) and aesthetics (D, E, F; 26 points). The six dimen-
sions, using the terminology of the main paper, are user ex-
perience (A1–A4, 8 points), information organization (B1–
B5, 15 points), deliverable quality (G1–G2, 4 points), lay-
out quality (D1–D4, 12 points), consistency (E1–E2, 4
points), and visual presentation (F1–F5, 10 points). B1–
B5 and D1–D4 use integer scores from 0 to 3. The other
thirteen criteria use integer scores from 0 to 2. The item-
specific anchors below govern scoring.
Pre-scoring checks.
Scoring begins only after the gener-
ated artifact passes the rule-based screen-count and blank-
screen checks described in Sec. A.2. Artifacts that fail either
check are not scored; they must pass both checks after cor-
rection or regeneration before entering evaluation. For an
eligible artifact, the total score S is the sum of the 22 crite-
rion scores, with a maximum of 53. A percentage score is
reported as 100S/53.
Evidence and scoring procedure.
The judge reviews all
supplied screenshots in sequence before scoring, using the
task brief for context and for identifying required core
screens. Each item receives a score and a brief rationale
identifying the screen, region, observed evidence, and its
effect on the user or design. The original Chinese prompt
asks for 30–120 Chinese characters per item. Scoring fol-
lows impact, and a defect is counted under multiple items
only when it has independently observable effects. Appli-
cable aspects are assessed within each item. For example, a
workflow without a step indicator can receive full credit on
A2 when that indicator is unnecessary. Dynamic behavior
that cannot be established from screenshots is marked un-
verified in the rationale. When A3 is judged without realis-
tic content, its conclusion is limited to the supplied sample.
The output contains an integer for every item. G1 and G2
use the Figma layer summary, with a score of 1 for each
affected item and an explicit missing-evidence note if the
summary is unavailable or inadequate.

Judges and evaluation evidence.

than individual screens. The original reference design is not
used as a scoring target.

B.3. Human Preference Evaluation Protocol
Review unit and blinding.
Professional designers com-
pare two submissions for the same task.
The interface
provides the standardized task specification, aligned screen
views, and access to the editable Figma artifacts, as de-
scribed in Sec. 3.4. Model identities and automated scores
are concealed, and the left–right assignment is randomized.
Reviewers assess the overall deliverable using requirement
completeness, information organization, visual quality, and
usability. An inability to judge is recorded separately rather
than converted into a loss or a tie. Each overall observa-
tion is linked to its task, reviewer, review round, candidate
identifiers, and artifact version.
Pair selection and within-task ordering.
The Arena
supports incremental pairwise sorting to avoid repeatedly
asking a reviewer to compare designs whose order is already
known within that review session. Direct comparisons es-
tablish partial orders; subsequent comparisons merge or-
dered groups or insert a new submission. Transitive rela-
tions can advance the sorting procedure, but are stored sep-
arately from the submitted votes. Relations are not trans-
ferred between reviewers, tasks, rounds, or artifact ver-
sions. Cyclic or unresolved evidence requires review be-
fore a complete task ordering is published. Corrected votes
remain in the history, while superseded observations are
removed from the active evidence. A completed ordering
summarizes that review session; it does not turn every in-
ferred pair into a new independent human vote.

C.1. Synthetic Defect Construction
We construct 1,485 repair tasks from 846 source Figma
screens by injecting rubric-guided defects. Given an orig-
inal screen, rule-based miners identify eligible elements,
and mechanism-specific mutators apply executable edits to
produce a corrupted screen.
We define 20 defect mech-
anisms spanning information organization, layout quality,
consistency, visual presentation, and deliverable quality.
Each task contains multiple defect instances, with weighted
sampling prioritizing layout defects while limiting the fre-
quency of individual mechanisms.
Compatibility checks
address node, subtree, and spatial conflicts, while geomet-
ric and layer-order heuristics identify existing occlusion.
Where applicable, perturbation magnitudes are set to large
or extra-large.

D.2. Additional Failure Cases
Failure Occurrence Statistics.
To characterize recurring design failures, we analyze the evaluation feedback obtained
using our 22 rubrics and group commonly observed issues into representative failure categories. We then identify these failure
patterns from the rubric judges’ textual feedback using rule-based matching. For each evaluation run, a failure category is
counted once if the corresponding issue is identified, regardless of how many times it occurs. Multiple failure categories may
co-occur within the same run. The occurrence rate of each category is calculated as the proportion of runs exhibiting that
failure. As shown in Fig. 7, excessive empty space (70%), logic/data inconsistencies (68%), and misalignment or uneven
spacing (65%) are among the most frequently observed issues.
