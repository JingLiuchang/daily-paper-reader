Abstract
Annually 1.9 million deaths and 89.7 million disability-adjusted life years are
caused by exposure to agents during work (e.g., welding fumes, silica dust,
and noise pollution). To improve worker health, high-quality exposure as-
sessments are crucial. Therefore, free-text job descriptions are collected in
large-scale occupational cohorts and manually standardized through occu-
pational classifications. Subsequently, using Job Exposure Matrices (JEM),
these classifications are linked to hazardous exposures.
However, manual
coding is slow, unreliable (i.e., 44–89% inter-coder reliability), and costly,
due to i) short, ambiguous free-text job descriptions, ii) classifications con-
taining hundreds to thousands of categories, and iii) the absence of gold
standards. With 46%–79% accuracy, state-of-the-art automatic occupational
coding tools achieve classification performance on human level. However,
their performance drops to 17–26% when tested on external, out-of-distribution
datasets. Furthermore, their supervised architectures limit them to occupa-
tional classifications present in the training set. Consequently, JEM utiliz-

assessment, improving worker health.

1. Introduction
Work is a central part of adult life, with a large impact on overall health.
Workers are exposed to various negative conditions, such as repetitive mo-
tions, noise pollution, and physical or chemical agents like welding fumes
and silica dust (GBD 2013 Risk Factors Collaborators, 2015). These occu-
pational exposures are globally responsible for 1.9 million deaths annually
and contribute 89.7 million disability-adjusted life years (GBD 2016 Occupa-
tional Risk Factors Collaborators, 2020). Epidemiological studies that assess
exposure-response associations are crucial for fueling interventions to pro-
mote worker health, but they rely on high-quality exposure assessments to
ensure correct and unbiased results (Kriebel et al., 2007).
In large population-based cohorts, occupational data is typically col-
lected through open-ended survey questions, yielding free-text job descrip-
tions (Hoffmann et al., 1995). Subsequently, a Job Exposure Matrix (JEM)
can be used to assign a wide range of exposures to occupations (Peters et al.,
2022). A JEM comprises estimated probabilities and intensities of exposure
to harmful agents using standardized occupational titles and industries (Pe-
ters et al., 2016). Consequently, free-text job descriptions must first be stan-
dardized into such occupational groups using occupational classification sys-
2


tems. This process, known as occupational coding, is often performed man-
ually by expert coders, which is time-consuming, expensive, and requires ex-
pert knowledge. Even with extensive documentation and training, consistent
coding is challenging due to the large number of outcome categories in com-
bination with the lack of a gold standard for each job description (Hoffmann
et al., 1995). Consequently, many large-scale studies must deploy multiple
expert coders, which introduces an inter-coder reliability ranging from 44%
to 71% (Maaz et al., 2009; Russ et al., 2016; Conrad et al., 2016).
To address manual occupational coding challenges, several automated
coding tools have been developed with prediction accuracies ranging 46%–
79% at the most detailed coding level (Bao et al., 2020; Garcia et al., 2021;
Gweon et al., 2017; Jones and Elias, 2004; Kouretsis et al., 2020; Langezaal
et al., 2023; Park et al., 2015; Russ et al., 2016; Savic et al., 2022; Russ
et al., 2023, 2025a).
These classification models have been implemented
in Decision Support Systems (DSS) and semi-automatic coding tools that
provide confidence scores (i.e., estimated correctness probability) alongside
predictions (Langezaal et al., 2023; Jones and Elias, 2004; Russ et al., 2016,
2023). This enables automatic coding of suggestions exceeding a customiz-
able threshold, such as automatically coding all predictions with confidence
scores above 95%. At this 95% threshold, estimated workload reductions
range 19.7%–55.7% (Langezaal et al., 2023). When predictions with confi-
dence scores are presented to experts solely as suggestions without automatic
coding (i.e., as a DSS), experts experience a 37.5% workload reduction while
maintaining outcome validity (Langezaal et al., 2025).
Current automatic coding tools predominantly use supervised ML ap-
proaches. These rely on training data from occupational databases, which
contain job entries with accompanying labels, provided by experts. Both the
entries and the labels possess specific characteristics (see Figure 1). The la-
bels consist of attributes that have characteristics reflecting the geographical,
cultural, and study context of the sample. The labels reflect the labor market
from which the sample was drawn, as well as any study and expert biases.
In the development of supervised ML models, the data is often divided into
training and testing data. As the train and test samples are drawn from the
same database and, hence, from the same data distribution, they have simi-
lar characteristics. Consequently, their performance on both the training and
test data is similar (Bao et al., 2020; Garcia et al., 2021; Gweon et al., 2017;
Jones and Elias, 2004; Kouretsis et al., 2020; Langezaal et al., 2023; Park
et al., 2015; Russ et al., 2016; Savic et al., 2022; Russ et al., 2023, 2025a).

When automatic coding models are applied on out-of-distribution (OoD)
datasets (i.e., data sets with different job entry and label characteristics) in-
stead of within-distribution datasets, their performance drops significantly,
to 17%–26% (Wan et al., 2023).
Publicly available expert-labeled occupational datasets remain scarce,
which limits the development of supervised ML models with occupational
classification coverage required for universal use in many different occupa-
tional epidemiological studies. To enable such universal use, alternative train-
ing sources are required, such as coding indices published alongside occupa-
tional classification systems (Peterson et al., 1999). These indices provide
labeled examples suitable for supervised training. However, these examples
are limited (i.e., 1–5 per occupational group) and lack the heterogeneity of
real-world free-text job descriptions. Consequently, models trained solely on
4


these small, homogeneous corpora are prone to overfitting to coding index
phrasing and will likely fail to generalize to real-world occupational datasets,
limiting their OoD performance (Chicco, 2017).
LLMs reduce the dependence on extensive expert-labeled datasets by uti-
lizing their broad linguistic representations acquired during pretraining on
massive corpora of textual data (Naveed et al., 2025). Contrastingly to su-
pervised ML models that may overfit to specific textual characteristics of
occupational databases, LLMs can likely accurately process the linguistic
variation inherent in free-text job descriptions, making them a promising
alternative for AOC. However, despite these linguistic capabilities, LLMs’
performance is often limited in data-scarce domains underrepresented in
their pre-training corpora. When applied to such domains, these knowledge
gaps frequently result in hallucinations where the model generates plausible-
sounding but factually incorrect information, leading to increased classifica-
tion errors. Furthermore, the plausible reasoning provided alongside a clas-
sification may mislead an expert into accepting an incorrect suggested code.
Accurate classification of job descriptions is crucial for reliable exposure as-
sessment and subsequent health outcomes. Consequently, the potential for
such errors severely limits the reliable application of LLMs in data-scarce,
high-stakes domains such as AOC.
To improve LLMs’ performance in data-scarce domains, Retrieval-augmented
generation (RAG) supplements LLMs’ knowledge gaps with external, domain-specific
sources such as coding indices (Lewis et al., 2020). In addition to example
job descriptions, these indices contain relevant information used by experts
during occupational coding, including general descriptions and explanations
for each occupational group (International Labour Office, 2012). In addi-
tion to filling the LLMs’ knowledge gap, this information can be used by
LLMs to mimic expert-coder strategies to code occupations accurately, mak-
ing RAG a promising approach for AOC. Hence, we developed OPERAG,
a RAG framework enabling accurate OoD AOC without reliance on expert-
labeled databases. We aim to address four specific AOC challenges: 1) the
ambiguous and brief nature of job descriptions, 2) the high dimensionality
of outcome categories, 3) the scarcity of public expert-labeled data, and 4)
severe class imbalance. We address these challenges through the following
contributions:
1. Utilization of LLMs for AOC, harnessing their robust language under-
standing and reasoning capabilities to tackle AOC’s ambiguous and
5


short job descriptions and vast number of outcome categories.
2. A processing pipeline that enables effective usage of occupational clas-
sifications’ coding indices in RAG applications for AOC, eliminating
the need for expert-labeled data.
3. A RAG framework that uses coding indices as the sole knowledge base
for AOC, ensuring the classification process remains independent of
prior occupational distributions and thereby mitigating issues of class
imbalance.
The framework is assessed on occupational coding and exposure assessment
performance through evaluation on a real-world occupational dataset and
JEM applications. Consequently, this demonstrates the framework’s perfor-
mance on occupational classification and its practical implications for expo-
sure assessment in real-world occupational epidemiological studies.
The remainder of the paper is structured as follows: Section 2 introduces
the conceptual background of occupational coding, as well as the technical
background of LLMs and RAG. In Section 3, we describe the development of
OPERAG, including the description of the evaluation dataset and ISCO-88
coding index used to enable AOC without expert-labeled databases. Ad-
ditionally, we detail the construction of the RAG component, including its
structure-aware knowledge base and retriever, followed by the reasoning-LLM
reranking component, LLM-classifier design, and inference protocol. Subse-
quently, we outline the evaluation methodology for both occupational coding
and exposure assessment. In Section 4, we present OPERAG’s evaluation
results, including benchmarks against expert coders and other automatic
coding tools. We conclude in Section 5 with a discussion of practical impli-
cations, limitations, and avenues for future work.
2. Background
2.1. Occupational coding
In large-scale occupational cohorts, occupational information is collected
from respondents in free-text format (Hoffmann et al., 1995). Such job de-
scriptions are often ambiguous, incomplete, contain misspellings, and are fre-
quently very short (i.e., 2-4 words per description). To standardize these free-
text job descriptions, occupational classification systems are employed (In-
ternational Labour Office, 2012). These systems contain hundreds to thou-
sands of hierarchically structured occupational groups, where each group is
6


denoted by multiple characters (e.g., 3433). The first character denotes the
most aggregated occupational group, with each subsequent character adding
detail until the most specific group is reached, denoted by the last character.
This information is often published in the coding index alongside the occupa-
tional classification. The coding index contains the classification’s structure,
each group’s definitions, and example job descriptions for most occupational
groups. Although sharing similarities in structure, occupational classifica-
tion systems differ across countries to reflect differences in national labor
markets and social contexts (Emmel and Cosca, 2010). Consequently, hun-
dreds of national and international occupational classification systems have
been developed, each following its own taxonomy of occupational groups.
Manual occupational coding is typically performed by expert coders,
which is time-consuming, expensive, and requires specialized knowledge (Burstyn
et al., 2014). Even after extensive training and experience, coders generally
process only approximately 2,700 job episodes per month. This inherently
slow and variable nature of the occupational coding process stems from occu-
pational descriptions lacking a single, objective "gold-standard" code, except
for the specific examples in the coding index (International Labour Office,
2012). Consequently, expert coders must rely on a combination of the coding
index’s definitions and supplementary coding rules. These rules can be gen-
erally applicable (e.g., inclusion or exclusion criteria specified in the index)
and context-dependent rules (e.g., how to handle incomplete or ambiguous
occupational information or cases where only coding to a more aggregated
coding level is possible) (Hoffmann et al., 1995). Such rules are crucial for
ensuring consistent and accurate occupational coding to secure reliable re-
sults in the following study. However, although considered the gold-standard
for occupational coding, manual coding’s inter-coder reliability only ranges
from 42% to 71% (Maaz et al., 2009; Russ et al., 2016; Massing et al., 2019;
Conrad et al., 2016). Beyond inter-coder variability, expert coders are also
susceptible to intra-coder variability, as a single coder may assign different
codes to the same occupational information at different coding sessions (Pa-
tel et al., 2012). Combined, these factors highlight the inherent variability
and difficulty of occupational coding.
To address the challenges of manual occupational coding, several auto-
matic coding tools have been developed for different languages and occupa-
tional classification systems. These tools enable automatic classification of
job descriptions into occupational classifications without the need for human
intervention. They employ different classification architectures, ranging from
7


word-matching techniques to LLM-based classifiers. Consequently, classifi-
cation performance differs, ranging 46%–79% on the most detailed coding
level when tested on a subset obtained from the model’s training dataset
(i.e., the model’s within-distribution performance) (Bao et al., 2020; Gar-
cia et al., 2021; Gweon et al., 2017; Jones and Elias, 2004; Kouretsis et al.,
2020; Langezaal et al., 2023; Park et al., 2015; Russ et al., 2016; Savic et al.,
2022; Russ et al., 2023, 2025a). However, performance on data not stemming
from their training dataset is much lower due to the large difference in data
characteristics between occupational datasets, including description length
and used terminology. For example, the automatic coding systems developed
by (Kouretsis et al., 2020; Jones and Elias, 2004; Garcia et al., 2021) achieved
an OoD classification performance of 17%, 26%, and 20%, respectively. This
shows current tool’s inability to be effectively used in occupational stud-
ies using datasets other than the datasets used during model training. An
overview of current automatic coding tools, used occupational classifications,
and within-distribution classification performance can be found in Table 1.

2.2. Large Language Models
Deep Learning (DL) architectures, such as feedforward and long short-term
memory networks, have demonstrated state-of-the-art performance across di-
verse classification tasks, including text-classification (Minaee et al., 2021).
By employing multiple layers of interconnected neurons, these models auto-
matically derive representations of data with multiple levels of abstraction,
thereby minimizing the dependence on manually engineered features typically
required for traditional ML approaches.
However, their high parameteri-
zation requires extensive datasets to achieve competitive performance (Liu
et al., 2017). Consequently, in domains with limited example-outcome pairs
or homogeneous datasets that fail to capture the heterogeneity of the tar-
get domain, DL approaches are prone to overfitting or underfitting. Conse-
quently, this restricts their generalizability and limits their successful appli-
cation in such data-scarce environments (Neyshabur et al., 2017).
LLMs reduce DL’s dependence on large labeled datasets through transformer-
based architectures and large-scale unsupervised pretraining on diverse text
corpora, resulting in base models with extensive linguistic representations
and contextual understanding (Naveed et al., 2025). After pretraining, these
base models typically undergo additional supervised fine-tuning to improve
conversational fluency, instruction adherence, or to adapt their internal knowl-
edge and linguistic properties to specific domains (Peng et al., 2023). These
include instruction-tuned LLMs, which are trained on datasets containing
human-aligned instruction–response pairs, demonstrating strong dialogue and
task-following abilities. However, LLMs’ performance is limited in domains
that are insufficiently represented in their pretraining data (Yang et al.,
2024).
To address this limitation, domain-specific fine-tuning adjusts an
LLM’s parameters to capture domain terminology and knowledge (Anisuz-
zaman et al., 2024). However, the performance of such models is limited for
complex tasks (Ge et al., 2023). Here, performance improves when "Chain-of-
Thought" prompting is applied, which is a technique that explicitly prompts
10


the model to produce a series of intermediate reasoning steps before gener-
ating the final output. To further improve performance in complex tasks,
LLMs have been post-trained on curated datasets containing step-by-step
reasoning-answer pairs (Kumar et al., 2025). Through this training, models
generate structured intermediate reasoning steps during inference, achiev-
ing state-of-the-art performance across a wide range of tasks, including text
classification.
Interaction with an LLM typically occurs through a user-submitted prompt
that specifies the query, after which the model generates a corresponding
response (Marvin et al., 2023). Such interactions can be preceded by a pre-
defined system prompt to establish the model’s overall behavior or tone.
Both prompt types can be engineered using various prompting structures
and strategies (i.e., prompt engineering) to optimize performance for spe-
cific tasks. For text classification applications, the system prompt commonly
instructs the LLM to assume the role of an expert within the relevant clas-
sification domain (Clavié et al., 2023).
Subsequently, the user prompt is
structured following a consistent format, comprising: 1) a task description,
2) the input data requiring classification, and 3) an output schema to enable
consistent parsing. To guide the model’s reasoning through domain-relevant
classification steps, task-specific classification rules can be specified in the
user-prompt (Mu et al., 2023). Additionally, prompting strategies such as
chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting are often employed to further encour-
age step-by-step reasoning before final label prediction (Yeo et al., 2025).
Through the combination of these approaches, the LLM may mimic an ex-
pert’s decision-making process, which has been shown to yield state-of-the-art
classification performance for task-specific applications.
A major constraint of LLMs is their fixed knowledge after pre-training,
determined by the contents and cutoff date of their pretraining corpus (Patil
and Gudivada, 2024). Consequently, performance in data-scarce or highly
specialized domains is often limited, often resulting in responses with hallu-
cinations (Feng et al., 2024). These are linguistically coherent but factually
incorrect or irrelevant responses. When such outputs are misinterpreted as
accurate, they pose significant risks, especially in high-stakes contexts re-
lated to health. To overcome these limitations, the aforementioned domain
fine-tuning can result in more accurate responses (Peng et al., 2023). How-
ever, this process still requires large datasets containing relevant, hetero-
geneous example-output pairs to yield robust, generalizable model perfor-
mance (Rahman et al., 2025). Therefore, frameworks that enable effective
11


domain-specific specialization in data-scarce settings are crucial for improv-
ing LLM reliability and accuracy in niche domains.
2.3. Retrieval-Augmented Generation
Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) is a framework designed to miti-
gate the limitations of LLMs’ knowledge gaps by integrating external knowl-
edge sources into the generation process (Lewis et al., 2020). Here, RAG
supplements the LLMs’ knowledge with dynamic, query-specific knowledge
retrieved from validated sources such as domain-specific databases, curated
corpora, or structured knowledge graphs. This enables LLMs to adapt to
underrepresented domains and data shifts without model retraining or fine-
tuning (Yu et al., 2024).
A typical RAG system comprises two components, namely 1) a retriever,
which identifies relevant information from a knowledge base, and 2) a gen-
erator, which returns an output based on the retrieved content and user’s
query (Lewis et al., 2020).
The generator, usually an LLM, uses the re-
triever’s retrieved content in combination with the user’s query to generate
an output. Although the choice of generator has a high impact on subsequent
classification performance, RAG’s ability to effectively retrieve and supply
the generator with relevant knowledge hinges on the setup and development
of its retriever (Zhao et al., 2024b), consisting of three components:
• Knowledge Base, which is the set of external information from which
evidence can be retrieved;
• Indexing Process, which is the pre-processing procedure that pre-
pares information for retrieval;
• Retrieval Function, which is the algorithm used for selecting, rank-
ing, and returning a list of relevant entries
Subsequently, as the retrieval function is generally optimized for efficiency
and broad recall, a more accurate and computationally expensive reranker
may subsequently be applied to produce a shorter, more accurate candidate
list (Zhang et al., 2025). In the following paragraphs, each component will
be explained in more detail.
The knowledge base contains all retrievable relevant information (Su
et al., 2025). It can include documents such as research papers, technical
manuals, or news articles, as well as structured resources like ontologies,
12


knowledge graphs, or databases. To ensure all LLMs’ knowledge gaps rele-
vant to the current tasks are covered, it is essential that relevant, task-specific
information is included.
The indexing process adapts, converts, and stores task-specific informa-
tion to enable accurate retrieval from its knowledge base (Şakar and Emekci,
2025). Typically, this process consists of three stages: chunking, embedding,
and vector storage. Chunking divides large documents into smaller, seman-
tically coherent units to enhance retrieval performance. Here, when seman-
tic boundaries are unclear, fixed-length splits can be used (Stäbler et al.,
2025).
However, this may result in scattering related information across
different chunks, hindering effective retrieval.
Contrastingly, semantic or
structure-aware segmentation detects contextual shifts or uses inherent doc-
ument structure to maintain semantic coherence within chunks (Qu et al.,
2025). After chunking, embedders map text chunks into high-dimensional
vectors where semantically similar content are near to each other in the
embedding space, allowing similarity search functions to identify the most
relevant passages to the query (Akbik et al., 2019). Consequently, retrieval
performance depends on the embedder’s ability to assign similar embeddings
to queries and relevant content, for which several types of embedders are
available (Darrin et al., 2024). These include general-purpose embedders that
capture broad meaning, domain-specific ones that improve performance for
domain-terminology, and instruction-aware embedders that integrate explicit
task instructions into embeddings, ensuring results are not only semantically
similar but also aligned with the retrieval’s intended purpose.
The retrieval function is used to determine which chunks of information
are most relevant to the user’s query (Cuconasu et al., 2024). Here, similarity
metrics are often employed, including cosine similarity, inner product, or L2
distance. These metrics calculate what information chunks are closest to the
query in the embedding space, thereby aiming to retrieve the most relevant
information.
However, such similarity-based metrics often fail to capture
deeper contextual, logical, or task-specific relationships between the query
and the indexed information (Gan et al., 2024). Consequently, the top-k
candidates retrieved may include irrelevant or only partially related results,
which can degrade performance due to LLMs’ position bias (Shi et al., 2024).
This bias is a systematic tendency to weigh information differently depending
on its position in the input sequence rather than purely on its semantic
relevance, which can manifest in several ways, namely:
13


• over-attending to the beginning or end of a prompt regardless of their
importance;
• under-utilizing information in the middle of long contexts (the “lost in
the middle” phenomenon);
• favoring items purely based on list order (e.g., the first option) or for-
matting position when comparing alternatives.
These effects limit accurate reasoning and classification, particularly in long-context
tasks and multi-item comparisons such as occupational coding (Zhang et al.,
2024).
To ensure the most relevant entries from the retrieval function are in
the top positions when provided to the generator, rerankers are often em-
ployed (Zhang et al., 2025). These modules assign relevance scores by jointly
evaluating the query and each candidate passage (Li et al., 2024). In cross-encoder-
based rerankers, the query–candidate pair is jointly encoded to produce an
independent relevance score for each candidate, after which candidates are
sorted accordingly (Li et al., 2024). Because the model attends across both
texts token by token, this approach often yields higher retrieval accuracy than
purely semantic similarity search. However, since cross-encoders primarily
assess semantic relationships, their usefulness is limited in tasks requiring
rule-based or multi-step reasoning (Déjean et al., 2024). Contrastingly, LLM-
based rerankers can incorporate multi-step reasoning and rule-based evalu-
ation, leveraging their advanced linguistic and contextual understanding to
identify complex query–document relationships (Ren et al., 2025). Moreover,
they enable the integration of domain-specific rules and logic into the rerank-
ing process, improving retrieval accuracy in ambiguous or reasoning-intensive
scenarios.

coding index, and used its document structure to chunk the information into

fective storage and retrieval mechanism. Third, we implement a reasoning-
LLM-based reranker module to rerank the top-k retrieved candidates using

3.1. Dataset
To evaluate OPERAG’s OoD classification performance, we used the Asia-
Lymph dataset (Friesen et al., 2016). This dataset contains real-world En-
glish occupational information coded with ISCO-88, allowing a real-world
occupational coding evaluation. AsiaLymph is a prototypical occupational

applied ISCO-88 classification (see Table 2). Furthermore, AsiaLymph has
been widely employed for training and benchmarking job coding tools, par-

AsiaLymph is a hospital-based case–control study of lymphoma among
Chinese populations in Eastern Asia (Friesen et al., 2016). It includes free-
text job episodes in traditional and simplified Chinese containing job titles,
tasks, employers, and employers’ main products. This information was man-
ually coded into the 5th revision of the Standard Occupational Classifica-
tion System of the Republic of China (SOCS-ROC) (Directorate-General of
Budget, Accounting and Statistics (DGBAS), 1992). Subsequently, the in-
formation was expert-translated to English, with the codes crosswalked to
ISCO-88. Two study centers independently coded the occupational data in
the original language, with discordances and crosswalk discrepancies (i.e., in
the case of a one-to-many mapping) being resolved by a third expert coder.
In our evaluation, we use the job episodes’ English translation with corre-
sponding ISCO-88 codes.

“452”), and 390 unit groups (e.g., “4521”) (Peterson et al., 1999). Here, each
additional digit adds specificity to the coded occupational information. When
the occupational information lacked sufficient detail to code to the most
specific level, trailing zeros were used to denote non-codable levels (e.g.,
“4500”). In case an episode cannot be coded to any occupational group, this

3.2. Retriever
The development of OPERAG’s retriever consisted of three stages: 1) knowledge-
base identification and construction, 2) Identifying the optimal chunking
strategy, embedder, and vector database for effective coding index informa-
tion storage, and 3) developing the retrieval mechanism, including prompt-
design for effective retrieval using the instruction-aware embedder, and iden-
tifying the optimal similarity metric. In the following paragraphs, each stage
will be explained in more detail.
Since OPERAG is exclusively used for occupational classification using
ISCO-88, OPERAG’s knowledge base consists solely of ISCO-88 coding in-
dex’s relevant information. The ISCO-88 coding index contains the detailed
information for each occupational group, including the group title, definition,
included tasks, example occupations, similar occupations classified elsewhere,
and notes that guide occupational coding (Peterson et al., 1999). As experts
often use all aforementioned information during occupational coding, we en-
sured OPERAG’s knowledge base contained all this information.
As the ISCO-88 coding index is organized into discrete occupational
group entries, we leveraged this structure to apply the structure-aware seg-
mentation chunking strategy to divide the occupational group’s information
into semantically coherent units for optimal retrieval performance. Conse-
quently, this resulted in 523 chunks, each containing all the aforementioned
information of one occupational group. An example chunk is provided in
Appendix A. Subsequently, we embedded each chunk into dense numer-
17


ical vectors encoding their semantic meaning using the instruction-aware
Qwen3-Embedding-8B (Zhang et al., 2025). We selected this embedder for its
state-of-the-art retrieval performance across multiple retrieval benchmarks,
enabling efficient and accurate similarity search against the to be classified
occupational information. Finally, we stored these vectors in a FAISS vec-
tor database, which is developed to enable accurate similarity search and
retrieval over the knowledge base (Douze et al., 2025).
The retrieval mechanism contains two phases: 1) embedding of the to be
classified job description, including instructions to leverage the instruction-
aware capabilities of the employed embedder, and 2) comparing this embed-
ding against the embeddings in the knowledge base using a similarity metric.
Consequently, we first aggregate all available information of a job episode
into a query in combination with the following instruction: "Given an oc-
cupation’s Title, Task(s), Employer, and Employer’s main product, retrieve
relevant passages that match the occupation". By subsequently embedding
this using “Qwen3-Embedding-8B” employed during the indexing process, we
ensure consistency in the semantic representation across both the query and
knowledge base entries while leveraging the embedder’s instruction-aware re-
trieval capabilities for optimal retrieval performance (Zhang et al., 2025).
Once embedded, the query vector is compared to all stored occupational
group vectors in the knowledge base using the L2 (Euclidean) distance met-
ric. This approach enables us to effectively quantify semantic similarity and
identify entries that are most closely aligned with the to-be-coded occupa-
tional information. Here, we retrieve the top 30 coding index entries with the
smallest L2 distances to the query embedding, representing the entries most
relevant to the occupational information. This information is subsequently
used in the reranker module.
3.3. Reranker
As classification performance in downstream tasks generally degrades with
increasing numbers of tokens in an LLM’s prompt, it is beneficial to re-
duce the number of coding index entries included in the classification LLM’s
context (An et al., 2024). Furthermore, LLMs exhibit the aforementioned
position bias, which is the systematic tendency to weigh information differ-
ently depending on its position in the input sequence rather than purely on
its relevance to the query (Shi et al., 2024). Consequently, to limit LLM’s
context length and position bias problems, we introduce a reranking mod-
ule. Here, an auxiliary model re-evaluates the candidate occupational codes
18


retrieved in the first stage and reordering them based on relevance to the oc-
cupational information (Li et al., 2024). This ensures that the most probable
codes are prioritized, while less relevant candidates are demoted or excluded.
Here, we select a top-5 higher-quality subset from the top-30 retrieved codes,
ordering them based on relevance to the to-be-coded job description before
classification.
Purely similarity-based reranking using conventional cross-encoder rerankers
is insufficient for reliable separation of highly similar occupational codes (Dé-
jean et al., 2024). This is due to occupational groups in the same detailed
group often being semantically similar, therefore occupying near-identical
regions of the embedding space (Langezaal et al., 2023). Hence, explicit ap-
plication of coding index rules (e.g., differentiation by required skill level) is
often required for accurate separation of these codes and, subsequently, ac-
curate occupational coding (Hoffmann et al., 1995). For example, ISCO-88
codes 3433 (Bookkeepers) and 4121 (Accounting and Bookkeeping Clerks)
list highly similar tasks but are performed at different skill levels (Peter-
son et al., 1999). However, this information is often not explicitly stated in
individual job descriptions, which warrants the application of coding rules
to determine the appropriate skill level and, consequently, the correct code.
This may require considering prior occupations or other contextual informa-
tion (e.g., if a person has never performed accounting tasks, they are more
likely to be a bookkeeper than an accountant, despite a job title stating "ac-
countant"). Therefore, a reranker that takes into account such coding rules
is required for accurate reranking of occupational codes.
To allow for the application of coding rules and other relevant context in
the reranking process, we employ a reasoning-LLM (i.e., Qwen3-14B-AWQ)
as the reranker module (Yang et al., 2025). This model is prompted to select
and order the top-30 codes from the retrieval stage into the top-5 most likely
codes based on the provided occupational information.
The reasoning is
guided by rules mimicking expert-coder coding, using the following prompt
structure:
• System Role: Indicate that the model is acting as an expert-coder,
trained to apply the coding index rules consistently;
• Context: The top-30 retrieved ISCO-88 coding index entries;
• Objective: Instruction to propose the top-5 most likely ISCO-88 codes
for the given occupation in order of relevance;
19


• Classification Rules: Expert coder mimicking coding rules to guide
the reranking;
• Input Data: The occupational information to classify;
• Output Format: Standardized schema to return the top-5 predicted
ISCO-88 codes in order of relevance, comma-separated, and followed
by a concise reasoning section.
The reranking stage’s full prompt can be found in Appendix B.
To maximize reranking performance, we used the following recommended
hyperparameter settings during inference: temperature = 0.6, topp = 0.95,
minp = 0, topk = 20, and presence penalty = 1.5 (Yang et al., 2025). To
reduce stochastic variability inherent to LLMs (i.e., the same occupational
information can elicit different top-5 occupational code lists when prompted
multiple times), each case is reranked five times separately with identical
prompts and settings (Chen et al., 2024). Subsequently, each ordered list is
passed to a separate classifier in the classification stage to identify the most
relevant occupational code to the occupational information.
3.4. Classification LLM
As mentioned, occupational coding is a highly rule-intensive task that re-
quires strong reasoning capabilities to disambiguate between the often very
similar occupational groups (Kumar et al., 2025). Therefore, we employ a
reasoning-LLM to select the top-1 occupational code for the to be classified
occupational information from the top-5 codes from the reranking module.
Since occupational data are often proprietary as they contain patient
or participant data, these datasets cannot be transmitted to third-party
providers for inference.
However, most leading LLMs are either (1) pro-
prietary, requiring external transmission of data, or (2) open-source but so
computationally demanding (e.g., Deepseek R1) that they cannot be realis-
tically deployed on consumer-grade hardware available to individual expert-
coders and/or study centers (Guo et al., 2025). Consequently, we employ a
compact, reasoning-LLM, Qwen3-14B-AWQ, to classify occupations into the
most suitable occupational code using the top-5 candidates obtained from
the reranking stage (Yang et al., 2025). Qwen3-14B-AWQ has demonstrated
state-of-the-art reasoning and instruction-following performance for its pa-
rameter count (i.e., 14 billion), while remaining computationally efficient
enough to run on consumer-grade hardware. This allows accurate automatic
20


occupational classification to be performed locally, enabling accurate, effi-
cient, and data privacy-preserving AOC.
To mimic expert-coder coding practice and ensure consistent performance,
each classification prompt follows a fixed, structured template including
expert-coder rules to maximize classification performance, similar to the
prompt used during the reranking stage (Clavié et al., 2023). Additionally, to
further improve performance, the model is instructed to reason step-by-step
(i.e., Chain-of-Thought reasoning) to determine which coding index entry
best fits the occupational information (Chen et al., 2025). Finally, the out-
put must follow a standardized schema consisting of: (1) the predicted code
at the most specific permissible level and (2) a brief justification showing
decisive inclusion/exclusion criteria. This schema simplifies parsing and val-
idation, and supports explainability, which can be vital for trust in AI pre-
dictions, as often required for decision-support systems (Jung et al., 2024).
The classification stage’s full prompt can be found in Appendix C.
As five different top-5 most relevant codes from the reranking stage are
provided to the classifier five separate times, the final prediction is selected
by majority vote. This limits the stochastic variability inherent to LLMs,
while improving classification performance (Chen et al., 2024).

3.5. Performance evaluation
To enable an extensive evaluation of OPERAG’s OoD performance, we use
two widely used evaluation metrics employed in similar studies, namely: Ac-
curacy and PABAK. This enables a direct OoD comparison with humans
and other tools employing the same metrics (Wan et al., 2023). We assess
21


OPERAG’s performance on occupational classification and exposure assess-
ment accuracy. In the following sections, we explain each evaluation stage in
more detail.
3.5.1. Occupational classification performance
Using the AsiaLymph dataset, we assessed percent-agreement and inter-coder
reliability using Accuracy. For each job episode, we consider the manually
expert-coded ISCO-88 code from AsiaLymph as the gold standard.
We deem accuracy to be an important metric in the current study, as it
indicates the proportion of occupational codes that will potentially require
less coding time during the occupational coding process. We define accuracy
as the proportion of correctly predicted codes relative to the total number of
predictions (Demšar, 2006; Witten and Frank, 2002). Given a confusion ma-
trix over K categories, where the diagonal elements nii represent the number
of correctly classified episodes for each category i, and N is the total number
of episodes, accuracy is calculated as:

As occupational datasets exhibit highly imbalanced label distributions
due to differences in the prevalence of occupations performed across popula-
tions, we deem the F1-score to be an important metric in this study. The F1-
score provides a harmonic mean of precision and recall, thereby accounting for
both false positives and false negatives in the classification process (Yacouby
and Axman, 2020). This is particularly relevant when some occupational
groups are substantially more frequent than others, as the F1-score prevents
the model from achieving high performance simply by predicting the majority
classes (Pivin-Bachler and van den Broek, 2025). Additionally, this metric
enables reliable performance estimation for individual categories while ac-
counting for the distribution within the dataset. For a given occupational
category, the F1-score is defined as:

Recall represents the proportion of correctly predicted episodes relative to
all episodes that truly belong to that category. We computed the macro-

to each category regardless of its frequency in the dataset. Additionally, we
calculated the F1-score for each major group to account for the imbalance in
occupations performed in the AsiaLymph dataset.
3.5.2. Exposure assessment evaluation
To enable a comprehensive exposure assessment evaluation over a multi-
tude of different exposures, we assessed OPERAG’s exposure assessment
performance using the ALOHA+-JEM and SHOCK-JEM (Matheson et al.,
2005), (Huss et al., 2013). In addition to computing Accuracy using Eq.
(2), we also employ PABAK, a metric commonly used in occupational stud-
ies for assessing inter-coder reliability (Mak et al., 2004). This is due to the
aforementioned imbalanced label distribution in occupational datasets, which
PABAK explicitly accounts for. PABAK does not use empirical marginals to
compute chance agreement and is therefore particularly suitable for instances
where label distribution is highly imbalanced. This enables us to 1) obtain
a reliable estimation of exposure assessment performance not influenced by
label distribution, and 2) facilitate comparison against other occupational
studies. Following Wan et al. (2023), we dichotomized each exposure into
unexposed (level 0) and exposed (levels 1 and 2) before computing both met-
rics, enabling a direct comparison with the automatic coding tools previously
evaluated on the same dataset. PABAK is defined as:
PABAK = 2Po −1,
(4)
where Po is the observed proportion of agreement between the dichotomized
exposure assignments based on the gold-standard and the OPERAG-predicted
codes (Mak et al., 2004).
The ALOHA+-JEM has originally been developed for a study assessing
the association of occupational exposure and symptoms of chronic bronchi-
tis and pulmonary ventilatory defects in a general population-based study
of five areas in Spain (Matheson et al., 2005). Subjects completed a respi-
ratory questionnaire on symptoms and occupation and underwent baseline
spirometry. Based on expert knowledge, the JEM was developed ad hoc by
23


two occupational experts. It contains a job axis and an exposure estimate
for 12 generic industrial exposures, namely: biological dust, mineral dust,
gas fumes, VGDF (i.e., vapors, gases, dust, and fumes), all pesticides, herbi-
cides, insecticides, fungicides, aromatic solvents, chlorinated solvents, other
types of solvents, and metals. Exposure estimates for each aforementioned
exposure can be obtained for all ISCO-88 codes. Here, three levels of expo-
sure can be assigned, namely 0: no exposure, 1: low exposure, and 2: high
exposure. For each gold standard manually coded and OPERAG-predicted
ISCO-88 code, we applied the ALOHA+-JEM to obtain a level of expo-
sure for each aforementioned exposure, and subsequently calculated percent-
agreement and PABAK for each exposure separately. In line with Wan et al.
(2023), codes without an exposure estimate in the JEM (i.e., codes only
assignable at an aggregated level, such as 6000) were assigned exposure level
0.
The SHOCK-JEM was developed to estimate the occupational risk of
electric shocks using routinely collected national accident statistics from five
European countries (Germany, UK, Austria, Switzerland, and the Nether-
lands) (Huss et al., 2013). Electrical injury cases were drawn from national
registries, and denominators (i.e., workers per occupation) were obtained
from EUROSTAT, enabling calculation of annual electrical injury rates per
10,000 workers by occupation and country, which were then pooled with a
random-effects model to account for heterogeneity across countries. The re-
sulting SHOCK-JEM provides an exposure estimate for each 3rd level ISCO-
88, with 0 being low, 1 being medium, and 2 being high chance of experi-
encing electric shocks. We applied the SHOCK-JEM to the gold standard
3-digit manually, and OPERAG predicted ISCO-88 codes to obtain the ex-
posure assessment for all job episodes. Here, we computed overall accuracy
and PABAK. Similar to the ALOHA+-JEM, we dichotomized the exposure
estimate into low (0) versus medium or high (1–2) chance.
To account for the chance level of a non-exposure assignment being sub-
stantially higher, given the many occupational codes in the JEMs resulting in
no exposure, we evaluated two groups: 1) all individuals and 2) exposed indi-
viduals. For the first group, we calculated the aforementioned metrics using
all individuals in the AsiaLymph dataset. For the second group, we only in-
cluded job episodes in the evaluation that were deemed exposed according to
the gold-standard expert codes. For each exposure, we considered an episode
exposed if its gold-standard exposure level for that exposure exceeded 0. Fur-
thermore, assigning an exposed individual a not-exposed status often has the
24


largest effect on the following epidemiological study. Hence, evaluating this
group will give more insight into the applicability of these classification mod-
els in real-world scenarios and allow for a direct comparison with OPERAS’
ML-based classification models. Here, we compute accuracy and PABAK for
both groups.

4.1. Classification performance evaluation
OPERAG’s overall system achieved state-of-the-art OoD automatic classifi-

5. Discussion
OPERAG achieved state-of-the-art out-of-distribution performance in both
occupational classification and exposure assessment, substantially outper-
forming other automatic coding tools while approaching human-level accu-
racy on the same dataset. Here, occupational classification accuracy on the
4th coding level was 42.6%, approaching the 51—56% human-coder range
reported for the same database. Similarly, exposure assessment performance
was state-of-the-art. For the ALOHA+-JEM, the evaluation for all individ-
uals resulted in a median PABAK of 0.87 (range: 0.80–0.97), and for ex-
posed individuals, the median PABAK was 0.80 (range: 0.45–0.95). For the
SHOCK-JEM, the PABAK was 0.84 for all individuals and 0.67 for exposed-
only individuals.
These results show OPERAG’s framework to be highly
effective for accurate, universal AOC.
5.1. Comparison with current coding tools
Differences in classification performance between OPERAG and the exist-
ing coding tools AUTONOC, CASCOT, and LabourR may be attributed to
the different classification and training frameworks applied. AUTONOC and
LabourR were developed using supervised ML techniques trained on other
datasets than AsiaLymph, which also utilize classification systems other than
ISCO-88. Here, AUTONOC employs a hierarchical ensemble encoder trained
on the Canadian National Occupational Classification (NOC) (Garcia et al.,
2021), while LabourR combines keyword lookup with k-nearest neighbours
to classify text into the European Skills, Competences, Qualifications, and
Occupations (ESCO) system (Kouretsis et al., 2020). Consequently, since
AsiaLymph likely exhibits different textual features than the databases used
30


during training, these models likely overfitted to the distributions and linguis-
tic properties of their training data, resulting in the lower OoD performance
on AsiaLymph. Contrastingly, CASCOT relies on a text-matching algorithm
to map inputs to ISCO-08 and the UK SOC systems, which does not require
supervised learning for model development, and is therefore not dependent
on training data (Jones and Elias, 2004). This highlights the importance
of approaches that do not require expert-labeled databases to achieve ro-
bust OoD coding performance, as they are unable to overfit on the textual
properties of specific occupational data used during training.
Despite both OPERAG and CASCOT being classification frameworks
that do not require expert-labeled databases, OPERAG substantially out-
performs CASCOT. This gap is likely partly explained by the limitations
of CASCOT’s approach, which relies on text-matching algorithms that typ-
ically compare input text only to predefined patterns or keywords.
Such
methods do not account for context, miss subtle semantic differences, and
struggle to handle incomplete or ambiguous information, which are frequent
issues when dealing with short, variable, and often ambiguous occupational
data (Hu et al., 2019). In contrast, OPERAG leverages LLMs, which pos-
sess a deep, generalizable understanding of language. This enables OPERAG
to interpret highly variable occupation descriptions as the same occupation,
contributing to its higher performance. This was also observed in another
RAG-based ISCO-08 classification tool, where job vacancies were classified
into ISCO-08, resulting in 58% accuracy when tested on 36,000 job adver-
tisements (Ge et al., 2025). Although this represents a different classification
problem due to differences in input length and available context, with job
advertisements containing a greater amount of (standardized) terminology
than job descriptions, vacancies that should belong to the same occupations
may still exhibit different linguistic properties. Consequently, this demon-
strates LLMs’ ability to handle variable linguistic properties and terminology
between occupational texts.
In addition to the difference in classification framework, the performance
gap between OPERAG and the other three systems may also be explained by
all other systems’ using only job titles as input. This approach neglects the
discriminative information contained in tasks, employers, or products, which
are features that expert coders rely on during classification. Contrastingly,
OPERAG integrates all AsiaLymph’s free-text fields into the classification
pipeline, enabling this discriminative information to be used during the clas-
sification process. This, in combination with the LLM’s ability to handle the
31


variable occupational data, likely resulted in the near-human OoD classifica-
tion performance.
5.2. Challenge
Although OPERAG achieved state-of-the-art performance, it did not perform
within AsiaLymph’s reported human-coder range (i.e., 51–56%) (Ge, 2021).
Several database-related factors may explain this gap, namely 1) expert-coder
biases, 2) geographical and study-specific assumptions, and 3) AsiaLymph’s
applied data transformations.
Occupational coding is inherently variable, as no absolute “gold standard”
exists beyond the coding index examples. Consequently, expert coders’ cod-
ing habits may differ depending on their training, prior experience, and study
context. In AsiaLymph’s coding procedure, coders had no prior experience
and were trained with feedback (Ge, 2021). While this procedure intends to
harmonize coding decisions and increase inter-coder reliability, it may also
have introduced systematic biases that reflect learned heuristics.
Contextual elements such as geography and study population may influ-
ence coding outcomes (Morris, 1990). For example, in a cohort where most
participants are agricultural workers, a job entry labeled as “farmer” may
be coded as “agricultural farmer” because this is considered the most likely
interpretation. Similarly, although coding to the most detailed level is typ-
ically preferred to enhance data quality, occupational epidemiology studies
often prioritize pragmatic, more efficient coding choices when coding to a
more detailed coding level does not affect study outcomes. For example, in
studies assessing risk of electrical shock using the SHOCK-JEM, where only
a 3rd-level ISCO-88 code is required, expert coders may assign episodes more
quickly to the 3rd level, even though the occupational information might en-
able coding to the 4th coding level (Huss et al., 2013). While not specified for
AsiaLymph, its study population stemming solely from Eastern Asia, with its
sole application for studying lymphoma, may have introduced geographical
and study-specific assumptions in the occupational coding process.
In addition to variability arising from expert-coder practices and context-dependent
considerations, another important source of variability may stem from the
data transformations applied to the AsiaLymph dataset (Ge, 2021). The
occupational information was originally collected in Chinese and coded into
the national SOCS-ROC system before being translated into English and
cross-walked to ISCO-88. During this process, linguistic nuances and cultural
32


coding logic may have been lost during translation, resulting in potential se-
mantic misalignments between the English job descriptions and their final
ISCO-88 assignments. Furthermore, although SOCS-ROC and ISCO-88 are
structurally similar, one-to-many crosswalks may introduce ambiguity or a
not fully-directly correlating occupational code, as it might not be present in
the ISCO-88 classification. Such textual and coding discrepancies may limit
OPERAG’s ability to achieve human-level performance, as the framework is
applied only to the English translations and ISCO-88 codes.
Although intended to improve inter-coder reliability among expert coders,
expert-coder biases, as well as context and study-dependent practices, per-
forming automatic coding evaluations on databases containing these system-
atic biases may hamper the accurate reflection of the automatic coding tool’s
real-world performance.
Consequently, OPERAG’s inter-coder reliability
and alignment with the AsiaLymph codes may be improved by formaliz-
ing and applying AsiaLymph’s context-specific and study-dependent rules in
OPERAG’s framework.
5.3. Performance evaluation
OPERAG’s classification performance varied substantially across major oc-
cupational groups, with “Legislators, Senior Officials, and Managers” achiev-
ing the lowest accuracy at 13.6%. This performance is largely attributable
to the majority of AsiaLymph’s episodes in this group being coded as “Direc-
tors and Chief Executives” (i.e., 1210). In practice, these episodes were often
classified into unit groups belonging to the sub-major group “General Man-
agers” (i.e., 1300). Here, the key difference between this sub-major group
and “Corporate Managers” (i.e., 1200) lies in the size of the managed com-
pany, which is rarely specified in AsiaLymph’s job episodes. Consequently,
accurate classification into either group was often impossible without relying
on the aforementioned context-dependent assumptions or rules. For exam-
ple, AsiaLymph’s expert coders may have been trained to code episodes with
titles such as “manager” or “boss” into “Directors and Chief Executives” (i.e.,
1210) unless other information clearly indicated an alternative code. More
likely, however, expert coders used external sources (e.g., company websites)
when an employer name was provided, enabling them to infer company size
and assign the appropriate code. Without such contextual information, the
LLM frequently defaulted to codes corresponding to the generally more com-
mon smaller companies, thereby classifying them into unit groups under
sub-major group 13.
Moreover, as many of these job episodes contained
33


industry-specific descriptors (e.g., “factory chief”), the OPERAG’s retriever
often retrieved industry-specific minor groups from sub-major group 13 (e.g.,
1312 – General Managers in Manufacturing) due to their semantic similar-
ity, which have no equivalent in sub-major group 12. Consequently, these or
similar entries were most prominent in such episode’s top-30 retrieval results
and top-5 reranked candidates, making it highly unlikely for "1210" to be
selected due to LLM’s position bias. A similar effect was observed in the
major group “Skilled agricultural and fishery workers” (i.e., "6000"), where
the most prevalent category, “Market gardeners and crop growers” (i.e., 6110,
accounting for 7.1% of AsiaLymph episodes), achieved only 3.9% accuracy.
Here, many episodes were instead coded into the more detailed occupational
group 6111, reflecting the model’s tendency to assign to more detailed cate-
gories even in cases where information does not allow for coding to this level
of detail.
Apart from the small Armed Forces group (2.1% of episodes), the ma-
jor group "Professionals" obtained the highest classification accuracy. The
comparatively strong performance for this group may be explained due to
their unit groups being more distinctive, with related occupations often hav-
ing clearer lexical cues, reducing ambiguity during retrieval and classifica-
tion (Peterson et al., 1999). Misclassifications that did occur in this major
group typically arose when the model deemed the occupational episode to
lack sufficient detail to support assignment to less aggregated coding levels.
These findings highlight the need to incorporate additional expert-derived
contextual rules into AOC frameworks to improve classification performance.
At each coding level, OPERAG’s macro F1 scores were comparable to, or
marginally lower than, their respective accuracy. In contrast to supervised
learning models, this shows that OPERAG does not exhibit disproportion-
ate prediction of certain classes based on their prevalence in a study pop-
ulation (Yacouby and Axman, 2020). However, the overall macro F1 score
(0.39) was still lower than the overall accuracy (42.6%) on the 4th coding
level. This can be attributed to 55 rare ISCO-88 codes achieving F1 scores
of zero. As the macro F1 calculates the harmonic mean across all classes and
treats each occupational code with equal weight regardless of its frequency,
these zero-scoring codes substantially reduced the F1-score. Contrastingly,
within individual major groups, the macro F1 scores often equaled or even
exceeded the corresponding accuracy.
This occurred in groups where the
most prevalent unit group (i.e., the 4th coding level) within a major group
(i.e., the first coding level) exhibited poor classification performance. For
34


example, in major group "Skilled agricultural and fishery workers" (6), code
6110 accounted for 55.0% of all episodes but achieved only 3.9% accuracy. As
accuracy is sample-weighted, this majority class heavily influenced the over-
all major group accuracy, whereas the macro F1 score, being unweighted by
sample size, more accurately reflected the typical performance across all oc-
cupational codes within the group. These findings demonstrate OPERAG’s
independence from population-specific occupational distributions, in contrast
to conventional supervised machine learning approaches, thereby reinforcing
its potential as a universally applicable AOC framework.
OPERAG’s exposure assessment performance was substantially higher
than its classification performance. This is likely due to the high proportion
of occupational codes resulting in “no exposure” within the ALOHA+ and
SHOCK-JEMs. Consequently, in case of discordant occupational codes, the
resulting exposure assignments may still frequently be the same. Neverthe-
less, exposure assessment accuracy decreased when analyses were restricted
to exposed individuals, for some exposures by as much as 18 percentage
points. This highlights an important consideration for real-world applica-
tions, as misclassifying exposed individuals may have a large impact on sub-
sequent epidemiological analyses and can severely impact health outcomes.
However, OPERAG’s framework can be adapted to study-specific contexts
through potential fine-tuning or adaptation of the LLM’s classification rules.
For example, additional context-based rules or LLM-based verification steps
could be incorporated to double-check occupations that have a high likeli-
hood of exposure. Such adaptations further ensure the reliable application
of OPERAG’s framework in large-scale epidemiological studies.
5.4. Limitations
While the usage of LLMs enabled the generalizable interpretation of job
descriptions and AOC without the need for expert-labeled databases, their
highly parameterized architecture precludes full mechanistic explainability
due to their reliance on highly nonlinear and distributed computations across
millions to billions of parameters (Zhao et al., 2024a).
Consequently, it
is practically infeasible to attribute a specific output to a clearly defined,
human-interpretable rule or input feature based on the LLMs’ internal mech-
anisms alone. Contrastingly, glass-box AI models (e.g., symbolic AI) inher-
ently enable mechanistic explainability, as they allow for direct traceability
between inputs and outputs through explicit, rule-based logic or structured
35


knowledge representations that can be inspected and verified by human ex-
perts.
Although the currently employed LLMs provide textual reasoning
traces, these are generated post-hoc by the model itself and may not reflect
the actual internal decision-making process, leading to potential fabricated or
hallucinated justifications for the model’s predictions. As such traces could
contain plausible-sounding but incorrect reasoning steps, the expert-coder
could be misled into choosing an incorrect occupational coding. Especially
for less experienced expert-coders, this could lead to misclassifications of
occupations and incorrect or biased subsequent exposure assessment. Subse-
quently, this could lead to severe negative health outcomes or less impactful
interventions that promote workers’ health. Consequently, it is crucial to
take this into account if reasoning traces are shown to experts to ensure
accurate occupational coding.
OPERAG’s retriever performance is largely determined by the segmen-
tation and embedding of the coding index entries. Although we currently
employ structure-aware chunking, which typically yields strong retrieval per-
formance, alternative chunking strategies could further enhance retrieval ef-
ficiency in practice (Stäbler et al., 2025). For example, we currently include
excluded occupations within the chunks, which may introduce semantically
confounding information, as these entries frequently contain terminology that
has a semantic overlap with closely related occupational groups. Because em-
bedding models are unlikely to distinguish between “included” and “excluded”
information, retrieval performance may degrade due to such overlaps. Simi-
larly, while our current embedder was selected due to its state-of-the-art per-
formance across general retrieval benchmarks, these benchmarks do not nec-
essarily reflect the challenges of short-text occupational classification (Kenter
and De Rijke, 2015). In settings where input texts are particularly short and
ambiguous, embedders trained on corpora that more closely match the lin-
guistic characteristics of occupational data could outperform the currently
used general-purpose embedder. Consequently, systematically evaluating al-
ternative chunking strategies and embedders across occupational datasets
may improve OPERAG’s retrieval performance.
As cross-encoders are typically optimized for broader retrieval tasks and
may not adequately disambiguate between closely related occupational codes,
we adopted a reasoning-LLM as reranker (Déjean et al., 2024). The reasoning-based
reranker produced improvements, increasing top-5 retrieval accuracy and
thereby improving classification performance.
However, the improvement
was limited (4.3 percentage points), partly because in 12.2% of cases the
36


gold standard code was absent from the initial top-30 retrieval results. This
absence may reflect limitations in semantic similarity search when occupa-
tional information lacked semantic overlap with index entries. For example,
the ISCO-88 group, “Administrative Associate Professionals not classified
elsewhere”, comprising 4.9% of AsiaLymph’s job episodes, was often not re-
trieved by the RAG-components. This is likely due to the corresponding
occupational information often being described more generally rather than
being semantically similar (e.g., "Director"). This resulted in the occupa-
tional information likely being embedded further away from the coding index
entry in the embedding space, disabling effective retrieval. Consequently, al-
ternative embedding strategies, or text augmentation strategies using (e.g.,
applying thesauruses to find synonyms), could be employed to improve re-
trieval performance.
We employed Qwen3-14B-AWQ as the classifier component, as it pro-
vides strong reasoning capabilities relative to its parameter size while remain-
ing suitable for local deployment on consumer-grade hardware (Yang et al.,
2025). This design choice is especially important given that occupational
data are often proprietary, frequently precluding inference on third-party in-
frastructure. Regardless, several limitations remain. First, reasoning LLMs
with a larger number of parameters could potentially yield further perfor-
mance improvements due to their enhanced capacity for complex reason-
ing (Guo et al., 2025). Second, the model used in this study is a quantized
version of the original model, meaning that its weights were compressed
from full precision (i.e., 16-bit BF16) into lower precision formats (i.e., 4-bit)
to reduce memory requirements and accelerate inference (Egashira et al.,
2024). While quantization is essential to enable efficient local deployment
on consumer-grade hardware, it often degrades performance, especially for
smaller models (Marchisio et al., 2024).
However, quantized model ver-
sions often still outperform other models utilizing all their parameters, while
both employ the same amount. Third, alternative model architectures with
pretraining corpora more closely aligned to occupational texts, or models
fine-tuned specifically for this domain, may outperform the general-purpose
LLM employed here. Consequently, although our framework currently pri-
oritizes generalizability, adaptability to different classification systems, and
local deployability, an evaluation of alternative architectures and model sizes,
particularly those trained or adapted on representative and domain-specific
occupational datasets, may further improve OoD automatic occupational
classification performance.
37


Currently, OPERAG was evaluated solely using the AsiaLymph dataset
using only the ISCO-88 classification. Although the framework was explic-
itly designed to be independent of expert-labeled data while being capable
of adapting to diverse classification systems, its current validation does not
guarantee similar performance across other datasets or occupational classifi-
cations. As occupational data can differ substantially in linguistic character-
istics and apply other classification systems with highly differing taxonomies,
model performance likely varies. Consequently, to enable a more compre-
hensive understanding of OPERAG framework’s generalizability, robustness,
and sensitivity to dataset- or classification-specific characteristics, it should
be evaluated using multiple occupational datasets coded into different occu-
pational classification systems.
5.5. Future Work
OPERAG’s framework can be extended to improve its classification perfor-
mance in both database-dependent and independent settings, namely by: 1)
allowing for confidence scores to be obtained for each prediction, 2) integrat-
ing active learning, and 3) using current LLMs’ agentic features to enable
automatic retrieval of external web-based information based on the user’s
query.
In most (semi-)AOC tools, confidence scores are provided alongside each
prediction (Russ et al., 2016; Langezaal et al., 2023; Jones and Elias, 2004).
These scores estimate the likelihood that a given code is correct. When there
is strong calibration between the confidence score and the actual probabil-
ity of correctness (e.g., a prediction with a 95% confidence score is correct
in 95% of cases), expert coders can establish thresholds above which pre-
dictions are accepted automatically. This strategy is commonly applied in
semi-automatic or decision-supported occupational coding. In such scenarios,
codes that exceed the pre-defined confidence threshold are accepted without
further review, while the remaining cases are presented to the expert coder
for manual verification and potential correction. This strategy combines the
efficiency of automatic coding with the reliability of manual coding. However,
due to the autoregressive nature of LLMs, they are inherently unable to pro-
vide reliable confidence scores for their predicted classes (Geng et al., 2023).
Consequently, without the use of a calibration set, two suboptimal alterna-
tives remain: (1) approximating confidence based on token-level logit distri-
butions, or (2) prompting the model to self-report its confidence. Both ap-
proaches have been shown to produce poorly calibrated estimates when com-
38


pared to the confidence scores generated by conventional ML classifiers. To
address this limitation, calibration mechanisms can be introduced in which
model outputs are calibrated using a subset of expert-annotated occupational
codes and confidence scores, thereby establishing reliable task-specific con-
fidence thresholds. Regardless, because OPERAG was designed as a frame-
work that does not rely on expert-labeled databases, the generation and use
of such a calibration set was not feasible within the scope of this study. In
real-world deployments, however, expert coders could annotate a limited ini-
tial subset of job descriptions with associated confidence scores, enabling the
creation of a calibration set. This would allow OPERAG to generate reliable
confidence estimates, thereby supporting its integration into semi-automatic
coding workflows. Such an extension would enhance both the efficiency and
reliability of occupational coding in large-scale epidemiological studies and
related applications.
While the current framework enables accurate AOC without requiring
expert-labeled databases, its performance has not yet reached human-level
accuracy. This limitation can partly be attributed to the framework’s inabil-
ity to adapt to expert-coded occupations, which implicitly reflect the afore-
mentioned expert-coder bias, as well as context and study-dependent rules
required to achieve human performance. Extending OPERAG with mech-
anisms that iteratively improve expert-alignment based on feedback could
therefore enable more stable and reliable occupational classification (Lu et al.,
2023). Within the current framework, such adaptation could be achieved ei-
ther through periodic fine-tuning of the classification component or through
incremental updates to the retrieval component with corrected entries. Al-
though fine-tuning reasoning models is comparatively resource-intensive, as
it requires the construction of a reasoning database containing a high num-
ber of complete reasoning traces combined with correct outcomes, this has
been shown to achieve state-of-the-art classification performance (Guo et al.,
2025). Potentially, certain aspects of the reasoning-trace generation process
can be partially automated to reduce the manual burden of manually con-
structing these (Whitehouse et al., 2023). Alternatively, a lightweight adap-
tation mechanism could be employed, in which a supplementary retrieval
system identifies previously coded episodes that are semantically similar to
the input and integrates these, together with the corrected code, into the
prompt as context. Such a mechanism may further enhance the robustness
and stability of AOC.
Currently, expert coders often employ context-specific information be-
39


yond the provided raw free-text occupational information to support their
coding decisions. For example, while ISCO-88 groups "Directors and Chief
Executives" and "General Managers" share highly similar tasks, the main
differentiating aspect is the size of the company that the person manages (Pe-
terson et al., 1999). When provided with a company name, expert coders may
consult external sources (e.g., internet searches) to determine the company’s
industry, primary products, or organizational size, thereby enabling more
definitive classification in either group. In contrast, the present OPERAG
framework may treat the company name as plain contextual input if this
company’s information is not present in the LLM’s pre-training corpus. If no
additional information is supplied within the occupational description itself,
the model lacks access to further contextual knowledge, limiting its ability to
fully mimic this expert-coder decision process and thereby constraining per-
formance relative to human coders. However, recent advances in LLMs have
introduced agentic capabilities, such as web-browsing, which allow models to
dynamically query external resources when additional information is required
based on the input (Plaat et al., 2025). By integrating such functionality,
the framework could autonomously retrieve and leverage relevant contextual
occupational data, further improving its classification performance.
5.6. Conclusions
We show that OPERAG is a state-of-the-art framework for database and
classification-independent occupational coding, outperforming current cod-
ing tools on OoD occupational classification performance while approach-
ing human performance. OPERAG’s classification performance may be im-
proved by refining retrieval strategies, adapting to study and context-specific
coding rules, and extending the framework with confidence calibration, ac-
tive learning, and agentic capabilities for external information retrieval. Ad-
ditionally, OPERAG’s evaluation should be extended to include additional
occupational datasets and classification systems, thereby improving insight
into the influence of embedder choice, model architecture, and coding-system
variability on classification performance. Consequently, the OPERAG frame-
work may unlock current and future occupational data potential in finding
crucial exposure response associations, leading to new interventions that im-
prove workers’ health.
