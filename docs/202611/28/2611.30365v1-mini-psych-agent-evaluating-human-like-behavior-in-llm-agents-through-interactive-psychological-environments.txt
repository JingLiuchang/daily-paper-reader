ABSTRACT
A Large Language Model (LLM), its harness, and the test-time compute budget jointly determine
its behavior. Consequently, the same underlying model, embedded in different agentic workflows
or allocated different amounts of test-time compute, can exhibit markedly different performance.
Psychologists face an analogous challenge when evaluating human performance: the same person
may behave differently under different incentives and levels of deliberation. Psychological tasks
therefore provide an ideal testbed for studying such behavioral variation. However, when adapted to
LLMs, these tasks are typically text-transcribed, replaying fixed, language-rendered experiments as
static prompts. This format is ill-suited to agent evaluation, where actions can alter future observations
and task states. Conversely, although dynamic environments are prevalent in the evaluation of LLM
agents, they rarely ask whether the resulting behavior is human-like. Here, we bridge this gap by
transforming psychological tasks into dynamic environments in which agents can interact and by
using a learned human-policy model to evaluate how compatible the resulting behavioral trajectories
are with human behavior. Across these environments, we find that this compatibility varies with an
LLM’s agentic configuration and the psychological task, and that memory raises it mainly by making
choices more predictable rather than more human-like. These findings motivate a more holistic
evaluation of LLM agents in psychology-inspired environments, accounting for both the backbone
model and its agentic scaffold.
1
Introduction
Psychological tasks are originally designed to isolate and test specific aspects of human cognition. To understand
aspects of machine cognition, it is therefore natural for psychologists to adapt their carefully designed tasks to examine
the behavior of LLMs. However, a common approach to evaluating LLMs on psychological tasks is to replay, in natural
language, the experimental experiences originally encountered by human participants [Binz and Schulz, 2023, Binz
et al., 2025, 2026, Bai et al., 2025, Frank and Goodman, 2026, Liu et al., 2025, Zhu and Griffiths, 2024]. For example,
the Centaur model, which is a finetuned Llama-3.1-70B model using psychological data, transcribes each experiment
into a text sequence containing the task instructions, stimuli, and trial-by-trial interaction history [Binz et al., 2025]. At

Verbatim replay provides a controlled and statistically principled measure of human-likeness through next-response
prediction, following a long tradition of cognitive modeling [Daw, 2011, Wilson and Collins, 2019]. But it does not
directly assess closed-loop behavior, in which an agent’s actions determine its subsequent observations and task states.
As a result, replaying a human participant’s experience evaluates model behavior only under the distribution of task
states and histories generated by the participant [Binz et al., 2025, Xie and Zhu, 2025]. This can conceal compounding
errors: at every trial, the model is always returned to the participant’s recorded history and therefore never has to recover
from the consequences of its own earlier non-human-like decisions.
But an autonomous agent may take an unusual action and enter a state that few or no human participants encountered.
Replay provides no evidence about its behavior in such states. The supplied human history may also be unlikely under
the model’s own policy. For example, the replay transcript may state that “you previously chose option A,” even though
the model would almost certainly have chosen option B. The model is thus asked to predict a response conditional on a
history that it would rarely generate through its own decisions.
Evaluating autonomous agents therefore also requires assessing behavior under the histories generated by their own
actions. To support this evaluation, we developed MindGym, a suite of interactive environments comprising 25 distinct
psychological tasks that probe executive functions (e.g., the N-back task), social cognition (e.g., the ultimatum game),
risky choice (e.g., the Balloon Analogue Risk Task), and reward learning (e.g., bandit tasks). Unlike approaches that
only transcribe participants’ experiences into text, we transform these tasks into interactive environments in which both
humans and LLM agents can act through a defined set of permissible actions. As a result, these environments allow
entirely new action sequences to be generated and evaluated under instructions similar to those given to the original
human participants.
MindGym thus offers a unique opportunity to examine the behavior of LLM agents and evaluate their workflow designs
through psychology-inspired environments. We created five mini-psych-agent variants by varying how agents
maintain and use an external memory file (i.e., MEMORY.md). These variants range from a stateless baseline without
persistent memory to more sophisticated approaches to context management, including retrieval-augmented generation
(RAG) and surprisal-based episodic memory. External memory provides a practical mechanism for maintaining

persistent agent state across model calls, allowing information from earlier interactions to be stored, updated, and
retrieved to guide subsequent behavior.
Evaluating the human-likeness of mini-psych-agent variants in MindGym introduces an additional challenge: how to
assess human-likeness when few or no matching human behavioral sequences are available. Our agents can generate
novel trajectories that no human participant in the dataset has experienced. We address this challenge by training
recurrent neural networks (RNNs) on human behavioral sequences, with one RNN for each of the 25 psychological
tasks. Given sufficient human data, these RNNs can generalize to unseen histories within each task and serve as learned
evaluators of agent behavior. We then freeze the RNNs and assess how compatible agent behavior is with the human
policy using the likelihood these learned human-policy models assign to agent-generated actions, conditioned on the
corresponding observations and interaction histories.
Our contributions are threefold. (i) We standardized and abstracted a range of psychological experiments into interactive
environments in which agents can act and receive feedback. (ii) We developed novel likelihood metrics for evaluating
how compatible behavioral sequences are with human policies learned by RNNs, reported alongside KL divergence and
trajectory-level features. (iii) We systematically evaluated different memory-management strategies for LLM agents
across interactive psychological environments.
2
Related Work
Evaluating human alignment of LLMs. Psychological experiments are now widely adapted to characterize LLM
behavior, often through static prompts such as vignettes, questionnaires, and choice problems [e.g., Binz and Schulz,
2023, Zhu and Griffiths, 2024, Bai et al., 2025, Liu et al., 2025]. For example, CogGym standardizes 258 commonsense
reasoning experiments and compares 50 LLMs with humans on matched trials [Ying et al., 2026]. However, fewer
studies evaluate LLMs in closed-loop interactions, where the model’s own choices determine its subsequent task
states [e.g., Binz and Schulz, 2023, Coda-Forno et al., 2024, Schubert et al., 2024, Akata et al., 2025]. These
studies often summarize behavior using a few task-specific signatures, such as regret, stay probabilities, cooperation
rates, or fitted model parameters (e.g., CogBench [Coda-Forno et al., 2024] and CogArena [Fernandez et al., 2025]).
Although informative, these summaries require bespoke analyses for each task and retain only selected aspects of
trial-level behavior. In parallel, interactive environments are well established for training and evaluating non-LLM
agents. Psychlab implements psychological experiments for both humans and deep RL agents [Leibo et al., 2018],
while NeuroGym provides neuroscience tasks for training neural networks [Molano-Mazón et al., 2022]. For LLM
agents, game-based benchmarks such as BALROG and TextArena provide interactive environments for evaluating task
performance [Paglieri et al., 2025, Guertler et al., 2025]. However, these benchmarks primarily assess game progress
or skill ratings rather than the human-likeness of the LLM behavior. A remaining challenge is therefore to combine
interactive environments with a common measure of human-likeness for trajectories generated by LLM agents.
Learned models of human behavior. Psychologists commonly develop computational models to explain cognitive
mechanisms or predict human behavior. Whether interpretable cognitive models or black-box neural networks, these
models are typically themselves the object of evaluation. For sequential tasks, a common criterion is how well they
predict each response from a participant’s preceding history [Daw, 2011, Wilson and Collins, 2019]. Flexible learned
models increasingly outperform hand-crafted theories in risky and strategic choice [Peterson et al., 2021, Zhu et al.,
2025] and sequential decision-making [Dezfouli et al., 2019, Ji-An et al., 2025], while hybrid neural-cognitive models
retain interpretable structure [Eckstein et al., 2026, Cao and Eckstein, 2025]. In sufficiently data-rich settings, neural
networks can also approach empirically estimated predictive limits [Kuperwajs et al., 2023, Yan et al., 2026], capturing
maximal explainable variances in human data. Learned neural network models have also guided cognitive theory
refinement through scientific regret minimization [Agrawal et al., 2020] and provided behavioral representations for
evaluating LLM-generated behavior [Lei et al., 2026]. In this paper, we use RNNs trained on human trajectories in
each psychological task to evaluate the human-likeness of trajectories generated by autonomous LLM agents in the
corresponding interactive environment. This extends the role of learned models from objects of evaluation to evaluators
of novel agent-generated behavior within the same task.
Memory management in LLM agents. An LLM agent’s behavior depends not only on its backbone but also on
its scaffold [Yang et al., 2024, Kapoor et al., 2024], of which memory management is a central component. CoALA
frames LLM agents as cognitive architectures with working and long-term memory [Sumers et al., 2024], while
concrete designs include RAG [Lewis et al., 2020], memory streams with retrieval and reflection [Park et al., 2023],
OS-inspired context management [Packer et al., 2023], and surprise-based event segmentation [Fountas et al., 2025].
Memory benchmarks, however, commonly emphasize task performance, including question answering over long
interaction histories and, more recently, on-policy conversational evaluation [Wu et al., 2025, Cheng et al., 2026].
Memory management also shapes decisions: in a study of multi-armed bandits, satisfactory exploration emerged only

examined how different memory-management strategies affect the human-likeness of agent-generated trajectories

3
Evaluation Framework
Interactive psychological environments. Following the agent-environment interface used by OpenAI Gym [Brockman
et al., 2016], we implement the MindGym environments using Gymnasium [Towers et al., 2024]. Each environment
supports interaction through natural-language instructions and observations, together with structured, task-specific
actions. Specifically, each environment maintains an internal state that evolves in response to the agent’s actions. As
illustrated in Figure 1, interaction under task configuration ξ proceeds as
ut ∼πLLM(· | ct), at = parseξ(ut), st+1 ∼Pξ(· | st, at), ot+1 = gξ(st+1).
(1)
where t denotes the decision step and ct is the input context constructed by the agent runtime. This context combines
the task instructions and current textual observation ot with any memory or interaction history supplied by the agent
architecture (see Equation 2 for details). Conditioned on ct, the LLM generates its response ut under policy π, which is
further parsed into an executable action at, which updates the internal environment state st through the transition kernel
Pξ. The rendering function gξ exposes participant-visible information and feedback permitted by the task protocol,
while internal scoring and diagnostic information are recorded separately. Repeated interaction produces a behavioral
trajectory shaped by the agent’s own choices and their consequences.
The present study evaluates 25 psychological tasks spanning executive control, feedback learning and adaptation,
information acquisition, social learning and strategic interaction, risk and decision making, planning and transfer,
memory retrieval and integration, and affective updating. These categories reflect the primary demands of the
implemented tasks. We draw on human behavioral datasets from Psych-201 [Binz et al., 2026] and datasets identified
through OpenCogData [Zorowitz and NIMH DSST, 2024]. We selected tasks with human behavioral data from more
than 500 participants to support reliable training and held-out evaluation of task-specific RNN models of human
behavior. For each task, we draw on published paradigms and available experimental materials to specify the available
actions, outcome dynamics, feedback schedule, and stopping conditions. The full task inventory and adaptations
required for language-based interaction are documented in Appendix A.

Between the Stateless and Cumulative extremes, we consider more selective memory strategies inspired by cog-

cent interaction records. The Retrieval agent instead receives up to six records with the highest relevance scores,
ρt(ei) = cos(tf(ot), tf(oi)), where tf denotes word-count vectors and ties favor more recent records.
Finally, inspired by surprise-based event segmentation in EM-LLM [Fountas et al., 2025], our Surprise agent uses
elicited forecasts of public outcomes or stimuli to partition experience into events. Each closed event Ej is replaced by
a textual summary σj = SummarizeLLM(Ej). At decision step t, the agent receives the Jt completed summaries in
chronological order, followed by the current event’s uncompressed records Bt.
Learned human-policy model. To assess human-like behavior in states visited by few or no human participants, we
train a separate RNN on human sequences for each of the 25 psychological tasks. Each model is trained to approximate
the human policy conditioned on the current task context and preceding interaction history, providing a task-specific
behavioral reference:
πRNN(at | x1:t) ≈πhuman(at | x1:t),
(4)
where x1:t denotes the sequence of structured inputs available before action at is selected, including the current stimuli
or options and preceding responses and feedback. That is, x1:t is an abstraction of ct in Equation 2.
Crucially, our RNNs use symbolic encodings of task features and responses, following common practice in neural
network models of human behavior [Ji-An et al., 2025, Eckstein et al., 2026, Cao and Eckstein, 2025, Kuperwajs et al.,
2023, Yan et al., 2026]. Unlike LLM-based approaches that represent psychological tasks in natural language [Binz and
Schulz, 2023], our RNN models learn directly from these structured representations. They therefore operate on task
abstractions rather than directly processing the stimuli presented to participants, as “stimulus-computable” models do
[Frank and Goodman, 2026]. This abstraction focuses learning on task-relevant behavioral dependencies, with the aim
of supporting generalization to novel interaction histories within the same task.

We trained these RNN models through next-action prediction with teacher forcing: each human response is predicted
from the current task inputs and preceding observed human responses and feedback. Categorical decisions are trained
with cross-entropy, and continuous decisions with task-specific regression losses; tasks with multiple response types
combine the corresponding objectives. Human data are partitioned approximately 80/10/10 into training, validation,
and test sets by source-defined identity groups, with each group assigned to a single partition. Checkpoints are selected
based on validation performance and frozen for subsequent evaluation. Task-specific encodings, losses, and training
configurations are detailed in Appendix B.
Predictive validation. On held-out human trajectories, the RNNs exceeded uniform-random prediction accuracy
by 37.6 ± 11.4 percentage points (mean ± SD across tasks), with above-chance performance on all 23 tasks with
categorical decisions (Figure 2a). The RNNs also capture qualitative patterns in human behavior, including behavioral
adaptation following reversals in the reversal learning task (see Figure 2b), declining accuracy with increasing N-back
load (see Figure 2c), and increasing preference for advantageous decks in the Iowa Gambling Task (see Figure 2d).
These findings are consistent with previous RNN modeling results and support the use of learned RNNs as black-box
behavioral references for human trajectories [Kuperwajs et al., 2023, Ji-An et al., 2025, Eckstein et al., 2026, Yan et al.,
2026]. Full predictive metrics, including results for continuous decisions, are reported in Appendix B. Appendix L
extends these behavioral checks using qualitative features from 16 tasks: the RNN curves stay within 0.06 of the human
curves in 14 tasks and within 0.09 in the other two.

under agent-generated histories, including those rarely or never observed in the human data. This evaluation relies on
the RNN’s ability to generalize within the task beyond the human trajectories on which it was trained.

KL divergence from the human policy. Because ¯S is computed from the agent’s sampled actions alone, it increases

4
Experiments
We evaluated mini-psych-agent on 25 psychological tasks across GPT-5.6 Luna [OpenAI, 2026], DeepSeek V4.1
Flash [DeepSeek-AI, 2026], and Qwen3.8 Flash [Alibaba Cloud, 2026]. Each backbone LLM was evaluated under five
memory configurations (Stateless, Cumulative, Window, Retrieval, and Surprise), with five environment seeds per task,
matched across configurations. Our analyses included 1,690 episodes across the 23 tasks with categorical decisions.
Results for the 2 tasks with continuous or mixed decisions are reported in Appendix I.
For the subset of 17 tasks with a defined optimality baseline, we measured task performance as the proportion of
decisions that exactly matched the task-specific optimal action. We assessed human-likeness using ¯S and its random-to-
human completeness, defined as

Memory effects on task performance. We evaluated how memory influences LLM agents’ task performance,
averaging task-specific optimality rates for cross-task aggregation. Across the 17 tasks, averaging the four memory
configurations yielded a performance gain of 0.075 over Stateless (CI [0.030, 0.123]), with positive gains in 14 tasks.
At the task–backbone level, individual memory configurations improved performance relative to Stateless in 149 of 204
comparisons (73.0%), reduced it in 45, and tied in 10. Qwen showed consistent gains, improving in 15 of 17 tasks, with
a mean gain of 0.084 (CI [0.048, 0.123]).

As shown in Figure 3, more specific patterns emerged across tasks with different history requirements. In experiential
risk learning, Cumulative outperformed Window in all 15 paired backbone–seed comparisons, with a mean gain of 10.9
percentage points in optimal-choice rate. In Castle two-step, its optimal-terminal rate was 1.5 percentage points lower
than Window’s on average, with positive differences in 4 of 15 comparisons. In N-back, Window outperformed Retrieval

accumulated experience in experiential risk learning and from preserving recent observations in their original order in
N-back. The best memory configuration nevertheless varied across tasks and backbone LLMs (Appendix F).
Memory effects on human-likeness. We find that including interaction history in the context supplied to the LLM
improves average alignment with the learned human policy. Across the 23 tasks, mean completeness increased from
0.664 under Stateless to 0.885 when averaged over the four other memory configurations, which provide interaction
history in different forms (∆= 0.222, CI [0.119, 0.336]). Mean log likelihood ¯S also increased by 0.168 nats per
decision (CI [0.096, 0.246]). All four memory configurations had higher task-averaged completeness than Stateless for
every backbone, with Cumulative scoring highest (Table 1). Averaged across the four configurations, completeness
gains were 0.125 for GPT, 0.284 for DeepSeek, and 0.256 for Qwen. Holm-corrected Wilcoxon tests yielded p = 0.030,
p = 0.015, and p < 0.001, respectively (Appendix D).
Task-specific memory effects were also evident as measured by ¯S. Cumulative exceeded Window by 0.234 nats in
experiential risk learning and 0.251 nats in Castle two-step. In N-back, Window exceeded Retrieval by 0.127 nats, with
positive differences in all 15 paired backbone–seed comparisons. These patterns suggest that the benefits of memory
depend on the history demands of particular tasks, although better human-policy fit need not coincide with better task
performance, as illustrated by Castle two-step.

5
Discussion
We introduced MindGym, a suite of psychology-inspired interactive environments for evaluating LLM agents. Compared
with approaches that turn human experiences in psychological tasks into static prompts, MindGym supports closed-loop
evaluation, where agents make their own decisions and influence subsequent task states. Agents can therefore be
evaluated under their own interaction histories without repeatedly returning to a human participant’s experience. This
introduces an evaluation challenge: little or no human data may be available for states rarely or never visited by human
participants. We address this by using task-specific RNNs trained on human trajectories as learned behavioral references
for evaluating agent-generated trajectories.
Using mini-psych-agent, we systematically varied how LLM agents manage their external memory files and
incorporate past interactions into the context at each decision step. Overall, retaining more past interactions in context
generally improved both task performance and compatibility with the human policy, although no single memory strategy
performed best across all tasks and backbones. For the two backbones whose action probabilities we could measure,
most of the gain in ¯S reflected more deterministic choice; KL divergence from the human policy fell modestly and was
lowest under Cumulative (Table 2). At the level of complete trajectories, memory moved history-dependent behavior
toward the human range but also made agents more likely to repeat previous decisions than humans (Appendix M;
Figures 10–12). We therefore interpret the memory gain as greater predictability and compatibility with the human
policy rather than as greater human-likeness. Cumulative outperformed Window in feedback learning and planning
tasks, while Window outperformed Retrieval in working memory tasks. These patterns suggest that memory benefits
depend on both the amount of past experience retained and the preservation of recent temporal order. Window and
Surprise nevertheless captured much of Cumulative’s benefit for compatibility while using substantially fewer tokens in
prompts for decision making.
Alignment with optimal behavior and alignment with human behavior were found to be positively related, but could
also diverge in certain tasks. This suggests that greater optimality can coincide with higher compatibility with the
human policy in some cognitive domains. Agents with high compatibility scores could nevertheless exhibit qualitatively
distinct behavioral patterns over complete trajectories. Therefore, task performance, conditional human-policy fit, and

more holistic evaluation of autonomous agents.
Limitations and future research. Our environments and agents represent early steps toward psychology-inspired
evaluation of LLM agents. We also note several limitations. First, the human-policy RNNs were validated on human
histories, whereas agents can reach states that people rarely visit. Therefore, likelihood scores from these RNNs
are extrapolations, and a more direct test would require human responses collected from agent-generated histories
(Appendix L). Second, the environments are still text-based: they preserve the choice structure of each task but not
perceptual interference or response timing, as in Stroop, and no human data were collected on the text interface, so our
claims concern choice behavior. Third, DKL is potentially a better metric, although it is only available for open-weight
models and has to be approximated using model logits. Additional psychological tasks could be implemented as
interactive environments within MindGym to examine a broader range of cognitive processes. More sophisticated
memory strategies, particularly those that modify stored representations of individual trials, could be incorporated into
mini-psych-agent, alongside additional skills and tool-use capabilities. Another promising direction is to train LLM
agents to behave more like humans within psychological environments, using learned human-policy models as training
targets. We believe these efforts could help develop autonomous agents whose decisions are better aligned with human
behavior.

AI use statement
We used generative AI tools to assist with implementing and debugging code. LLM-generated trajectories are
experimental outputs of the evaluated agents and are distinguished from the human behavioral data used to train the
RNNs. The authors take responsibility for the final methods, analyses, claims, and artifacts presented in this work.
Ethics statement
This work uses previously collected human behavioral datasets and computational experiments with LLM agents. The
learned human-policy models reflect patterns in their source populations and should not be interpreted as universal
models of human cognition. The reported alignment scores are not intended for clinical assessment or individual
psychological profiling, and higher scores do not necessarily imply human-like reasoning or optimal behavior. Data
access and any redistribution remain subject to the original datasets’ licensing, access, and privacy requirements.

Illustrations for interactive psychological environments in MindGym
Each environment exposes task instructions, a textual observation, and the currently legal responses. Executing a
response updates the task state and returns the next observation, any protocol-permitted feedback, and a termination
signal. The observation contains participant-visible information: for example, previously revealed outcomes, current
offers, or a revealed portion of a payoff matrix. Hidden reward parameters, unrevealed outcomes, and evaluator-only
optimality calculations are kept separate. The number of environment steps need not equal the number of experimental
trials: information search, navigation, and multistage decisions can require several actions within one trial or round.
Tables 3–4 summarize the 25 implemented tasks. All are untimed text adaptations; visual displays are described
symbolically and response times are not modeled. Source schedules and feedback conditions are retained where
available, while interface instructions and cover stories are not uniformly reproduced. Iowa gambling, momentary mood,
and sparse-event estimation include declared reconstructions where original implementation details are unavailable.

Human-policy models and predictive validation
Each task has a separately fitted RNN model on training human trajectories. Encodings represent visible stimuli,
protocol stage, and causally available response/outcome history (Table 5). Discrete identities use indicators; numerical
features use task-specific scaling. Candidate-based choices pool masked candidate encodings before recurrent updating,
with shared utility heads scoring legal options. Previous responses are shifted: the probability of response t is computed
before that response enters history. Observation-only events can update recurrent state without contributing a response
loss. State resets at source sequence boundaries.
Categorical heads minimize binary or multiclass cross-entropy, masking illegal actions and unscored events. Retrieve-
compare uses separate two-choice and 21-category report heads; costly search uses conditional typed heads. Mood uses
mean squared error; sparse-event estimation uses squared error normalized by protocol slider range. Happiness and
self–other models combine categorical losses with a weight-10 smooth-L1 rating loss on the [0, 1] scale. Continuous
outputs are evaluated in native units. Adam-family optimization and validation-based checkpoint selection are used; the
table records selected widths, learning rates, batch sizes, maximum epoch budgets, and stopping patience. Maximum

This identity follows by expanding the trajectory KL divergence: the shared environment factors in Equation 7 cancel,
leaving the sum of action log-probability ratios.
A higher expected score can therefore reflect both a smaller divergence from the reference policy and lower action
entropy. In particular, an agent that consistently chooses modal human responses can score higher than one that
reproduces human variability. We consequently interpret the score as behavioral compatibility with a learned human
policy. Computing it requires only the agent’s sampled actions and the RNN’s probabilities, without access to the
agent’s own action probabilities. Obtaining the latter can be difficult even when token-level probabilities are available,
because multiple response strings and reasoning traces can lead to the same parsed action. Computing an action’s
probability generally requires marginalizing over these alternatives; the probability of an answer token conditioned
on a single reasoning trace need not equal the agent’s marginal action probability. Moreover, interpreting the mean
log likelihoods as human-likeness also depends on the RNN’s predictive validity under agent-generated histories.
Performance on held-out human trajectories supports the use of the RNN as a reference, but does not by itself establish
reliable extrapolation to rarely or never observed histories.
F
Exploratory statistical analysis of task performance
We analyzed the existing scalar performance endpoints of all 19 tasks with a defined improvement direction. The
comparison family comprised 228 contrasts: four memory configurations versus Stateless for each of three backbones
and 19 tasks. Within each task and backbone, we retained seeds with complete, finite outcomes under all five
configurations and required at least three matched seeds, yielding 224 estimable contrasts. Each episode contributed
one summary outcome; sequential decisions were not treated as independent replicates. Positive differences indicate
improved performance, with signs reversed for lower-is-better endpoints.
We report mean paired differences, marginal unadjusted 95% paired-t confidence intervals, and both two-sided paired-t
and exact sign-flip tests of the mean difference. Holm adjustment was applied separately to each test procedure
across the same 228 planned contrasts. Undefined tests were flagged and assigned p = 1 solely for adjustment
bookkeeping. Paired-t inference assumes independent, approximately normal seed differences; sign-flip inference
requires the corresponding null symmetry or within-pair exchangeability. With five pairs, the usual two-sided exact test
cannot yield p < 0.0625. Small sample sizes limit assessment of these assumptions. The confidence intervals are not
multiplicity-adjusted.
These analyses were specified after inspection of descriptive outcomes and are exploratory. Adjustment across the full
comparison family does not remove prior task or reporting selection. Matching protocol seeds does not imply identical
realized trajectories in adaptive or action-dependent tasks. Uncertainty is conditional on the evaluated tasks, backbones,
protocol, frozen endpoints, and retained complete runs; neither test is treated as independent confirmation or selected
according to its significance.
G
The best memory configuration depends on the task
No single memory mechanism is the winner for every task and backbone (Figure 5). Although Cumulative memory
performs best on average, the configuration that most closely matches the learned human policy in raw likelihood is
task- and backbone-dependent; many near-ties further caution against interpreting small mean differences as reliable
superiority.

Trajectory-level behavior and prompt budget
Behavioral effects in the agents’ own episodes. Figures 10–12 repeat the analysis of Figure 9 for the LLM agents.
For each backbone and memory configuration we computed the same behavioral effect on the agent’s own episodes,
pooling the five seeds, and plotted it against held-out participants. This comparison does not involve the human-policy
RNNs. All 16 effects use the definitions applied to the participants. Memory moves several history-dependent effects
toward the participants: the dependence of repeating on the previous reward in the reward bandit (DeepSeek with
Window or Retrieval, Qwen with Cumulative), the decline of observing in observe-or-bet (Qwen with Cumulative),
and acceptance in the ultimatum game (Qwen with Window). It also produces perseveration that participants do not
show: after a rewarded round of Castle two-step, GPT (Cumulative, Window, Surprise) and DeepSeek (all four memory
configurations) repeat their first-stage choice even when the goal has been reversed (0.81–0.99; participants 0.25). In
five tasks every configuration of every backbone is far from the participants: responses do not depend on the objective
probability in weather classification, there is no learning in self/other learning, transfer choices stay near 0.5 in range
learning, and agents cash out after a few pumps in BART and turn far fewer cards in the Columbia card task. In BART
and information sampling the agents reach only the first bins of the participants’ range.
Trajectory-level features. We computed 44 pre-specified behavioral features in the nine branching tasks of Appendix H
and asked whether each agent’s value lies inside the central 95% interval of the RNN’s own rollouts (Table 16). Memory
moves features that require using past outcomes into the human range, but it also makes agents more likely to repeat
previous decisions than humans, most strongly under Cumulative: the share of features inside the interval falls from
53% under Stateless to 34–47% with memory (Table 17). In the risk tasks every configuration is far from human
behavior.
Prompt budget and seeds. Memory configurations differ in prompt tokens by design (Table 7). Table 18 separates
mechanism from budget. Window and Retrieval use the same budget: their difference in ¯S is small (0.015–0.044; the
interval excludes zero only for GPT, narrowly), but Window has the lower divergence for both backbones with action
probabilities. Cumulative uses five to nine times the tokens of Window for a gain in ¯S whose interval includes zero for
every backbone. Seed-to-seed variation accounts for about 2% of the variance of the pooled memory effect, which
has the same sign in every seed; five seeds do not suffice to rank individual configurations within a task. Figure 13
relates prompt length to the outcomes within task and backbone: a tenfold increase in prompt tokens per decision goes
with higher task performance (0.40 within-task standard deviations, CI [0.17, 0.69]), higher ¯S (0.11, CI [0.06, 0.17])
and lower DKL (−0.029, CI [−0.044, −0.013]). Because each configuration has its own budget, prompt length and
configuration are nearly collinear; with configuration in the regression, none of the three slopes differs from zero, so the
equal-budget contrast in Table 18 is the informative test.
