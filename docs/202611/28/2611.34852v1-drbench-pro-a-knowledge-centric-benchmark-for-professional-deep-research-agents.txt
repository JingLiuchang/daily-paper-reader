Benchmark for Professional Deep Research

Deep research agents gather information from multiple sources and synthesize it into reports on
complex, open-ended questions. Existing benchmarks assess report quality along several dimensions,
but lack a dedicated knowledge-centric evaluation of which research directions a report covers and how
deeply it explores them. We introduce DRBench Pro, a benchmark that makes structured knowledge
coverage the primary evaluation target. Our automated pipeline builds local knowledge trees from
Wikipedia and uses sampled subgraphs to generate research queries and evidence-grounded rubrics.
Each rubric corresponds to a relation in the tree, preserving the structure of the knowledge being
evaluated. Agents receive only the research query. This structure allows us to measure breadth across
major research directions and depth along related knowledge chains, with their geometric mean giving
overall coverage. The benchmark includes 144 cases across 24 domains and 44,102 rubrics. Evaluating
16 deep research agents reveals different strengths in breadth and depth, with the best-performing
agent reaching 57.04% overall coverage. Rubric judgments agree closely with human annotations, and
agent rankings remain stable across the tested metric configurations.

report therefore depends on both covering the major directions relevant to a question and following chains of
related knowledge within those directions.
Evaluating deep research is challenging because open-ended questions admit multiple valid reports, while a
single reference answer rarely captures all relevant knowledge. Factuality checks can identify unsupported
statements but cannot penalize omitted knowledge (Min et al., 2023; Wei et al., 2024; Song et al., 2024b).
Existing benchmarks therefore use expert reports, reference facts, and task-specific rubrics to define expected
content and assess factuality, comprehensiveness, citation quality, and writing (Du et al., 2026; Sharma et al.,
2026; Li et al., 2026b). Recent approaches further reduce manual effort by generating evaluation criteria from
Wikipedia, research graphs, or structured task representations (Wang et al., 2026c;d; Dong et al., 2026; Wang
et al., 2026a), providing explicit criteria for assessing report coverage.
However, existing benchmarks lack a dedicated knowledge-centric evaluation of how broadly and deeply reports
cover a structured reference space. Knowledge coverage is typically one component of broader assessments
that also consider formatting, organization, and writing style (Du et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026b). Expert
references and manually authored rubrics further limit expansion across domains, while individually specifying
or checking detailed criteria makes rubric coverage costly to scale within each task. More fundamentally, even
automatically generated criteria lose their relationships under flat aggregation, obscuring whether reports
cover more research directions or develop selected directions more deeply. Recent tree- and DAG-based
approaches preserve structure for supervision, task completion, or evidence traceability (Zhu et al., 2026;
Wang et al., 2026a; Sun et al., 2026; Gao et al., 2026). They do not, however, explicitly treat structured
knowledge coverage as their primary evaluation target. Specifically, coverage scores use topology-derived
weights that are fixed in advance for each task and do not adapt to interactions among knowledge items
covered in a report. Yet the value of a knowledge item also depends on whether it connects to related
knowledge that is already covered in the report. Complete-path checks establish whether conclusions have
supporting evidence, but do not provide a graded measure of how far coverage extends along a knowledge
chain when coverage is partial or interrupted by missing intermediate relations. These limitations leave both
the breadth of coverage across research directions and the depth of coverage along related knowledge chains
insufficiently characterized (Table 1).
To fill this gap, we introduce DRBench Pro, a knowledge-centric benchmark that treats structured knowledge
coverage as its primary evaluation target. Our automated pipeline builds a local entity–relation tree through
multi-hop expansion from a Wikipedia root entity and samples coherent subgraphs defining distinct research
scopes. Each subgraph is used to generate both a root-centered research query and a rubric set. Every
retained edge defines a rubric grounded in Wikipedia evidence, preserving its structural position in the
reference tree. This shared construction aligns task scope with evaluation units and makes each judgment
traceable to its originating relation and structural position.
We further propose topology-aware metrics that aggregate edge-level judgments bottom-up over the reference
tree. Breadth measures how comprehensively a report covers major research directions, allowing descendant
coverage to partially compensate for an omitted relation. Depth measures how far reports develop related
knowledge chains, with descendant credit conditioned on whether connecting relations are covered. It rewards
continuity while retaining discounted credit beyond missing intermediate relations, providing graded scores for
incomplete chains. Subtrees are ranked by their realized coverage in each report to emphasize well-developed
chains. Their geometric mean yields overall coverage. Evaluating 16 deep research agents reveals substantially
interleaved breadth and depth rankings, exposing coverage differences that a single aggregate score can conceal
2


(Figure 1). The strongest evaluated agent, Claude Opus 5, achieves 57.04% overall coverage, followed by
Grok 4.5 at 50.98%, leaving substantial room for improvement even among frontier agents. Rubric judgments
agree with human labels at a Macro-F1 of 0.893, and agent rankings remain stable under perturbations of all
metric parameters.
Our main contributions are as follows:
• We introduce DRBench Pro, a knowledge-centric benchmark dedicated to structured knowledge coverage
in deep research reports, comprising 144 cases across 24 domains and 44,102 evidence-grounded rubrics,
built by an automated pipeline that jointly generates each research query and its hidden rubrics from the
same Wikipedia-derived subgraph while preserving the evidence provenance and structural position of
every rubric.
• We propose topology-aware breadth and depth metrics, together with overall coverage, to distinguish
coverage across research directions from progression along related knowledge chains, supported by human-
consistency and stability analyses.
• We evaluate 16 deep research agents and identify distinct coverage strengths and remaining gaps. We
also provide a local research framework with a fixed Wikipedia snapshot, standardized tools, and shared
workflow and interaction budgets for controlled evaluation of backbone LLMs.

Related Work
Deep research evaluation.
Deep research benchmarks evaluate reports through task-specific criteria covering
comprehensiveness, factuality, and citation quality (Du et al., 2026; Sharma et al., 2026; Han et al., 2025;
Zhong et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026b; Avraham et al., 2026). Expected content is drawn from expert reports,
Wikipedia, generated criteria, or research graphs, with varying degrees of automation in task and rubric
construction (Li et al., 2026b; Wang et al., 2026c;d; Dong et al., 2026). For survey-based reports, reference-
literature recall and statement veracity measure source coverage and factual accuracy (Li et al., 2025b).
Complementary evaluations examine information gathering through broad exploration, passage-retrieval
completeness, and exhaustive collection (Java et al., 2026; Kim et al., 2026; Wong et al., 2026; Gupta et al.,
2026).
Factuality and content coverage.
Factuality and coverage address complementary questions: whether
reported claims are supported, and whether relevant information is missing. Claim-level verification and
citation audits check generated statements against evidence (Min et al., 2023; Wei et al., 2024; Song et al.,
2024b; Gao et al., 2023; Venkit et al., 2025), while joint analysis of retrieval and generation helps locate
failures in systems that use retrieved evidence (Ru et al., 2024). Coverage evaluation instead identifies
omissions by comparing outputs with reference content units and key facts (Nenkova & Passonneau, 2004; Liu
et al., 2023; Song et al., 2024a; Lee et al., 2024), testing whether they answer reference questions (Deutsch
et al., 2021; Scialom et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2024; Tan et al., 2024), or checking for salient aspects and
insights across documents (Samarinas et al., 2025; Pradeep et al., 2025; Laban et al., 2024). Automated
rubric generation and judgment calibration support the construction and application of these evaluation
criteria (Rao & Callison-Burch, 2026; Hashemi et al., 2024).
Structured construction and evaluation.
Graphs and trees organize multi-hop questions (Welbl et al., 2018;
Yang et al., 2018; Ho et al., 2020; Trivedi et al., 2022; Zhu et al., 2024) and synthetic web-search tasks (Li
et al., 2025a; Lu et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025; Tao et al., 2025; 2026; Li et al., 2026a). For deep research,
they connect queries, evidence, and rubrics to support reinforcement learning supervision and propagation of
rubric weights (Zhu et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026a). Structured evidence also enables tracing across research
stages and depth and width tests for system comparison (Sun et al., 2026; Gao et al., 2026). Using structure
to construct tasks or trace evidence is distinct from retaining it to aggregate report coverage. Our benchmark
makes structured knowledge coverage the primary evaluation target: each rubric retains its position in an

Benchmark Construction and Evaluation Protocol
Measuring knowledge coverage requires a reference that specifies both what a report could cover and how
that knowledge is organized. We introduce DRBench Pro, which uses Wikipedia-derived knowledge trees
to define research tasks and their evaluation criteria within a shared reference space. The tree structure
separates major research directions from chains of related knowledge within them, enabling distinct measures
of breadth and depth. Figure 2 illustrates the construction and evaluation pipeline. The prompts used for
benchmark construction and evaluation are provided in Appendix J.
3.1
Wikipedia-derived Local Graph Construction
A reference space for knowledge coverage needs to connect research topics with supporting evidence. Wikipedia
provides linked entities whose relationships can be examined in their source context. To span diverse domains,
we adopt all 24 topic categories from WebOrganizer (Wettig et al., 2025) and select two root entities per
category. Each root has a sufficiently detailed, reliably parseable article and enough outgoing links to support
expansion.
Starting from each root, we expand Wikipedia links breadth-first. Nodes represent entities, and edges
represent relationships supported by link anchors and surrounding text. We organize the retained expansion
as a rooted tree, with one parent per non-root node and a unique path from the root. This structure gives
each relation an identifiable position for subsequent rubric construction and coverage aggregation. Distance
from the root is measured by the number of links along this path, and expansion stops at three hops.
To provide diverse candidate research directions while bounding the size of deeper expansions, we retain 50
children of the root and at most five children of each other node. Invalid or duplicate candidates are filtered
4


before Claude Opus 4.8 selects neighbors for their relevance to the current entity and usefulness for research
tasks. The resulting 48 local knowledge trees contain approximately 1.5K nodes each on average. Appendix A
reports detailed statistics, and Appendix C.1 analyzes the choice of two roots per domain. Model-call costs
for construction are reported in Appendix H.
3.2
Subgraph Sampling
A full knowledge tree spans more material than a single research request can reasonably target. We therefore
sample smaller subgraphs, each defining the scope of one research case. A branch consists of one child of the
root and all its descendants: it represents a research direction, while paths within it capture chains of related
knowledge. Each case retains the root and 10 complete branches, preserving the knowledge structure within
the selected directions.
To make each scope coherent, Claude Opus 4.8 jointly selects three groups of branches from each tree, using
the candidate entities’ page summaries, relations to the root, and descendant information. Branches within
a group are selected to provide complementary perspectives on a common research task; the three groups
do not share root branches. Retaining complete branches preserves the deeper relations needed to evaluate
coverage within each direction. This yields three cases per root and 144 cases across the 24 domains.
3.3
Query and Rubric Construction
The research request and its evaluation criteria should refer to the same knowledge scope. We therefore
use Claude Opus 4.8 to generate both the query and the rubrics from each sampled subgraph. For query
generation, the model receives the root entity and the sampled knowledge scope, and is instructed to frame
a natural research request around their broader themes. The prompt discourages listing hidden entities or
restating the subgraph as a checklist, so the agent is asked to investigate a topic rather than reproduce an
explicit answer outline.
To make the expected coverage traceable to evidence, we create one rubric per retained relation edge. Each
rubric asks whether the report covers the relationship between its source and target entities. The generator
uses only the supplied evidence: page summaries, the anchor sentence, relevant paragraphs, and available
backlink context from the two pages. This ties each scoring requirement to documented content rather than
an unsupported model-generated expectation.
Each rubric retains its edge’s position in the reference tree, allowing its judgment to be aggregated with those
of related rubrics. We also record its hop distance as the distance from the root to the edge’s target node for
structural diagnostics. These annotations are kept outside the rubric text. Evaluated agents receive only the
query; the reference tree and rubrics remain hidden.
3.4
Topology-aware Knowledge Coverage Evaluation
Counting satisfied rubrics alone can give similar scores to reports with different coverage patterns. We
therefore retain the reference tree when aggregating judgments, distinguishing coverage across research
directions from coverage along related knowledge chains. An LLM judge assigns each hidden rubric one
of three verdicts: Satisfied, Partially Satisfied, or Not Satisfied. For coverage metric computation, we map
Satisfied to 1 (covered) and both Partially Satisfied and Not Satisfied to 0 (uncovered). These binary values
are then aggregated over the reference tree.
Breadth.
Breadth measures how comprehensively a report covers the selected research directions. We give
these directions equal weight so that a branch with more rubrics does not dominate the score. Within a
branch, deeper relations can demonstrate coverage of a direction even when a shallower relation is missing.
We preserve each relation’s direct score as a weighted component and use the mean breadth of its child
subtrees to account for descendant coverage. Averaging the resulting branch scores gives case-level breadth.
5


Depth.
Depth measures coverage along related knowledge chains in the reference tree. A report can cover
one chain deeply without covering every branch equally, so we give greater weight to child subtrees with higher
depth scores while retaining smaller contributions from the others. Each relation contributes its own score
and regulates how much descendant coverage is credited: an uncovered intermediate relation reduces that
credit without discarding it. This rewards continuity along knowledge chains. We apply the same rank-based
weighting across the selected research directions to obtain case-level depth. Appendix C.5 analyzes the textual
organization of supporting evidence for consecutive satisfied rubrics.
Overall coverage.

Evaluation
4.1
Evaluation Setup
Evaluation methods.
We evaluate 16 deep research agents and group them into three categories according
to how search and research orchestration are provided.
(1) Deep Research Systems.
These are end-to-end research agents explicitly designed for autonomous,
long-horizon investigation. They natively combine planning, iterative evidence gathering, and long-form
synthesis. We evaluate o3 Deep Research, Perplexity Sonar Deep Research, and Qwen Deep Research.
(2) Native Search LLMs.
These models integrate provider-native web search directly into generation
without relying on a separate Deep Research product workflow. We evaluate Grok 4.5, Claude Opus 5,
GPT-5.6 Sol, Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview, Sonar Reasoning Pro, and Qwen3.8 Max with native web search
enabled.
(3) Backbone LLMs under the local research framework.
As a controlled and reproducible evalua-
tion setting, we implement the local research framework backed by the fixed English Wikipedia dump
enwiki-20260701-v1.
The framework exposes Search and Open operations over the same snapshot,
disables provider-native web search, and keeps the research workflow and budget fixed across backbone LLMs.
Its purpose is to provide a common search service into which different backbone LLMs can be integrated,
rather than to improve their benchmark scores. This design removes temporal drift in the retrieval corpus,
reduces variation from external search infrastructure, and retains auditable evidence records. We evaluate
Gemma 4 31B, DeepSeek V4 Pro, DeepSeek V4 Flash, Qwen3.8 Max, Qwen3.7 Plus, GLM 5.2, and MiniMax
M3 as backbone LLMs. The execution protocol and formal configuration of the local research framework are
detailed in Appendix F. Appendix D.5 evaluates LangChain Open Deep Research with DeepSeek V4 Flash
on a small sample.
Implementation details.
The evaluated reports were collected during the periods listed in Appendix G.
We use Gemini 2.5 Pro as the judge for the formal evaluation. It assigns each rubric one of three verdicts:
Satisfied, Partially Satisfied, or Not Satisfied. We retain these original judgments and convert them into
binary rubric scores of 0 or 1 for the metrics in Section 3.4. Appendix H reports the recorded model-call
costs for report generation and judge evaluation.

4.2
Evaluation Results
Overall performance.
Table 2 shows that Claude Opus 5 achieves the highest overall coverage (57.04%) and
ranks first in both breadth (53.09%) and depth (61.91%), yielding strong performance in both dimensions.
Grok 4.5 and o3 Deep Research follow, with relatively stronger breadth and depth, respectively. High overall
coverage therefore requires both comprehensive coverage across major directions and progress along related
knowledge chains. Appendix C.3 lists selected results from prior benchmarks for context.
Interleaved breadth–depth profiles.
The agent orderings differ substantially between breadth and depth,
revealing distinct research strengths and showing that research workflows can produce markedly different
coverage profiles. The clearest example is Qwen3.8 Max: under the local research framework, its breadth
and depth scores are 15.54 and 6.28 percentage points higher, respectively, than under its Native Search
configuration, with the difference concentrated primarily in breadth. The two configurations of the same-named
model therefore exhibit different knowledge-exploration patterns: the local research framework configuration
covers major research directions more comprehensively, whereas the depth difference is comparatively limited.
A similar contrast appears within the Perplexity product line: Perplexity Sonar Deep Research attains higher
depth but slightly lower breadth than Sonar Reasoning Pro. Together, these results show that breadth and
depth expose agent specializations and research-strategy differences that overall coverage alone cannot reveal.

5
Conclusion
We introduce DRBench Pro, a knowledge-centric benchmark for professional deep research agents. From each
sampled multi-hop Wikipedia-derived entity subgraph, it jointly generates a research query and traceable edge-
grounded rubrics, giving the case, knowledge scope, and evaluation criteria a shared structural foundation.
Topology-aware aggregation captures breadth across major research directions and depth along related
knowledge chains, with their geometric mean yielding overall coverage. Evaluation across 16 deep research
agents reveals interleaved breadth–depth profiles; with the knowledge source, workflow, tool interface, and
interaction budget fixed, the local research framework further distinguishes the coverage behavior of different
backbone LLMs. Overall, DRBench Pro uses graph topology in both benchmark construction and metric
formulation, providing a scalable, structure-aware foundation for evaluating knowledge coverage in deep
research reports.

B.2
Breadth: Local-descendant Coverage Fusion
Breadth measures how comprehensively a report covers the selected research directions. A covered relation
provides local evidence but does not alone establish coverage of its descendant subtopics. We therefore
compute breadth as a weighted average of the current relation’s coverage and the mean breadth of its
child subtrees, allowing coverage of descendant subtopics to contribute recursively to the current direction’s
breadth.
For a non-leaf, non-root node v, let mB(v) be the mean of B(w) over w ∈Ch(v). We define
B(v) = βx(v) + (1 −β)mB(v).
(3)
Here, 0 < β < 1 weights the current relation’s coverage, while 1 −β weights the mean breadth of its child
subtrees. Covering a non-leaf relation alone therefore does not yield full breadth credit for its subtree.
Conversely, covered descendants can still contribute when the current relation is uncovered, partially
compensating for the current relation’s lack of coverage. Their contribution propagates upward through the
recursion to the branch-level breadth score.

Depth should also reward continuous coverage along a knowledge chain, where successive parent–child relations
are covered. Descendant evidence deserves greater credit when the report also covers the relation linking the

C.2
Research Query Quality Criteria
Research Value.
This criterion asks whether the query warrants a research report. A strong query calls for
substantive investigation, retrieval, and synthesis across relevant evidence; a request answerable through a
short factual lookup has limited research value.
Public Answerability.
This criterion asks whether reliable, publicly available sources can, in principle,
support an answer to the query. The answer need not appear in a single source, but the requested findings
must be verifiable without private information or unsupported speculation.
Query–Domain Match.
This criterion asks whether the query’s central topic and requested analysis fit
its assigned domain. The domain is shown alongside the query during review. Cross-domain connections
are compatible with a match when the assigned domain remains central; a merely incidental connection is
insufficient.

Additional Results from Prior Benchmarks
Table 13 compiles selected results from prior benchmarks, focusing on model families represented in Table 2
and evaluation dimensions with definitions similar to ours. These results serve as an external reference for
our evaluation, with each row labeled by its operating setting in the source study. Setting 1 (Deep Research
product) denotes an end-to-end research product or system that integrates research planning, iterative
retrieval, and report generation. Setting 2 (native search / search tools) denotes a model using native search
or search tools during generation. Setting 3 (external framework + backbone) denotes a backbone model
embedded in an external retrieval or research framework that organizes tool calls and the research workflow.
These labels describe the source experiments; the same model can use different settings across studies. In
HiEviDR (Sun et al., 2026), 3R and 3D denote the RAG and Deep Research workflows, respectively. For
ResearchRubrics (Sharma et al., 2026), we report binary compliance on tasks with high conceptual breadth
or deep logical nesting; DR-Arena’s Depth/Width values describe the composition of failure types, rather
than standalone ability scores (Gao et al., 2026).
Cross-benchmark comparisons require accounting for differences in experimental settings, model versions,
and metric definitions. Studies may use different retrieval resources, tool interfaces, and execution budgets;
evaluations conducted at different times often cover different versions within the same model family; and
even similarly named metrics may differ in their reference content, scoring criteria, and aggregation rules. We
therefore compare relative performance trends among related models and interpret agreement and variation
in light of these conditions.
For model pairs with relatively clear correspondences, our results agree with several prior studies. In Table 2,
o3 Deep Research achieves higher overall coverage than Perplexity Sonar Deep Research. The correspond-
ing OpenAI–Perplexity product-family ordering also appears in the selected dimensions of DeepResearch
Bench (Du et al., 2026), DeepResearch Bench II (Li et al., 2026b), ResearchRubrics, Wiki Live Challenge’s
Broad metric (Wang et al., 2026c), DeepResearchEval (Wang et al., 2026d), and LiveResearchBench (Wang
et al., 2026b). The advantage of o3 Deep Research over Qwen Deep Research is also consistent with the
corresponding product-family results in DeepResearch Bench II, Wiki Live Challenge, and DeepResearchEval.
Among native-search models, LiveResearchBench orders GPT-5 above Gemini 2.5 Pro and Sonar Reasoning
Pro, matching the overall-coverage order of the corresponding model families in Table 2. Although versions
and protocols differ, these recurring relative advantages provide useful external context for our results.
Despite these recurring trends, some metrics and model pairs exhibit different relative orderings, including Wiki
Live Challenge’s Cov. Wiki, Super Research’s Coverage (Dong et al., 2026), and the GLM-5.2–DeepSeek-V4-
Pro comparison in From Simple QA to Deep Research (Wang et al., 2026a). Taken together, these comparisons
show that our evaluation recovers several relative performance trends reported in prior benchmarks, providing
complementary evidence for its validity.

from a different domain. The six agents comprise Perplexity Sonar Deep Research; GPT-5.6 Sol, Gemini 3.1

research framework. For each case, the same three annotators independently rate all six reports on breadth,
depth, and overall quality using a 1–10 integer scale, and their mean ratings serve as the human reference.
Annotation details are provided in Appendix I.2.

the supporting evidence appeared in different sections and found that knowledge points in a coherent logical
chain do not necessarily have to appear consecutively in the text. Reports may devote separate sections
to different explanatory steps, analytical dimensions, or historical stages, or introduce a phenomenon in
one section before tracing its origins in another. In such cases, mechanisms, supporting evidence, sources,
or applicability conditions can connect explanations across sections and sustain meaningful progression.
Appendix E.4 illustrates how evidence from different report sections can support a coherent knowledge chain.

The roguelike genre was born from a stew of technical experiments and
cultural influences in the late 1970s. A crucial ingredient was Dun-
geons & Dragons (D&D), the landmark 1974 tabletop RPG that in-
troduced dungeon exploration, monsters, loot, and character progres-
sion (insight.ieeeusa.org). With D&D’s popularity soaring, early com-
puter enthusiasts sought to recreate its adventurous feel in software.
By 1975, some of the first computer dungeon-crawl games appeared
on the PLATO time-sharing system – notably pedit5 (1975) and dnd
(also known as The Game of Dungeons, 1975) (arstechnica.com). These
PLATO games were rudimentary attempts to capture D&D’s perilous
dungeon expeditions: pedit5 featured a single-level maze of 40–50
rooms with monsters guarding treasure, while dnd expanded this idea
to multiple dungeon levels with traps and a final boss encounter (ar-
stechnica.com). Around the same time, a text adventure called Colos-
sal Cave Adventure (1977) shaped the genre’s thematic tone (arstech-
nica.com). Unlike later roguelikes, Adventure wasn’t about combat or
random dungeons, but it established the template of an underground
quest filled with treasure and danger (e.g. navigating “a maze of twisty
little passages, all alike”). Its cave exploration vibe, inventory puzzles,
and myriad ways to die (with no easy resurrection) planted seeds that
would influence roguelike narratives (arstechnica.com). In short, Dun-
geons & Dragons and these first digital forays into interactive fantasy
provided the conceptual foundation – a world of monster-filled mazes,
treasure hunts, and high-stakes adventure – upon which the roguelike
genre would build.
Text-Only Technology and ASCII Art Dungeons
The late ’70s and early ’80s were an era of text-only or very low-
resolution computer displays, a factor that heavily shaped roguelike
design. Early personal computers like the Apple II (launched 1977)
had extremely limited graphics capabilities (insight.ieeeusa.org). Mean-
while, university systems and mainframes were often accessed via text
terminals with typically 80×24 character screens. These constraints
meant that complex graphical imagery was not feasible – but cleverly ar-
ranged text characters could serve as primitive graphics. A key break-
through was the curses library on Unix (developed by Ken Arnold)
which allowed programs to position characters at arbitrary places on
the terminal screen (insight.ieeeusa.org). This essentially let developers
paint a dungeon map using ASCII symbols. One of the earliest games to
use this approach was Rogue (1980), which drew top-down maze levels
out of letters and punctuation (insight.ieeeusa.org). Walls might be “|”
and “–” characters or “#” symbols for corridors, monsters were letters of
the alphabet, and the player was famously represented by an “@” sym-
bol (arstechnica.com). This visual style wasn’t chosen just for whimsy
– updating a screen of text was far less processor-intensive than draw-

Procedural Generation, Challenge, and “Perma-Death”
Another technical and design challenge of the time was how to create a
sizeable adventure on limited memory and to keep players engaged be-
yond a single play-through. Early adventure games like Colossal Cave
had fixed layouts that, once solved, offered little replay value (arstech-
nica.com). The solution arrived in the form of procedural generation –
algorithms that could randomize dungeon layouts, monster placements,
and item drops anew each game. This meant a vast game world didn’t
have to be stored in memory; it could be generated on the fly from a
few formulas. The concept was foreshadowed by pen-and-paper RPG
mechanics (which used dice rolls to inject unpredictability) and by com-
puter experiments like the PLATO dungeon crawlers. In fact, Beneath
Apple Manor (1978) on the Apple II was a pioneer in this area: it fea-
tured a dungeon that was randomly generated on each run (up to 10 lev-
els deep) with a “fog of war” effect that revealed rooms as you explored
(arstechnica.com). More famously, Rogue took procedural generation
to new heights – not only randomizing maps and monster encounters,
but also randomizing item identities so that a potion or wand’s effect
differed each game (insight.ieeeusa.org). This design, described at the
time as making a game “satisfying in the same ways as Rogue” while
staying unpredictable (arstechnica.com) (insight.ieeeusa.org), vastly in-
creased replayability and demanded that players learn general strategies
rather than memorizing levels.

Hand-in-hand with randomization came the ethos of permanent death,
or “perma-death.” If every excursion into the dungeon is unique, then
dying is meant to be an irreversible consequence that sends you back
to square one – a high stakes risk that makes the experience of each
run matter.
Early roguelikes embraced this fully.
In Rogue, death
was easy to come by and meant starting over from scratch, which
forced players to focus on mastering the game’s systems rather than
any single lucky run (arstechnica.com). (Notably, when some crafty
students found they could copy save files to cheat death, Rogue’s au-
thors changed the code to delete the save upon loading, enforcing per-
madeath (arstechnica.com).) This harsh mechanic had precedent in the
arcade and mainframe era (where many games simply didn’t allow sav-
ing progress), but it also echoed the spirit of D&D campaigns where a
character’s death could be permanent unless extraordinary means were
used. The combination of procedurally generated unknowns and per-
manent consequences established a level of tension and difficulty that
became a core identity of roguelikes. Players came to expect that each
dive into the dungeon would be unpredictable and unforgiving – a de-
sign philosophy that remained remarkably persistent even as games mi-
grated to more powerful computers. By 2008, the roguelike develop-
ment community formalized these traits in the “Berlin Interpretation,”
which listed procedural generation and permadeath (along with turn-
based play, resource management, and focus on exploration/combat
over narrative) as key defining factors of the genre (insight.ieeeusa.org).
The Birth of Rogue and its Founding Developers
While many of the ingredients of roguelikes were percolating in the
1970s, it was Rogue (1980) that truly combined them into the archetype
and gave the genre its name. The game was created by two University
of California, Santa Cruz students, Michael Toy and Glenn Wichman.
According to their recollections, they were directly inspired by earlier
interactive games – they loved Colossal Cave Adventure but wanted an
adventure that would be different each time, and they were avid D&D
players who craved a similar dungeon-crawling thrill on the computer
(arstechnica.com). They even drew ideas from a popular text-based Star
Trek strategy game from the 1970s (which had players hunt Klingon
ships in a grid-like sector map) (arstechnica.com). With these influ-
ences in mind, Toy and Wichman set out to build a turn-based dungeon
exploration game that featured D&D-style monsters, treasures, and lev-
eling, but in a randomized maze that felt fresh on every play.
Working on a UC Santa Cruz Unix mainframe, they leveraged
Ken Arnold’s new curses library to handle the screen display (in-
sight.ieeeusa.org).

Michael Toy continued refining Rogue when he transferred to UC

had Rogue installed by default. In the words of one history, “Rogue

BSD installs. .. by 1984, Rogue had been ported to DOS and Macin-

software-sharing culture, Rogue quickly leapt from campus to campus,
then onto early personal computers (through both free ports and a com-

– as opposed to any earlier lookalike – became the genre-defining title.
It reached the “right people” in large numbers (arstechnica.com), estab-

Core Identity and Lasting Legacy of the Roguelike Genre
Looking at how roguelikes coalesced in the late ’70s and early ’80s, it’s
clear that their defining features emerged from a meld of cultural inspi-
ration and technical limitation – and those features proved remarkably
enduring. The foundational recipe included procedurally generated
environments, turn-based play, permadeath, and minimalist graph-
ics, all wrapped in a dungeon-crawling, treasure-hunting framework.
None of these elements were entirely new in isolation, but together they
formed a distinctive identity. Crucially, each element reinforced the
others: for example, random dungeons and unidentified items made ev-
ery expedition unpredictable, and the threat of permanent death gave
those expeditions weight and tension. The sparse ASCII visuals not
only solved hardware constraints but also forced players to engage their
imagination – much as one might in a pen-and-paper RPG – which in
turn kept the focus on gameplay and strategy rather than flashy graph-
ics. And the DNA from Dungeons & Dragons provided a ready-made
structure for progression (experience levels, hit points, magic spells)
and narrative flavor (a lone hero delving into darkness on a quest), en-
suring these games had depth despite their simple appearance (arstech-
nica.com) (insight.ieeeusa.org). In effect, technical constraints and de-
sign choices acted in concert to produce a game experience greater than
the sum of its parts.
It’s telling that as computing power grew exponentially in subsequent
decades, roguelikes didn’t fade away or radically reinvent themselves
– instead, their core tenets persisted and spread into the wider gam-
ing world. Many later RPGs and action games borrowed the idea of
procedural content and high replay value (consider Diablo and its suc-
cessors, which were directly inspired by Rogue’s random dungeon con-
cept, though they dropped turn-based permadeath to court a broader
audience). The roguelike community itself continued to thrive by em-
bracing the old-school format: even when 3D graphics and real-time
gameplay became the industry norm, devotees kept making and playing
ASCII or tile-based roguelikes that felt very much like 1980s Rogue on
the surface. The reason is partly tradition – the roguelike fanbase came
to love the quirky symbolism of @ signs and letters, and the games’
brutally fair challenge – and partly that those core design elements are
effective at what they set out to do. Random generation and permadeath
create a unique drama and difficulty that scripted, save-heavy games
often lack, so the appeal endures as a particular kind of gaming chal-
lenge. For example, the “Berlin Interpretation” of 2008 (a roguelike
37


developer conference) explicitly reaffirmed that features like turn-based
movement, permadeath, procedural generation, resource management,
and focus on exploration/combat over plot are the essence of the genre
(insight.ieeeusa.org). Developers have proven that even if you swap the
ASCII for modern graphics or hybridize the format (leading to so-called
“roguelite” games), the heart of a roguelike – unpredictable adventures
testing players’ ingenuity under daunting stakes – remains compelling.
In summary, the roguelike genre was forged by the interplay of 1970s
culture and 1980s computers, and it solidified a set of gameplay princi-
ples so robust that they continue to resonate with creators and players
well into the 21st century, long after the era of text-based terminals has
passed.

Human Annotation Protocol
I.1
Rubric-level annotation
Annotators and annotation procedure.
The annotations support the rubric-level human–judge comparison
in Section 4.5. Ten PhD-level annotators participated in the human evaluation. The annotation sample
comprised 3,695 report–rubric pairs from 12 reports, with each report representing a different benchmark
domain. Under the annotation protocol, each pair receives two independent initial labels. Annotators do not
have access to one another’s labels or the LLM judges’ verdicts. A third annotator adjudicates disagreements
using the relevant report passages and the same annotation criteria, producing the final human reference.
Annotation criteria.
Each report–rubric pair received one of three labels: Satisfied, Partially Satisfied, or
Not Satisfied. Satisfied indicates that the report explicitly states or clearly paraphrases the rubric’s required
knowledge, including its essential relation and qualifications. Partially Satisfied indicates that the report
covers a substantive part of the requirement but omits an essential detail or qualification. Not Satisfied
indicates that the required knowledge is absent; merely mentioning a related entity or discussing the general
topic does not constitute coverage. Judgments concern content present in the report, without filling gaps
from external knowledge. Figure 12 illustrates the annotation interface during the report review process.
Agreement and judge comparison.
Initial inter-annotator agreement was Cohen’s κ = 0.841, computed
before adjudication over all 3,695 report–rubric pairs using the original three-category labels. The resulting
human-reviewed labels serve as the reference for comparison with three LLM judges. We compare the judges’
original three-category verdicts with this reference. For the binary comparison, Satisfied is mapped to 1
(covered), while Partially Satisfied and Not Satisfied are mapped to 0 (uncovered), for both human and judge
labels. Macro-F1 is computed separately for each report and then averaged equally across the 12 reports.

I.2
Report-level expert assessment
The report-level assessment in Section 4.5 comprises 72 reports from six deep research agents across 12
cases from distinct domains. Each case includes six reports answering the same research query. Ten PhD-
level annotators participate in this assessment. For each case, the same three annotators from this pool
independently rate all six reports on breadth, depth, and overall quality using a 1–10 integer scale, and their
mean ratings serve as the human reference. Annotators assess reports without access to agent identities,
automatically computed scores, or one another’s ratings. Differences among annotators are reflected in the
mean rating.
