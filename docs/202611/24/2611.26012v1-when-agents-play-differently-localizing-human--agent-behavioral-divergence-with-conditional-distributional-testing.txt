When Agents Play Differently: Localizing
Human–Agent Behavioral Divergence with
Conditional Distributional Testing
Oleg Smirnov∗, John Pertoft∗, Sara Karimi∗†
∗King AI Labs, Microsoft Gaming, Stockholm, Sweden
†KTH Royal Institute of Technology, Stockholm, Sweden
Abstract—Evaluating whether artificial agents behave like
humans requires comparing not just average outcomes but entire
behavioral distributions across diverse contexts. We propose a
stratified framework that computes nonparametric discrepancy
statistics within contextual cells, and discovers subgroup rules
identifying where agent behavior departs from human play. We
show that session-level metrics dominated by win/loss outcomes
offer little signal beyond mean comparisons, whereas process-
level metrics such as move efficiency reveal genuine behavioral
differences. Applied to a commercial match-3 game, it uncovers
patterns invisible to aggregate metrics: a playtesting bot is
diverging substantially from humans in certain game modes,
while showing no detectable divergence in others. We release
our implementation as open source to support reproducibility
and adoption.
Index Terms—Believable agents, game-playing agents, human-
likeness, automated playtesting, agent evaluation, distributional
testing.
I. INTRODUCTION
The evaluation of artificial agents hinges on their ability to
behave in a human-like manner. In interactive domains such as
digital games, human-likeness is not merely aesthetic: agents
that emulate human play enable scalable testing, balance
evaluation, and user modeling without the cost of large-scale
human trials [1, 2]. Conversely, agents that diverge sharply
from human strategies can produce misleading test results or
exploit unrealistic gameplay patterns. A bot validated only on
aggregate outcomes such as overall win rate may still play a
level in a distinctly non-human way, leading a designer to mis-
estimate its difficulty or misjudge how players will experience
it.
Existing metrics often rely on aggregate performance mea-
sures, e.g., average scores or success rates, which summarize
only the mean outcome [3]. Such scalar indicators obscure
differences in variability, timing, or decision structure that
define how humans actually behave: two agents may exhibit
identical mean success rates yet differ fundamentally in the
distribution or dynamics of their actions. A more rigorous for-
mulation views human-likeness as a problem of distributional
alignment: given observations from humans and agents under
shared contexts, assess whether their behavioral distributions
coincide. This connects directly to nonparametric two-sample
hypothesis testing [4, 5]
In practice, however, such comparisons must be made
conditionally on relevant covariates, such as content or player

which content and turns a diffuse “not human-like enough”

II. RELATED WORK
a) Human-likeness and playtesting in games: Believabil-
ity and human-likeness in games have long been assessed
through Turing-test-style judgments and believability compe-
titions [7, 8, 9]. Recent work quantifies human-likeness in
specific behaviors such as navigation with learned or statistical
evaluators [10, 3], while learned agents are increasingly used
for automated playtesting, from procedural personas to deep-
learning policies [2, 11]. Such approaches typically render a
global verdict, human-like or not, or optimize agents toward
it. We instead localize where and how an agent’s behavior
diverges from human play, conditional on game context.
b) Distributional two-sample testing:
Comparing full
distributions rather than means is well studied: Wasserstein
distance [12, 13], energy distance [14], and kernel MMD [15]
each provide nonparametric test procedures with known con-
sistency guarantees. Classifier-based two-sample tests instead
use a trained discriminator’s held-out accuracy as the statis-
tic [16, 17]. We favor kernel discrepancies, which require no
training step and align with distributional indistinguishability.
Our approach treats these as interchangeable cell-level build-
ing blocks rather than standalone global tests, embedding them
within a stratified aggregation and rule-discovery pipeline.
c) Stratification and meta-analytic aggregation: Com-
bining within-stratum evidence into a global test dates to the
van Elteren and CMH procedures [18, 19] and to random-
effects meta-analysis with inverse-variance weighting [20].
Our aggregation of cell-level standardized statistics follows
this tradition but extends it in two directions: the per-cell tests
are distributional rather than mean-based, and the aggregated
statistics feed a tree-based rule-discovery step that localizes
heterogeneous effects.
d) Multiple testing over discovered structure: Exploring
many candidate subgroups demands error control, for which
the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure provides false-discovery-
rate (FDR) guarantees [21]. We apply depth-wise correction
within tree levels and control false discoveries over the data-
selected partition by multi-split aggregation of held-out p-
values [22]. The goal of localizing where two distributions
differ, rather than only testing globally, is formalized by
Kim et al. [6], who propose a two-stage procedure that first
tests a global null and then identifies regions of significant
divergence. Our rule tree serves a similar purpose but discovers
interpretable subgroup structure through recursive partitioning
rather than post-hoc region detection.

Applying this criterion to common game metrics: win rate is
a Bernoulli variable fully characterized by its mean, so testing
it is exactly a proportions test. Total moves used and game
score are bimodal mixtures whose mode masses track the
success rate, and are therefore outcome-dominated. Attempts-
per-success, though not outcome-dominated, is undefined for
deterministic single-rollout agents, which produce a fixed out-
come on a given level. Process-level move-efficiency metrics
avoid all three: they are continuous rather than binary, largely
orthogonal to the win/loss outcome, and defined on every
session for both groups regardless of outcome.
We therefore test two process-level move-efficiency metrics,
score per move and cascades per move. These capture how an
agent plays rather than whether it succeeds: a winning and
a losing session can show similar per-move efficiency if the
player performed well but ran out of moves. Two agents may
achieve identical success rates yet differ in play style, e.g.,
with one proceeding through steady progress, while the other
relying on high-variance cascades, where a cascade is a chain
reaction in which cleared tiles trigger further clears without
an additional move.

Any discrepancy yielding an effect estimate and a standard

a) Cells and per-cell testing: Observations from both
groups are merged and continuous covariates discretized into
ordered bins, yielding a grid of context cells. Where a covariate
is undefined for one group, those observations act as wildcards
that match all concrete values. Within each cell, a two-sample
test contributes an effect estimate ti and standard error si
as local evidence of divergence. A well-defined SE is essen-
tial for the meta-analytic aggregation that follows, obtained
analytically under asymptotic normality or by resampling.
When cells share second-group observations their statistics are
correlated, so bootstrap resampling is performed jointly: the
second group is resampled once per iteration and filtered to
each cell, yielding correct marginal standard errors.

V. EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
We apply the conditional two-sample testing pipeline to
session-level data from a commercial match-3 game, compar-
ing two playtesting bots (automated agents that play levels
in place of human testers) against human players: a super-
vised behavioral cloning model (SL) trained on the produc-
tion data inspired by the deep-learning playtesting approach
of Gudmundsson et al. [11], and a heuristic model (Hbot)
implementing handcrafted hint-based strategies. Both agents
are evaluated against the same human population across 24
context cells defined by level difficulty (0–3) and game mode:
Chain (cascade-oriented), Clear (objective clearing), and Route
(stream routing, with three variants). The three modes differ
in objective: Chain rewards cascades, Clear requires removing
designated target tiles within the move limit, and Route
requires guiding board elements to fixed destinations. For each
agent, the dataset comprises approximately 110,000 player
sessions and 5,000 agent sessions obtained via a table sample
of the full dataset. Two continuous move efficiency metrics are
tested: score per move and cascades per move. Both metrics
exhibit low outcome dominance (R2(win) = 0.036 for score
per move and 0.121 for cascades per move), confirming that
their distributional content is not reducible to success rate
differences.
The pipeline utilizes euMMD [26] as a computationally ef-
ficient discrepancy measure. Per-cell p-values use default 200
permutations. The two-sided p-value is floored at 0.5/201 ≈
0.0025, which is the p ≈0.002 saturation seen in most cells.
Per-cell standard errors are computed separately from 100
distinct bootstrap resamples. Partial pooling is applied via
DerSimonian-Laird random effects, and rule discovery uses
χ2 gain with depth-wise Benjamini-Hochberg correction at
α = 0.05. The main analysis focuses on these two agents,
which exhibit qualitatively different divergence profiles.

A. Per-Cell Two-Sample Tests
Figure 2 reports per-cell effect estimates for both agents
on both metrics. Here ∆is the unbiased MMD2 U-statistic
and can be negative under the null, and p-values are obtained
by permutation. For score per move, the two agents show
similar gradients: large effects in Chain at all difficulties and at
medium-difficulty Route variants, with hard Clear emerging as
the most aligned subgroup for both (∆≈0.000–0.001, non-

structurally between the two agents. The SL model diverges

C. What the Divergence Looks Like
The rule trees identify where the agents diverge, whereas
the underlying distributions show how. Figure 3 overlays the
human and agent distributions of cascades per move in the
most divergent Chain cell and in a near-aligned Clear cell. In
Chain, human play spreads toward high cascade rates, with
substantial mass beyond three cascades per move, whereas
both agents concentrate at lower values and rarely sustain the
long chains that humans produce, with the learned SL model
the most shifted. The gap is one of both location and shape,
and it is precisely the behavior a cascade-oriented mode is
meant to elicit. In hard Clear the three distributions nearly
coincide, the distributional counterpart of the non-significant
effect estimate there.
D. Analysis
The main finding is a contextual dissociation between the
two agents on cascades per move. Although both models
cover the full context, their cascade divergence concentrates
on opposite sides of the Chain axis: the SL model is most
non-human in Chain levels dominated by cascade reactions,
while the heuristic model is most non-human outside Chain,
such as objective-driven levels where hints are tailored for
targeted completion. A single global mean over cells averages
these effects to a small positive number for both agents, hiding
the structural contrast. The pipeline recovers the contrast as
the dominant feature of the cascade rule trees and makes it
explicit at the cell level. Notably, this discovered contrast is be-
tween learning from human demonstrations versus handcrafted
rules, rather than between architectures or objectives within
the learning-based class. While both agents were originally
designed to be human-like, SL by learning from human play
and Hbot through hints tuned to guide play as a human would,
each of them departs from human behavior in a different,
context-localized way that an aggregate score would report
as near-alignment.
A secondary finding is a model-independent alignment
subgroup: hard Clear shows no detectable divergence for either
agent on score per move (∆≈0.001, non-significant), and this
pattern replicates across both score-tree variants. Establishing
practical equivalence rather than mere non-significance is
possible through a calibrated human self-comparison.
To confirm that the pipeline localizes genuine structure
rather than noise, we validate it on a synthetic benchmark
with a divergent region planted outcome-orthogonally, so that
group win rates and overall metric means are matched and only
the within-outcome shape differs. Table I reports the outcome:
under a global null the full pipeline is well calibrated (realized
FDR 0.004), whereas the same tree driven by a mean statistic
inflates to 0.216. On the planted region the MMD pipeline
recovers the divergent cells (precision 0.94, recall 1.00) while
the mean-only and success-rate baselines miss them almost
entirely. The discovery thus reflects distributional structure
invisible to outcome-level comparison.

VII. CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK
This work proposes the evaluation of human-likeness based
on conditional distributional alignment across contexts rather
than aggregate outcome matching. By framing human-likeness
as a stratified two-sample testing problem, the framework pro-
vides both a global discrepancy measure and an interpretable
rule structure describing where agents diverge from human
play. Applied to a large-scale commercial game, the analysis
reveals that an agent diverges from human play in game modes
dominated by specific mechanics while showing no detectable
divergence in others, a structured pattern that an aggregated
score would obscure.
Beyond diagnosis, the per-cell discrepancy statistics and rule
tree define a structured error signal that can further inform


targeted finetuning, curriculum design, or reward shaping.
However, such optimization requires care: directing an agent
to close a localized gap can invoke Goodhart’s law, raising
the targeted metric without making the underlying behavior
more human-like [27], so the signal is best used to inform the
training objective rather than to define it.
Among the method limitations, finer context partitions or
alternative metrics could reveal differentiation that the present
analysis does not surface. Moreover, the formal false-discovery
control over the discovered partition currently rests on the
multi-split procedure.
The concentration of cascade divergence in specific contexts
raises the question of whether the agent lacks spatial under-
standing needed to reproduce human cascade behavior. We
leave to future work a broader study across agent architectures
and training regimes and controlled interventions, such as
varying the model’s capacity, that would test those hypotheses
directly.
