ZARC: EXACT NEAREST NEIGHBORS OVER A BILLION VECTORS IN SECONDS
Dongfang Zhao (dzhao@uw.edu)
ABSTRACT
Exact k-nearest-neighbor vector search lies on
the critical path of machine learning systems,
from sparse attention in the latest large language
models (LLMs) to long-context memories and
training-data deduplication.
Exhaustive GPU
search scans the whole dataset for every query,
while approximate indexes return inexact neigh-
bors. Recent methods skip almost every vector
with a cheap half-precision proof that it lies too
far away, but only on one GPU that holds the
whole dataset. Our insight is to keep only the
short rows of these proofs in GPU memory and
to fetch the few remaining full vectors from host
memory. Our system, ZARC, answers Euclidean,
cosine, inner-product, and filtered queries exactly
under updates. On four H100 GPUs, it answers
10,000 exact queries over one billion vectors in
1.1 seconds instead of five minutes. On eleven
open LLMs, it retrieves the exact top-10 attention
keys 2.2× to 13.1× faster than exhaustive search.
In sparse attention over one million tokens, it runs
3.1× faster than bf16 selection with FlashInfer,
which misses the exact top-64 for most queries.

INTRODUCTION
Exact k-nearest-neighbor vector search lies on the critical
path of machine learning systems, in which every missed
neighbor becomes an error of the model or of its data. In
large language models (LLMs), sparse attention such as
that of DeepSeek-V4 (DeepSeek-AI, 2026) attends only to
the top-k entries of the preceding context for each query
token. Long-context memories such as kNN-LM (Khan-
delwal et al., 2020) and Memorizing Transformers (Wu
et al., 2022) likewise attend to the nearest keys of a memory
that grows with every generated token. Beyond attention,
semantic deduplication such as SemDeDup (Abbas et al.,
2023) relies on nearest neighbors, as does the evaluation
of representations such as CLIP (Radford et al., 2021) and
DINOv2 (Oquab et al., 2024) and of approximate search

GPU vector search systems either evaluate every distance

Exhaustive search in
Faiss (Johnson et al., 2021) and cuVS (NVIDIA, 2026)
scans every vector for every query. As a result, 10,000
queries over BIGANN-1B (Jegou et al., 2011) take about
five minutes on four H100 GPUs (Figure 1(a)). Exhaus-
tive search also keeps the raw vectors in device memory,
which model weights and KV caches claim as well. One
billion 128-dimensional vectors alone occupy 512 GB in
single precision (Figure 1(b)). Approximate indexes such
as HNSW (Malkov & Yashunin, 2020), DiskANN (Subra-
manya et al., 2019), CAGRA (Ootomo et al., 2024), and
product-quantized inverted files (J´egou et al., 2011; Babenko
& Lempitsky, 2012) return inexact neighbors without a
per-query guarantee, whose errors reach the model and its
data. For instance, CAGRA at its most accurate setting re-
turns the exact top-10 attention keys of Qwen2.5-7B (Yang
et al., 2024) for at most 45% of the queries. Similarly, NN-
Descent (Dong et al., 2011) leaves 3% to 56% of the rows
of the DataComp (Gadre et al., 2023) deduplication graph
inexact (Figure 1(c)). On BIGANN-10M at 0.1% selectivity,
the filtered graph index ACORN (Patel et al., 2024) returns
the exact neighbors of only 3.4% of the queries.
The two designs differ in the evidence on which they skip
a vector. Exhaustive search skips no vector and therefore
pays for every distance, whereas approximate search skips
vectors without proof that they lie beyond the k-th distance
and therefore loses neighbors. A vector can be skipped with-
out any loss of exactness only with such a proof, namely a
certified lower bound on its distance above the k-th distance.
Certified exclusion (Zhao, 2026) obtains this proof at the
speed of the tensor cores by computing the lower bounds in
half precision and including the rounding error of the prod-
uct in every bound. As a result, only the few vectors that are
not skipped need exact distances. Yet the CertEx engine that
introduced certified exclusion holds the whole dataset in the
memory of one GPU and supports no deletions, concurrent
updates, filters, or inner products.

This paper makes the following contributions:
• We design certified screening across the memory hi-
erarchy, with half-precision screening rows in GPU
memory, full-precision vectors in host memory, and
verification of the survivors in the order of their cer-
tified lower bounds. Thresholds derived from data
partitions and projections fitted to each partition cut
the raw vectors that a query reads over PCIe to 699 on
BIGANN-500M (Jegou et al., 2011). Updates, filters,
and inner products reuse the same certified bounds.
• We implement ZARC as a library with the interface of
cuVS (NVIDIA, 2026), ahead-of-time compiled tensor-
core kernels for Hopper and Ampere GPUs, concurrent
updates, a calibrated cost model, and sharded search
over the GPUs of one server.
• We evaluate ZARC on eleven open LLMs from 7B to
1.6T parameters, in sparse attention over a one-million-
token context, and on billion-scale benchmarks, filtered
search, streaming updates, semantic deduplication, and
passage retrieval, where it is exact for every query, up
to 270× faster than exhaustive GPU search, and 2.2×
to 13.1× faster on LLM attention keys.

MOTIVATION AND CHALLENGES
2.1
Exact Search Workloads
Various critical components of modern AI systems rely on
efficient and accurate vector search capabilities, such as
sparse attention, long-context memories, and deduplication.
These applications need exact answers over large datasets
that change over time. Exactness requires more than single-
precision distances whenever the largest scores of a query
lie in near ties, which attention scores often do (Section 4.2).
For example, the ten largest attention scores of a GLM-
5.3 (GLM-5 Team, 2026) query lie within 0.03% of each
other. The datasets are large enough that their raw vectors
compete for device memory with model weights and KV
caches. For example, BIGANN-500M (Jegou et al., 2011)
alone occupies 256 GB in single precision. The datasets

2.2
Comparison of Existing Systems
Table 1 compares vector search systems on these proper-
ties, where a partial mark denotes single-precision exact-
ness or insertions without deletions. Exhaustive search
in Faiss (Johnson et al., 2021; Douze et al., 2026) and
cuVS (NVIDIA, 2026) is exact but keeps the database
in device memory and scans it for every query. Inverted
files (Babenko & Lempitsky, 2012) and proximity graphs
such as HNSW (Malkov & Yashunin, 2020) avoid the full
scan by searching a fraction of the dataset, which makes
their recall depend on the probe count or the search width.
A graph also absorbs a change of the dataset only by an
extension or a rebuild. For instance, a rebuild takes 27.6 s
for CAGRA (Ootomo et al., 2024) over 8.8M passages on
an H100 and 49 minutes for ACORN-γ (Patel et al., 2024)
over the 10M vectors of BIGANN-10M (Jegou et al., 2011).
Filters raise the cost of both graph indexes and exhaustive
search. At low selectivity, the matching vertices of a graph
index often form a disconnected subgraph. Filtered graph
indexes such as ACORN (Patel et al., 2024) and Filtered-
DiskANN (Gollapudi et al., 2023) therefore add predicate-
agnostic or label-aware edges and still return approximate
answers. Exhaustive cuVS (NVIDIA, 2026) search stays
exact with a bitmap prefilter but still pays for the full scan.
2.3
Certified Exclusion and Its Limits
CertEx (Zhao, 2026) skips a vector when a lower bound on
its distance to the query exceeds a threshold, which bounds
the distance of the k-th nearest neighbor from above. The
threshold is the k-th smallest exact distance from the query
to a uniform sample of stored vectors, which CertEx calls
anchors. The lower bound needs only a short screening
row per vector, which holds in half precision the projection
of the vector onto the leading principal components of the
dataset. One tensor-core product evaluates the bounds of all
screening rows for a query. Because every bound includes
the worst-case rounding error of this product, no skipped
vector can be a true neighbor. Exact distances in full preci-
sion are then computed only for the few vectors that pass
this screen, which CertEx calls survivors.
Production workloads expose five limits of CertEx (Zhao,
2026), which keeps the raw vectors and the screening rows
of the whole dataset on one GPU. First, the uniform sample
loosens the threshold on clustered and near-duplicate data
(Section 3.2). Second, the raw vectors of a billion-scale
dataset do not fit beside its screening rows (Section 3.3).
Third, updates must keep every bound valid without a re-
build (Section 3.4). Fourth, the throughput of the exact
configurations varies by up to 5×, which calls for automatic
tuning of the number of projected dimensions and of the
number of anchors (Section 3.5). Finally, filters and inner
products need certified bounds of their own (Section 3.6).
3
DESIGN
3.1
System Architecture Overview
Figure 2 shows how ZARC executes a batch of queries in
four stages on the GPU while the raw vectors stay in host
memory. The threshold stage (A) computes for every query
a threshold on its k-th distance from the exact distances of
sampled anchors or of candidates in the nearest partitions
of the query. The screening stage (B) then screens the half-
precision rows of all vectors on the tensor cores and keeps
every row whose certified lower bound does not exceed
the threshold of the query (Appendix A). Each surviving
query-vector pair (C) carries its computed product, from
which ZARC derives a certified lower bound and orders the
survivors of each query. Verification (D) finally reads the


raw vectors of the survivors from host memory in this order
and computes their exact distances in double precision, until
the next lower bound exceeds the k-th distance found. Up-
dates (E) append the screening rows and raw vectors of new
vectors and mark the rows of deleted vectors, while queries
keep running on a consistent view of the index (Section 3.4).
Because every exclusion is certified and every survivor that
might be a neighbor is verified, the pipeline returns the exact
top-k. This guarantee rests on two invariants, namely that
every excluded row has a certified lower bound above its
threshold and that every threshold bounds the k-th distance
from above (Appendix B). Every mechanism below keeps
both invariants, including row placement across memories
and GPUs and concurrent updates.
ZARC screens on a persistent tensor-core kernel that never
writes the product matrix to memory. Each thread block
keeps a tile of query rows in shared memory and streams
tiles of screening rows through a pipeline of asynchronous
loads, whose products accumulate in single precision. Each
block reduces its rows in one sequential loop without
the split-K schedules of standard matrix-multiply libraries,
since the rounding-error bound holds only for this accumula-
tion order. The epilogue then compares every product with
the threshold of its query and compacts the survivors without
global synchronization. Each thread marks its survivors in a
bit mask and reserves output space with one shared-memory
atomic. As a result, an empty tile costs one comparison per
product and no memory traffic (Appendix C.1).
3.2
Tighter Bounds from Partitions
ZARC tightens the threshold with a k-means partition of the
dataset, whose partitions it stores as contiguous blocks of
rows. For every query, ZARC first screens under the anchor
threshold of CertEx (Zhao, 2026) only the rows of the few
partitions whose centroids are nearest to the query. A routed
variant of the screening kernel performs this step in one
launch over a list of work items, each of which pairs a tile
of queries with a tile of rows from one partition. Among
the screened rows, ZARC computes the exact distances of
a small multiple of k candidates with the smallest certified
lower bounds. The k-th smallest of these distances gives
a second threshold, which we call the partition threshold.
Screening of all rows then runs under the smaller threshold.
The partition threshold is safe for any choice of partitions
and candidates, because it is an exact distance to k distinct
stored vectors and thus bounds the true k-th distance from
above. The partitions therefore change only the speed of
ZARC, whereas the partitions of an inverted file decide
which vectors a query can return. Deduplication runs a self-
join, in which every stored vector is a query. Such a self-join
skips the screening of the nearest partitions. Instead, the
queries of a batch in storage order come from one or two
partitions, which directly give the threshold of the batch.
On a clustered dataset, the vectors lie far from one global
principal subspace but close to the subspace of their own
partition. ZARC therefore gives every partition its own local
projection onto the leading eigenvectors of the covariance of
the partition around its centroid, which tightens the bound.
Each partition encodes its rows with its own projection,
scale, and rounding-error constants, so that the certified
bound holds within the partition (Appendix D). A search
projects every query once per partition with one batched
matrix product, after which one routed launch screens every
partition with its query rows. The anchors and later inser-
tions keep the global projection and a plain launch. Every
vector thus meets one certified test, never two.
3.3
Tiered Placement Across GPUs
ZARC keeps in GPU memory only the half-precision screen-
ing rows and the anchor sample, which every query reads,
and keeps the full-precision vectors in pinned host mem-
ory, which the GPU reads by zero-copy access. With rows
several times shorter than full-precision vectors, the same
device memory holds several times as many vectors, and
optional one-byte integer rows halve the rows again (Ap-
pendix E). Host memory, however, sits behind PCIe, whose
bandwidth lies more than an order of magnitude below that
of device memory. With the survivors at scattered positions,
verification must read as few raw vectors as possible.
ZARC orders verification by the certified lower bounds them-
selves. After screening, a kernel computes the certified
lower bound of every survivor from its stored product and
packs the query and the bound into one integer key. A radix
sort of these keys then groups the survivors by query in
increasing order of their lower bounds. Verification runs
in rounds over all queries of a batch, in which every un-
finished query takes a window of its next survivors whose
lower bounds lie below its current k-th exact distance. The
window doubles from round to round instead of assigning
one warp to each query, which would serialize the long
survivor lists of the hardest queries over PCIe. One flat
launch computes the exact distances of a round in double
precision with several row reads of each warp in flight (Ap-
pendix C.2), after which a second launch merges them into
the top-k of each query. A query finishes once its window
reaches a lower bound above its k-th distance, which limits
the reads from host memory to a fraction of the survivors
(Appendix F).
A dataset whose screening rows exceed the memory of one
GPU splits into contiguous shards with one index per GPU,
which ZARC builds in parallel. Shards that screen against
their own k-th distances would keep many more survivors.
ZARC instead searches in two rounds. In the first round,
every shard computes a threshold for each query from its


own anchors. The smallest of these shard thresholds bounds
the global k-th distance, as any k distinct vectors of one
shard do (Appendix G). In the second round, every shard
screens and verifies under this shared threshold, after which
the host merges the exact shard answers. Only one threshold
and k pairs per shard and query cross the interconnect.
3.4
Updates Without Re-Indexing
ZARC inserts and deletes vectors while queries run, without
a rebuild. The host and device buffers of the raw vectors
and the screening rows are preallocated to a capacity, into
which an insertion appends its rows in place. The insertion
projects the new vectors with the existing projection, since
the lower bound stays valid for vectors that the projection
was not computed from. The rounding-error bound of every
query, however, depends on the largest norm and error terms
among all stored rows. A new vector with a larger norm
would therefore invalidate the bound of every running query,
unless the bound covered the new rows first.
ZARC enforces this order with a publication protocol (Ap-
pendix C.3). An update writes its rows beyond the published
rows without a lock and then publishes the new row count
together with the raised error bound under an exclusive lock.
Every search batch holds the same lock in shared mode.
Each batch thus reads one consistent view and never takes
its threshold from an anchor that it does not screen. A dele-
tion marks the row of the deleted vector so that it never
survives screening. It also removes the vector from the an-
chor sample. As insertions keep the projection fixed, ZARC
periodically recomputes it and rebuilds the rows.
3.5
Tuning by a Calibrated Cost Model
ZARC selects for every partition the number of projected
dimensions, the vectors per anchor, the query batch size,
and the memory tier of the full-precision vectors with a cost
model. It is calibrated once per device by microbenchmarks
and predicts the time of a batch from the screened rows,
the anchor distances, and the expected survivors per query
(Appendix H). As every configuration keeps every bound
valid, a wrong prediction costs only speed.
The survivors per query depend on the data in a way that
no static rule captures (Appendix I). For example, the best
number of dimensions shrinks as the dataset grows and
depends on the query distribution. Hence, the tuner runs the
search on two small prefixes of the dataset and extrapolates
the survivors and verified rows per query to the full size by
a power law. The cost model then ranks the configurations
by their predicted batch time and keeps the fastest one.
3.6
Filtered and Inner-Product Queries
A filtered query restricts its neighbors to the vectors that
carry a given label, such as a tenant or a category. Screening
needs no connectivity among the matching vectors, which
reduces a filter to the removal of rows from the screen. Only
the threshold depends on the label, because a threshold from
vectors of other labels can lie below the k-th distance among
the matching vectors and thus exclude true neighbors.
ZARC supports one label per vector and one label per query
in two ways. In the first way, the epilogue of the screen-
ing kernel never keeps a row of another label. The an-
chors of a query then come only from its label. This way
serves any per-row predicate, such as the bitset filters of
cuVS (NVIDIA, 2026), which only reject rows and leave
every other bound unchanged. In the second way, ZARC
stores every label as a contiguous block of rows with its own
anchors and sorts each batch of queries by label. The routed
kernel then pairs every tile of queries of one label only with
the rows of that label, so that the work of the routed kernel
shrinks in proportion to the selectivity (Appendix J).
For inner-product queries, ZARC bounds the inner product
directly. The standard reduction to Euclidean search ap-
pends to every vector a coordinate that raises its norm to
the largest norm of the dataset. Rounding this coordinate,
however, perturbs the norms by up to one unit in the last
place and can reorder near ties. ZARC instead builds the
screening rows without subtracting the mean of the dataset,
which preserves every norm. The same tensor-core product
then bounds the inner product of every vector from above
(Appendix B). Every vector whose bound falls below the
k-th largest exact inner product of any k distinct vectors
therefore lies outside the top-k. The threshold comes from
the exact inner products of the anchors. Verification then
ranks the survivors by exact inner products in double preci-
sion. Cosine queries screen normalized copies with a slack
for their rounding (Appendix K).

4.1
Experimental Setup
Open LLMs.
The LLM workload covers eleven open
LLMs from five developers with 7B to 1.6T parameters in
the DeepSeek-V4 (DeepSeek-AI, 2026; 2025), Qwen (Yang
et al., 2024; 2025a; Qwen Team, 2026), gpt-oss (OpenAI,
2025), GLM (GLM-5 Team, 2026), and Hy3 (Tencent Hun-
yuan Team, 2026) series. Per model, the memory holds
the keys of one layer before rotary position embedding
(RoPE) (Su et al., 2024), over the train books of PG-19 (Rae
et al., 2020), with 10M keys for each of the first four KV
heads on one H100. Each head receives 10,000 decoding
queries from the test books. For the multi-head latent atten-
tion (DeepSeek-AI, 2024) of DeepSeek and GLM, the mem-
ory holds the absorbed latent key that all heads share, whose
inner product with the absorbed query of a head equals its
attention logit before RoPE. The memory excludes the first
four positions of every chunk, the attention sinks (Xiao et al.,
2024b) that a deployed model keeps resident. Ground truth
comes from fp32 distances to all keys, rescored in fp64 and
checked against their rounding error (Appendix L).
Platform.
The main platform is a server with four 94 GB
H100 GPUs connected pairwise by NVLink, 192 cores of
the Intel Xeon Platinum 8468, and 960 GB of host DRAM,
which runs the billion-scale and LLM experiments. A sec-
ond server with one 94 GB H100 over PCIe 5.0 x16, 48
cores of the same processor, and 240 GB of host DRAM
runs the other experiments on one GPU. Both run Ubuntu
24.04, CUDA 12.6 with driver 560.35.05, CUTLASS 3.9.2,
PyTorch 2.14.1, and cuVS 26.10. On an A100 of the earlier
Ampere generation, every query stays exact and ZARC runs
11× to 26× faster than exhaustive search (Appendix M).

Metrics.
We report throughput in queries per second at
fixed batch sizes, the memory of each tier, and the build and
update throughput. For approximate baselines, the exact
fraction counts the queries whose k distances equal the exact
ones in double precision, which is stricter than Recall@10
under ties. Every answer of ZARC is checked against the
ground truth and matches it, which gives an exact fraction
of 100% throughout, including on adversarial inputs with
exact ties, duplicates, and extreme norms (Appendix O).

We evaluate the placement of the raw vectors in host memory

4.6
Streaming Dynamic Updates
We evaluate updates by growing a BIGANN (Jegou et al.,
2011) index from 50M to 100M vectors in ten insertion
steps and then deleting 10% of the vectors, with every query
checked against exhaustive search over the live vectors.
FreshDiskANN (Singh et al., 2021) on 48 CPU threads
serves as the baseline for insertions into a graph index.
Figure 9 shows that updates keep every answer exact at the
throughput of a rebuilt index. While insertions run at 78 to
82 million vectors per second, the throughput follows the
dataset size from 38.3k queries per second at 50M to 19.4k
at 100M, which matches the 18.7k of an index rebuilt at
100M (Figure 9(a)). While a background thread inserts 25
million vectors at 2 or 10 million vectors per second, the
queries keep 78% and 74% of their throughput, with every
one of the 170,000 checked answers exact (Figure 9(b)).
FreshDiskANN (Singh et al., 2021) on 48 CPU threads
inserts about 1,300× slower (Figure 9(c)), as it searches the
graph and repairs the edges around every new vector.

RELATED WORK
Exact search.
Metric access methods such as AESA (Vi-
dal Ruiz, 1986), cover trees (Beygelzimer et al., 2006),
VP-trees (Yianilos, 1993), M-trees (Ciaccia et al., 1997),
and FLANN (Muja & Lowe, 2014) and bound-based filters
such as GEMINI (Faloutsos et al., 1994), the VA-file (Weber
et al., 1998), and iDistance (Jagadish et al., 2005) prune can-
didates by pointer chasing and scalar comparisons, which
leave the matrix units of a GPU idle. Exhaustive GPU
search, from Garcia et al. (2008) to Faiss (Johnson et al.,
2021) and cuVS (NVIDIA, 2026), uses these matrix units
but evaluates every distance in single precision, which can
misrank near ties. Floating-point filters in computational
geometry (Fortune & Van Wyk, 1993; Shewchuk, 1997)
and validated numerics (Higham, 2002) resolve such cases
cheaply by evaluating in fast floating point and resorting
to exact arithmetic only where the error bound requires
it. ZARC applies such rounding-error bounds to the half-
precision tensor-core products that screen every vector.
Approximate
and
disk-resident
indexes.
Inverted
files (Johnson et al., 2021; Babenko & Lempitsky, 2012;
Baranchuk et al., 2018) over vectors compressed by
OPQ (Ge et al., 2013), ScaNN (Guo et al., 2020), or Ra-
BitQ (Gao & Long, 2024) and proximity graphs such as
HNSW (Malkov & Yashunin, 2020), NSG (Fu et al., 2019),
CAGRA (Ootomo et al., 2024), SONG (Zhao et al., 2020),
and GGNN (Groh et al., 2023) trade recall for through-
put through the probe count or the search width. HM-
ANN (Ren et al., 2020), BANG (Venkatasubba et al., 2025),
and DeepStore (Mailthody et al., 2019) place billion-scale
indexes in heterogeneous memory, on one GPU, or in stor-
age, which BaM (Qureshi et al., 2023) makes directly ac-
cessible to GPUs. DiskANN (Subramanya et al., 2019),
Starling (Wang et al., 2024), SPANN (Chen et al., 2021),
and FreshDiskANN (Singh et al., 2021) keep the full vec-
tors on solid-state storage, whose reads Rummy (Zhang
et al., 2024) and FusionANNS (Tian et al., 2025) overlap
with distance computation on the CPU and the GPU. Pi-
lotANN (Gui et al., 2026), SVFusion (Peng et al., 2026),
HAVEN (Hsu et al., 2026), and IVF-RaBitQ (Shi et al.,
2026) spread approximate search over the memory hierar-
chy of a GPU server, where the tier of a vector decides its
accuracy, whereas the tiers of ZARC decide only where a
vector is read from.
Filtered search.
Vector databases such as Milvus (Wang
et al., 2021), Manu (Guo et al., 2022), AnalyticDB-V (Wei
et al., 2020), and VBase (Zhang et al., 2023) integrate re-
lational predicates with vector indexes. At the index level,
ACORN (Patel et al., 2024) and Filtered-DiskANN (Gol-
lapudi et al., 2023) add predicate-agnostic or label-aware
edges to graph indexes. Both kinds of systems depend on
the connectivity of the matching vertices. ZARC, in contrast,
screens only the rows of the queried label.
Certified exclusion.
The error bounds of RaBitQ (Gao
& Long, 2024; Gao et al., 2025) and the conformal re-
call guarantees of ConANN (Horchidan et al., 2025; Vovk
et al., 2005) hold over a distribution of rotations or queries,
whereas those of ZARC hold for every query and every vec-
tor. These bounds come from CertEx (Zhao, 2026), which
proposed certified exclusion for data that fit on one GPU.
ZARC extends them with partition thresholds, memory tiers,
sharding, concurrent updates, filters, and inner products.
6
CONCLUSION
This paper presented ZARC, which makes exact vector
search practical at the scale of billion-vector datasets and
million-token contexts. Its central observation is that certi-
fied exclusion needs only the short half-precision screening
rows in GPU memory, whereas the few surviving vectors
can stay in host memory. On billion-scale benchmarks,
eleven open LLMs, and sparse attention over one million to-
kens, ZARC returns the exact answer for every query while
it runs up to 270× faster than exhaustive GPU search and,
in retrieval, faster than approximate indexes at their most
accurate settings. Since ZARC keeps the calls of cuVS,
Faiss, PyTorch, and FlashInfer, adopting it changes little
more than an import.

The paper states three guarantees of the design in prose,
which this section proves. The first concerns the partition
threshold of Section 3.2, the second the insertions and dele-
tions of Section 3.4, and the third the inner-product screen-
ing of Section 3.6. Each proof uses only the lower bound of
Section A and the certificate of Proposition 1.
Proposition 2 (Partition Threshold). For a query q and any
k distinct stored vectors x1, . . . , xk, the true k-th nearest
distance satisfies rk(q) ≤maxi ∥q −xi∥. Consequently,
screening under min(U0, U1) never excludes a true k near-
est neighbor of q.
Proof. The k-th nearest distance rk(q) is the smallest ra-
dius of a ball around q that contains k stored vectors. The
ball of radius maxi ∥q −xi∥contains the k distinct vectors
x1, . . . , xk, hence rk(q) ≤maxi ∥q −xi∥. The threshold
U0 is the k-th smallest exact distance among distinct live an-
chors, which equals this maximum for the k nearest anchors.
Similarly, U1 equals this maximum for the k nearest of the
ρk distinct candidates. Both thresholds and therefore their
minimum bound rk(q) from above. Every true neighbor x
then satisfies ∥ℓ(q) −ℓ(x)∥≤∥q −x∥≤min(U0, U1),
since the lifted distance never exceeds the true distance. The
rounding certificate of CertEx (Zhao, 2026) keeps every
vector whose lifted distance is at most the threshold, which
includes the true neighbor x.
Proposition 3 (Update Correctness Invariant). For a fixed
orthonormal projection and center, the lifted lower bound
holds for every stored vector. If the maxima of the rounding
certificate cover every stored row, the certified test never
excludes a true neighbor among the live vectors. Hence,
insertions with the existing projection and deletions that
disable rows and anchors preserve exactness.
Proof. The lifted lower bound ∥ℓ(q) −ℓ(x)∥≤∥q −x∥of
CertEx (Zhao, 2026) holds for every orthonormal projection
and every center, as its proof uses only the orthogonal split
of q−c and x−c into the span of V and its complement. An
insertion lifts the new vectors with the existing projection
and center, for which the bound also holds. The rounding
certificate bounds the error of the half-precision product
through the database maxima of the certificate coordinates.
Because an insertion raises these maxima to cover its rows
before any search reads them, the certified test keeps every
live vector within the threshold, as in a static dataset. A
deleted row meets the constant coordinate of every query
with the most negative half-precision value. Its product
thus falls below every threshold, which excludes the row
from every screening. A deleted anchor no longer enters
the threshold, which thus remains the k-th smallest exact
distance among live vectors and bounds the k-th distance
among the live vectors from above by Proposition 2.

C
IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS
C.1
GPU Screening Kernels
On Hopper GPUs (NVIDIA, 2022), every thread block
of the screening kernel of Section 3.1 keeps a tile of 128
query rows in shared memory and walks every G-th tile of
database rows, where G is the number of blocks that share
the query tile. The grid holds at most two resident blocks
per streaming multiprocessor, as one more block would start
a second wave and nearly double the pass time. The Tensor
Memory Accelerator (TMA) loads the row tiles through a
three-stage pipeline, from which warpgroup matrix-multiply
instructions accumulate their half-precision products in sin-
gle precision with the layouts of CUTLASS (NVIDIA,
2025). The reduction over the row runs in one sequential
loop in the block, which gives every product the accumula-
tion order that Proposition 1 assumes.
Each thread of the epilogue collects the survivors of its
accumulator fragment in a bit mask and reserves space for
them in the output segment of its block with one shared-
memory atomic. Blocks thus never contend for a global
counter. For rows of 128 and 256 halves, ZARC runs a warp-
specialized variant, in which a producer warpgroup streams
row tiles with TMA and two or three consumer warpgroups
take the tiles in turn. The epilogue of one consumer then
overlaps the tensor-core work of the others. Rows of 64
halves give each consumer too short a product to hide a
heavy epilogue, and the cooperative kernel therefore serves
them up to 225 rows.
The routed variant takes a list of work items, each of which
pairs a tile of queries with a tile of rows from one contiguous
partition that TMA loads at any row offset. The same routed
kernel screens the label partitions of filtered search. On
Ampere GPUs such as the A100, ZARC runs kernels with
the same epilogue that issue mma.sync instructions and load
tiles with asynchronous copies in place of TMA.
C.2
Verification from Host Memory
For the verification of Section 3.3, the GPU reads the raw
vectors from pinned host memory through unified virtual
addressing, which spares verification any staging of rows on
the device. The sort keys of Section F hold the query index
in their high bits and the order-preserving bit pattern of the
lower bound in their low bits, which a radix sort (Adinets &
Merrill, 2022) orders in one pass. Every temporary buffer
of a batch comes from a stream-ordered allocator, since
an ordinary device free synchronizes the whole GPU and
would stall the pipeline of a batch at every buffer.
In round r of verification, every unfinished query takes its
next 32 · 2r survivors whose lower bounds lie below its
current k-th exact distance. One flat launch computes the
exact distances of all these pairs in double precision, with
eight row reads of each warp in flight. A second launch
merges the distances of each query into its top-k. A query
finishes when its window stops at a lower bound above its
k-th distance or at its last survivor.
C.3
Updates and Interface
For the updates of Section 3.4, a deletion sets the coordinate
of the screening row that meets the constant coordinate of
every query row to the most negative half-precision value,
which pushes the product of the row below every threshold.
The deletion also marks the vector, which then no longer
sets a threshold as an anchor. Searches hold a reader-writer
lock in shared mode for the duration of a batch, while an
update takes it in exclusive mode only while it publishes the
new row count and the certificate maxima.
The interface of ZARC (Table 2) follows the build, extend,
remove, and search calls of cuVS (NVIDIA, 2026). A
search takes a batch of queries, the number k of neighbors,
and optionally one label per query or a bitset filter. The
index ranks the vectors by Euclidean distance, by cosine
similarity, or by inner product. For sparse attention, a key
cache keeps one index per KV head and returns the exact
top-k either in the layout of torch.topk (Paszke et al.,
2019) or as the int32 positions of the top-k transforms of
FlashInfer (Ye et al., 2025). The engine chooses the number
of projected dimensions and the anchor stride with the cost
model of Section 3.5 at build time.
D
PER-PARTITION LOCAL PROJECTIONS
The local bounds of Section 3.2 replace the global lift inside
every partition, which requires the certificate to hold per
partition. Every partition Pj receives its own center cj, its
own orthonormal directions Vj, its own scale αj, and its
own certificate maxima, with which its rows are encoded.
A search lifts every query once per partition with a batched
matrix product. The search then sets the threshold of each
pair of query and partition from the common U(q) with the
scale and maxima of the partition, after which one routed
pass screens every partition with its own query rows.
Proposition 5 (Local Projections). If the rows of every
partition are encoded with the projection and certificate
maxima of that partition, and the queries of the routed pass
for the partition are lifted with the same projection, then
Proposition 1 holds for every query of the routed pass and
every row of the partition.
Proof. The lifted bound of Eq. (1) holds for every orthonor-
mal V and every center, and in particular for (cj, Vj). The
rounding certificate of a pair bounds the error of its product
with the maxima of the rows that the query meets, which are
the maxima of partition Pj. Both premises of Proposition 1


hence hold within each partition, where every pair meets
the same U(q).
The anchors keep rows of the global projection, because the
anchor stage compares every query with every anchor. Vec-
tors added after the build likewise keep the global projection.
The routed pass covers the partitions, and the plain pass the
remaining rows, which subjects every vector to one certified
test. Under a filter, every admitted neighbor still survives,
as the threshold comes from admitted anchors. Verification
then drops the survivors that the filter rejects, after which
the remaining top-k is the exact filtered answer.

F
ORDERED VERIFICATION
Ordered verification (Section 3.3) reads the raw vector of
a survivor only while the survivor can still enter the top k,
which bounds the reads from host memory by the tightness
of the certified lower bounds. A kernel computes λ(q, x)
of Eq. (3) for every survivor and rounds it down to single
precision. The kernel then forms a key that concatenates
the query index with the order-preserving bit pattern of the
bound, with which one radix sort orders the survivors by
query and then by bound. A binary search finds the first
survivor of every query. The implementation runs this veri-
fication in rounds over all queries of a batch (Section C.2).
The k-th distance at the start of a round is never below the
one that a sequential verification would use at the same
survivor. As a result, the rounds verify every survivor that
sequential order verifies and return the same top-k.
Proposition 7 (Ordered Verification). If U(q) ≥rk(q)
and Proposition 1 holds, the verification that stops at the
first survivor whose bound exceeds the current k-th exact
distance returns k vectors of smallest distance.
Proof. Every vector x with ∥q −x∥2 ≤rk(q) satisfies
∥q −x∥2 ≤U(q) and therefore survives by Proposition 1.
Consider the moment when the verification stops with cur-
rent k-th exact distance b. Every unverified survivor has a
bound of at least the bound that triggered the stop, which
exceeds b. Proposition 1 then gives it a distance above b.
As a result, an unverified survivor cannot replace any of the
current k vectors, whose distances are at most b. The current
k vectors are thus k vectors of smallest distance among all
survivors, and hence among all vectors.
Proposition 7 bounds the number of exact distances by the
survivors whose bound lies below the k-th distance at the
start of the last round. Consequently, a query whose answer
settles within its first 32 survivors reads at most 32 raw rows,
while a survivor whose bound lies far above the k-th distance
costs one key in the sort and no read of its vector. This
property explains why the tiered layout of Section 3.3 keeps
its throughput: on BIGANN-500M (Jegou et al., 2011),
verification reads only 699 of the 5,593 survivors per query.
G
MULTI-GPU SHARED THRESHOLD
A dataset that exceeds one GPU splits into contiguous shards
with one index per device, over which a search runs in
two rounds (Section 3.3). In the first round, every shard
computes the threshold Us(q) of every query from its own


anchors. In the second round, every shard searches under
the smallest threshold over all shards and returns the exact
k nearest vectors of its own dataset within that threshold,
which the host merges by exact distance. Since every shard
skips its own threshold stage in the second round, the two
rounds cost little beyond a single-GPU search. Only k
identifier and distance pairs per shard and query cross the
interconnect, which keeps the merge independent of the size
of the shards. ZARC builds the shard indexes in parallel,
with one host thread driving each device, and on four GPUs
the host merges the answers of 10,000 queries in 16.7 ms.
Proposition 8 (Shared Threshold). The merge of the shard
answers under the threshold mins Us(q) returns k vectors
of smallest distance over all shards.
Proof. Each Us(q) is the k-th exact distance of k distinct
vectors of the dataset, hence mins Us(q) ≥rk(q) for the
k-th distance rk(q) over all shards by Proposition 2. Every
vector among the k nearest over all shards therefore lies
within the shared threshold and among the k nearest vectors
of its own shard. Each shard returns its exact k nearest
vectors within the threshold by Proposition 7. The merge of
these sets therefore contains the k nearest vectors.

CONFIGURATION EFFECTS
Measurements of the CertEx engine (Zhao, 2026) at every
evaluated width on 21 dataset configurations reveal three
effects that a static choice cannot capture, which motivate
the tuner of Section 3.5. First, the throughput is unimodal
in m with a flat top. Widths below the best one lose up to
a factor of 5 as the looser bound multiplies the survivors,
while widths above it lose only 5% to 35%. A static rule
that takes the smallest tile-aligned width capturing 95% of
the variance of the database reaches at least 0.87 of the
best throughput and selects the best width on 12 of the 21
configurations. Second, the best width shrinks as the dataset
grows, since the screening pass scales with N(m+5) while
the verification cost per query depends only on the number
of survivors. On BIGANN (Jegou et al., 2011), the width
123 is fastest up to 20M vectors, whereas the width 59
is fastest from 50M vectors on, which no rule based on
the variance alone can predict. Third, the variance of the
database does not describe queries from another distribution.
For CLIP (Radford et al., 2021) text queries against CLIP
image embeddings, the width of the 95% rule admits more
than 2 × 108 survivors per batch of 10,000 queries and
overflows the survivor buffer, whereas the next width is
the fastest. A batch whose survivors exceed the staging
buffer is re-executed for each half of its queries, which
keeps every answer exact even when the survivor count
was underestimated. The re-executed passes keep the shape
of the full batch and give the inactive queries an infinite
threshold, as a new batch shape would re-tune the kernel
during the query and cost several seconds.

FILTERED SEARCH AND LABEL
PARTITIONS
Filtered search admits for every query only the vectors that
a predicate accepts. An exact filtered answer is thus the
exact top-k over these admitted vectors, which both ways
of Section 3.6 return. The first way applies the predicate
only to the pairs that pass the threshold, after the anchor
stage has applied the same predicate to the anchors. The
threshold of every query thus comes from admitted vectors


alone. Proposition 2 and Proposition 7 with the admitted
vectors of each query then give the exact filtered answer, in
which every entry beyond the admitted vectors holds the id
−1 in place of a neighbor.
The second way sorts a copy of the screening rows by label
and maps every row of the copy to the id of its vector. A
search with query labels sorts each batch of queries by label
and routes every tile of queries to the rows of its label. This
routed kernel screens only tiles that pair queries of one
label with rows of the same label and masks the rows of a
loaded tile that lie outside the label. Vectors inserted after
partitioning form a tail, which the first way screens. The
survivors of the tail then join those of the routed pass.
Proposition 9 (Label Partitions). A search with query labels
over a label partition and its tail returns, for every query, k
live vectors of its label of smallest distance.
Proof. The routed pass screens every row of the label of
each query in the partition, while the first way screens every
row of the tail under the label predicate. Every admitted
vector of a query thus meets the certified test exactly once.
The masks also keep no pair of different labels. The thresh-
old comes from live anchors of the label of the query and
bounds the k-th admitted distance by Proposition 2. Since
the survivors map to vector ids before verification, Proposi-
tions 3 and 7 applied to the live vectors of the label of each
query then give the claim.

Datasets.
Table 4 summarizes the datasets of Section 4.1
and the workloads that each serves. The evaluation covers
local image descriptors, visual foundation-model embed-
dings, and web-scale multimodal embeddings. SIFT1M
and BIGANN (Jegou et al., 2011) contain 128-dimensional
SIFT (Lowe, 2004) descriptors under Euclidean distance.
The BIGANN prefixes from 10M to 1B vectors come with
official exact ground truth. DINOv2-1.28M contains 768-

2009) training images from the DINOv2 (Oquab et al., 2024)
ViT-B encoder. DataComp-12.8M contains the 12.79 mil-

image embeddings of the DataComp (Gadre et al., 2023)
small pool, normalized to unit length, on which Euclidean
distance ranks neighbors as cosine similarity does. Filtered
search assigns random labels to BIGANN-10M at 10%,
1%, and 0.1% selectivity. MS MARCO (Nguyen et al.,
2016) contains 8.84 million text passages, which we em-
bed with two models. The Sentence-BERT (Reimers &
Gurevych, 2019) model msmarco-distilbert-cos-v5 yields
unit vectors for cosine retrieval, while TAS-B (Hofst¨atter
et al., 2021) yields unnormalized vectors for inner-product
retrieval. Deep1B (Babenko & Lempitsky, 2016) contains
one billion 96-dimensional float32 image descriptors with
10,000 queries and official ground truth, whose 250M and
500M prefixes take their exact ground truth from a brute
force in double precision.
