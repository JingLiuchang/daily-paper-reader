Abstract
Large language model (LLM) agents achieve
state-of-the-art performance across domains
through chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning. De-
spite their ubiquitous use, little is understood
about the relationship between the externalized
trace and the model’s actual computation. We
address this by investigating how LLM agents
make decisions in iterative games with incom-
plete information. Using a series of mechanis-
tic interpretability techniques, we find that the
model forms and iteratively revises an internal
representation of the game throughout the CoT.
Once the model prematurely commits to an in-
correct belief, it rarely recovers. This failure
is manifested in both the literal trace and the
layer activations. Ultimately, as multi-agent
systems are more prevalent, our work opens
the door to a more focused diagnosis of their
strategic reasoning errors. For illustrative pur-
poses, we offer an interactive visualizer of our
methodology on selected sample traces.1

Introduction
Large language models (LLMs) of today (Ope-
nAI, 2024; DeepSeek-AI et al., 2025; Anthropic,
2025; Yang et al., 2025a) have shown remarkable
abilities as autonomous agents capable of making
complex, high-stakes decisions in a wide range
of domains (Yang et al., 2025b; Hakimov et al.,
2025; Shi et al., 2026). To achieve this state-of-the-
art performance, models are trained to externalize
their thinking and generate immediate reasoning
steps as a long, serial chain of thought (CoT) be-
fore finalizing the answer (Wei et al., 2022). De-
spite widespread adoption, our understanding of
the agents’ thought processes (Kosinski, 2024; Ull-
man, 2023; Das et al., 2024, 2025) and the possible
ways they can fail or be exploited (Abdelnabi et al.,
2024; Duan et al., 2024) is still limited.
1https://interp-self.github.io/gametrace/
Prior work has used game theory as a tool to
investigate how an LLM agent thinks and behaves
under uncertainty in repeated games in which it
must compete or cooperate to achieve its objec-
tives (Akata et al., 2023; Fontana et al., 2024; Guo,
2023; Willis et al., 2025).
However, these tra-
ditional methods have primarily focused on the
LLM’s external behavior (i.e., the final outcome
of a given round), which is insufficient to compre-
hend the strengths and/or weaknesses of its strate-
gic decision-making. We advance this line of re-
search by surfacing the internal mechanisms that
inform the model’s decision-making and identify-
ing areas where they deviate from game-theoretic
rationality.
We present a three-tiered interpretability pipeline
for analyzing intermediate belief formation along
the reasoning trajectory. First, we decompose each
round’s CoT into sentences and at each chunk, use
multiple-choice probes to elicit a verbalized read-
out of belief and action. A common concern with
this type of CoT interpretability work (Lanham
et al., 2023; Bogdan et al., 2025) is that the LLM-
generated trace might not represent the genuine
reasoning process that underpins the model’s pre-
dictions (Kambhampati et al., 2026; Turpin et al.,
2023; Arcuschin et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025).
Taking this criticism seriously, we choose to un-
embed the residual stream at the final layer to ob-
tain the next-token logit distribution at each chunk
boundary rather than reading off the literal output
text. Second, we study the causal dependencies
between sentences by removing each individual
sentence in the CoT and measuring the resulting
shift in downstream logits. This step allows us
to filter away performative sentences that are not
mechanistically relevant to the output. Third, we
look into the model’s intermediate activations to de-
code hidden representations that the LLMs might
leverage during deliberation but never explicitly
regurgitate in the reasoning trace.

Applying these techniques, our research suggests
that LLM agents develop some internal beliefs
about the state of the world, which are then used
to inform their behavior. Such representations are
maintained and updated throughout the CoT as new
information emerges. Although models may omit
some incriminating reasoning in the CoT, such as
fear of exploitation or punishment, these unspoken
concepts are present throughout the activations of
the early and middle layers.
Because the model’s final decision is tightly cou-
pled with these accumulated beliefs (verbalized or
not), the belief trajectory within a trace tends to
lead the action. Thus, reaching the best-response
action generally entails maintaining the correct be-
lief. We also find that the LLM’s irrational behav-
iors are often associated with its premature com-
mitment to a hypothesis, which is a self-reinforcing
process that worsens over time. Together, we see
our work as paving the way for more granular
debugging of misbehavior and reasoning failures,
which will become increasingly important as LLMs
are deployed in complex settings with real-world
consequences.

Experimental Setup
Task.
We choose to study the LLM’s behavior in
a two-player iterated Prisoner’s Dilemma against a
fixed opponent. In this game, both players choose
whether to defect (D) or cooperate (C) at each round
and receive payoffs based on their joint outcome.
The game is a dilemma in that the payoff matrix
T > R > P > S (see Appendix A) incentivizes
each rational, self-interested player to defect, de-
spite both parties winding up worse off than if they
had cooperated (Rapoport and Chammah, 1965;
Axelrod and Hamilton, 1981). However, if the
player is patient enough and prioritizes future util-
ity, mutual cooperation can emerge as the optimal
strategy in a game that is played indefinitely (or,
more practically, for an unknown number of times)
(Friedman, 1971; Fudenberg and Maskin, 1986).
Unfortunately, such an equilibrium relies on trust,
which for both humans (Bó, 2005; Dal Bó and
Fréchette, 2011; Axelrod and Hamilton, 1981) and
LLMs (Akata et al., 2023; Fontana et al., 2024;
Guo, 2023), is difficult to restore after the oppo-
nent’s betrayal.

the Qwen2.5 architecture (Qwen et al., 2024) with
48 layers, hidden dimension 5,120, 40 attention

152,064 tokens. We choose an open-weight model

In each round, we present the LLMs with the
payoff matrix and interaction history from the most

the Appendix (Box 1). To account for the player’s
limited memory capacity, we use a rolling window
of the ten most recent rounds rather than the entire
game history. Each game lasts thirty rounds, but
the LLM is unaware of this; the prompt simply
indicates that there is a good chance the game will
continue to the next round. We provide an example
of a complete game in Figure 1.

3
How does thinking affect
decision-making?
CoTs are useful insofar as the generated traces are
used in the actual reasoning that influences the
corresponding decision (Wei et al., 2022). Ear-
lier work (Lanham et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2025)
has shown that decisions in reasoning models are
consolidated early in the trace, after which the re-
maining text has little causal bearing on the final
response. This discrepancy implies that CoT texts
could be nothing more than an unfaithful, post-hoc
rationalization masquerading as the model’s gen-
uine reasoning.
In this section, we revisit the above assumption
by investigating how the LLM’s internal judgment
and decisions evolve sentence by sentence over
their reasoning traces.
3.1
Methodology
Chunking and asking.
We first split each
round’s CoT into K chunks {c1, . . . , cK} and, for
each index k ∈{0, 1, . . . , K}, prompt the LLM to
verbalize its verdict using only the partial trace
c≤k = c1 ⊕· · · ⊕ck,
(1)
which is concatenated up to the first k sentences
of the full trace. Here, we consider two question
prompts qopp and qact (see Box 2 and Box 3 in the
Appendix) to probe the two main variables of in-
terest: the perceived identity of the opponent (i.e.,
belief) and the decision to cooperate or defect (i.e.,
action). Formatting them as multiple-choice ques-
tions, we can find the next token probabilities for
each answer option by unembeding the residual
stream at the final layer and recording the logits.
We then take the softmax over the single-letter to-
kens to get P(act = D) and P(opp = ALLC). If
the CoT is required to make the final decision, we
can expect these probabilities to fluctuate as the
reasoning chunks accumulate.
Sentence Ablation.
To better understand the (rel-
ative) significance of each sentence in the CoT, we
also investigate how removing the chunk ci from
the original trace affects the subsequent token log-
its. For each downstream chunk cj, we calculate
the KL divergence between two next-token distri-
butions at each index j > i: the baseline trace c≤K
and the masked trace
c−i = c1 ⊕· · · ⊕ci−1 ⊕ci+1 ⊕· · · ⊕cK.
(2)
Averaging over all cj’s token positions yields how
much removing sentence i affects the model’s pre-
dictions across sentence j. Using this measure,
we can connect pairs of sentences with strong de-
pendence and iterate across the full trajectory (see
Appendix B for further details).

3.2
Case Study
To provide intuition, we start with a small-scale
investigation of the LLM’s thinking process in the
middle of a game (round 14) against ALLC. Fig-
ure 2 tracks the two probabilities of interest using
direct question prompts. Initially, the model is bi-
ased toward cooperation and spends the first ten
sentences repeating the game prompt, during which
it has not yet developed a mental model of the op-
ponent. As the LLM attempts to generalize the pat-
terns from the opponent’s history around c11 (“The
opponent has been consistently choosing
Cooperate in almost all rounds...”), we
see P(opp = ALLC) rising above chance, indi-
cating the model’s ability to update their internal
world model in response to new information. More-
over, removing this sentence has a causal effect on
the model’s predictions for subsequent sentences
in which the opponent’s belief eventually settles.
The complete trace from the sentence ablation ex-
periment is provided in the Appendix (Figure 8)
Note that the belief is still fickle at the early stage.
With multiple backtracking traces (”Wait,...”),
the LLM self-corrects its earlier misguided assump-
tion that the opponent cooperates in most but not
all rounds by subsequently performing several in-
dependent readings of the history. Such indeci-
siveness is reflected in the erratic evolution of
P(opp = ALLC) before stabilizing at the mid-
point of the trace around c34 (“So regardless
of my choice,
the opponent has always
Cooperated.“). After the LLM has confidently
determined the opponent’s identity, it takes as few
as five chunks to translate that knowledge into a
decision to defect, causing P(act = D) to abruptly
rise and remain in the defection regime until the
end of the trace.
3.3
Results
The first pass results in Section 3.2 lends credence
to the notion that (a) the CoT can influence the
LLM’s behavior, and (b) the LLM can store internal
representations of the game’s latent structures and
use them to guide the final action. To quantify these
claims, we repeat the aforementioned analysis on
250 independent games (50 for each opponent).
3.3.1
CoT allows for belief and decision
updating.
LLMs form hypotheses and iteratively revise them
in light of new information. Although the prompt
contains all of the necessary information for infer-
ence, their initial guess about the opponent or the
strategy prior to CoT is almost always inadequate.
Thinking out loud generally improves the oppo-
nent’s identification accuracy and the probability
of landing on the optimal action (see Table 2 in the
Appendix).
Anatomy of reasoning.
Applying the sentence
ablation protocol to the entire game population, we
find that most reasoning traces follow the same
qualitative shape. Before making a decision, the
model performs three core computations: (i) re-
viewing the history log, (ii) formulating and con-
firming the opponent’s hypothesis, and (iii) calcu-
lating the expected payoff. The ambiguity with
which the opponent can be identified—that is, the
number of possible policies that yield the same be-
havior as presented in the history log—determines
the order and quantity of these traces.
We present a few interesting patterns of the
model’s reasoning process:
• For easy-to-verify games, such as those
against ALLC and ALLD, the model allocates
around 31% of the total chains to identify-
ing the opponent’s strategy. In games with
more ambiguity, this amount is cut in half,
and more tokens (about 46% and 50% by mid
and late game) are reallocated to backtracking
and reciting the round-by-round history. This
transition shows the LLMs expressing more
uncertainty about their opponent.
• Because reasoning in ambiguous games is
crowded out by history recap sentences, other
computations are deferred late within the
trace, which is exacerbated as more rounds un-
fold. The end result is rash decision-making,
where the LLM retracts an already correct be-
lief. As an example, out of all the TFT rounds
that conclude with the incorrect opponent at
the end of the trace, 94.6% had the correct
answer earlier in the trace but backtracked. A
case study of an individual round is provided
in the Appendix (Figure 7).
• Sentences that express confusion gradually
deteriorate as the rounds progress. Over time,
the model’s confidence increases regardless
of the validity of the belief. This premature
commitment implies that if it converges on
the incorrect representation about the world,
it is unlikely to reassess, inevitably becoming
stuck on the suboptimal move.

Belief leads action within the trace.
The for-
mation of the opponent’s belief generally occurs
before the commitment to cooperate or defect. To
demonstrate this, we treat the belief and action
probe trajectories within each trace as a bivariate
time series and fit a cross-lagged (Granger) model
on their first differences. We compare the stan-
dardized belief-to-action coefficient βba against the
reverse βab.
Our finding shows that βba > βab at every lag
and for every opponent. The standardized gap is
smaller for opponents it finds difficult to determine
(0.084 for TFT, 0.032 for GRIM, and 0.049 for
RANDOM), and it is largest for opponents it con-
sistently pins down (0.168 for ALLC and 0.126
for ALLD). Intuitively, when the model can deter-
mine who it is dealing with, it commits to a belief
about the opponent early in the trace, and the action
follows suit; otherwise, the belief remains noisy,
providing little signal for the action to track. We
also find that the boundary where the model’s read-
out belief probabilities do not deviate from their fi-
nal value occurs at a median 29%, 40%, 67%, 82%,
and 88% of the trace for ALLD, ALLC, RANDOM,
GRIM, and TFT, while actions are always commit-
ted near the end (at a median 98%) of the trace.
3.3.2
Belief shapes action.
The LLM’s final action is correlated with its be-
lief’s content and the earliest point with which it
stabilizes in the trace (i.e., how soon the model
becomes sufficiently confident). Figure 3 depicts
this relationship by dividing each opponent’s round
distributions by the strength of their beliefs and
comparing the resulting P(optimal action) splits.
We observe evidence of a belief-action coupling
across most games, which suggests that accurate
identification of the opponent helps the model reach
the game-theoretically optimal action. However,
identification is not strictly necessary. It is most
important when the best response is to cooper-
ate, and less important when the best response is
to defect, which the model already prefers in the
presence of uncertainty. Against RANDOM, for in-
stance, the model identifies the opponent poorly yet
still plays near-optimally (ID accuracy 0.26 versus
P(optimal) = 0.87 at k=K; Table 2), since con-
stant defection is the uncertainty-robust response.
As expected, the model has high confidence in the
belief of ALLD and ALLC, so it plays D with high
certainty for most rounds. The untriggered GRIM,
by definition, occurs early in the game, when the
LLM has not tried an alternate strategy to gather
information, and thus its prior belief (which is to
play C) does not correlate with the best action. Al-
most half of the TFT rounds end up with the in-
correct model about the opponent, but those with
the correct belief learn to reciprocate and play C
accordingly.
We further illustrate this nuanced dynamic in
Figure 4, which inspects the probed probabilities
P(opp = true) and P(act = D) in the reasoning
trace during (early, mid, late) games against ALLC
and TFT.

stochastically and may commit to the wrong reason-
ing path, particularly when the decisions involved
are complex. Ultimately, the model learns to self-
correct. The true opponent’s identity recovered
by rounds 11–20, resulting in a gradual increase
in P(act=D) throughout the trace. This consoli-

Wrong beliefs diminish performance.
Because
CoT-driven revision does not always lead to the
truth, we can expect worse performance in rounds
with misclassified opponents. We should note, how-
ever, that a low probability of the true type is not
always an inference error. Since each round only
shows a truncated ten-round history, several oppo-
nent types can be observationally equivalent (see
Section 3.3). As reflected in Figure 4, we see the
games against TFT bifurcate into two categories
with time: those where the LLM can identify the
opponent (teal) and those where it cannot (coral).
As mutual defects accumulate in the log, the model
increasingly misinterprets the opponent as ALLD.
This causes P(act = D) to climb past 0.5, leading
to suboptimal retaliation. Remarkably, we find the
same bias towards defection against RANDOM and
GRIM, since the LLM also struggles to identify
the opponent in these situations (see Figure 3 and
Appendix C). However, the bias is only detrimental
against GRIM, where defection ultimately results
in irreversible retaliation. In contrast to RANDOM,
defection happens to coincide with optimal play, so
poor identification of the opponent does not matter.
Looking ahead, our findings point to the need for
human supervision and/or guardrails to mitigate
these systemic vulnerabilities.

Reading the intent beneath the CoT
Our analysis in Section 3 assumes that any infer-
ences drawn by the LLM about the game are exter-
nalized into the CoT. However, it is possible that
the model relies on intermediate ideas that do not
manifest in the reasoning process or are phrased
using a different vocabulary than the question. Ac-
cording to recent research on global workspace the-
ory in LLMs (Gurnee et al., 2026; Chalmers, 2026),
these hidden variables can emerge in the model’s
“workspace” layers during the forward pass and can
be decoded from a linear readout of intermediate
activations.

Results
We now extend the above corpus inspection pro-
tocol to 250 independent games (50 for each op-
ponent). For each round, we divide the generated
trace into 40 equal-fraction bins and record J-lens’
top-20 candidates for each token position. Figure 6
categorizes the most frequent readouts into seven

Five of the seven concept families are surfaced
regardless of the opponent.
Most concepts in
the model’s internal layers take on sufficiently sim-
ilar shapes across games (i.e., they are opponent
invariant; see the first panel of Figure 6). These
surfaced tokens can provide us with an algorithmic
intuition for how the LLM makes decisions in a
repeated normal-form game. With the exception
of RECIPROCITY, which syntactically aligns with
one of the opponent’s archetype names, most of
these readouts do not appear in the actual CoT of
the round.
We find that the LLM first considered the pos-
sibility of being taken advantage of via SELF-AS-
VICTIM in the beginning of the CoT but set aside
this frame as reasoning progressed. In Section 3.3,
we established that the CoT in ambiguous games
are crowded with hedging and history-recap lan-
guage. Such reasoning patterns are consistent with
the emergence of RISK, HEDGE, and eventually

of the trace.

5
Discussion
In this paper, we investigated how CoT reasoning
traces affect the performance of LLM agents play-
ing an iterative Prisoner’s Dilemma. Based on three
levels of mechanistic evidence (logit probes, sen-
tence ablation, and activation-level concept read-
outs), our findings provide a consistent picture of
the LLM’s decision-making process in a repeated
normal-form game. First, the model can retain
and revise valuable latent game information (i.e.,
belief) over time, which aids in the selection of the
final decisions (i.e., action). Second, the belief-
action coupling is shown to be persistent: once its
mental model of the game has stabilized, the LLM
rarely revisits it, which can lead to suboptimal de-
cisions that compound with time. Third, CoT can
be viewed as a partial account of the reasoning
process. Upon examining the activations, we find
that the model sometimes uses internal, nonverbal-
ized concepts to inform its reasoning throughout
the CoT. Because our explanations are based on
convergent lines of evidence, we think of internal
representations as guiding (leading and shaping)
the decision rather than being the sole causal factor.
Limitations
Looking forward, our work suggests concrete re-
search directions. First, we only look at an open-
weight LLM of moderate size, which raises the
question of whether significantly larger models be-
have similarly. A prior survey (Wang et al., 2025)
indicates that scale alone cannot resolve safety fail-
ures, but future research should investigate this
possibility. Second, our study only focuses on
fixed, non-adaptive opponents. To accommodate
the growing number of multiagent systems, our
pipeline can be extended to incorporate LLM-vs-
LLM play. Another intriguing follow-up is to carry
out an intervention experiment in an attempt to
reverse the lock-in behavior, such as ablating the
concept families from the residual stream at the
point in the trace where belief solidifies.

Figure 7 traces the
