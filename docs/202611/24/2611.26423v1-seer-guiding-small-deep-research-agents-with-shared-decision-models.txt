Seer: Guiding Small Deep-Research Agents with Shared Decision Models

1Sichuan University
2University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign

Abstract
Deep-research agents repeatedly decide what to search, read,
and verify, often using large cloud-hosted language models
for action proposal. However, privacy and local control moti-
vate running agents on users’ devices, where limited memory
and compute favor smaller models and complicate on-device
training. Yet inference-time guidance faces three obstacles:
exploring alternatives through real execution adds tool calls,
prompted self-evaluation is bounded by the agent’s judgment,
and model-specific guidance risks repeated adaptation costs.
We introduce Seer, an inference-time framework providing
local guidance that separates the agent’s action proposal
from learned consequence prediction and value estimation.
From a shared corpus of real research trajectories, we train
two models: a tool-response simulator on observed call–
response pairs and a value estimator on histories labeled
with discounted terminal correctness. The simulator and es-
timator are trained offline and reused across agents without
further training. Moreover, Seer executes only the selected
action and adds only real tool responses to the commit-
ted history, avoiding tool calls on discarded candidates at
additional inference cost. Across multiple deep-research
benchmarks, Seer substantially improves agent performance
for different language models. For instance, GAIA accu-
racy rises from 13.6% to 41.7% for Qwen3.5-4B and from
22.3% to 50.5% for Qwen3.5-9B. Furthermore, our analy-
ses suggest that broader candidate coverage can compen-
sate for imperfect simulation, while prediction and scoring
errors limit the benefits of wider imagined search. These
findings highlight the potential of reusable models for pre-
diction and evaluation to strengthen small research agents
through extra local computation without the real execution
of the discarded candidates.
1. Introduction
Deep-research agents use language models to decide what to
search, read, and verify, integrating evidence across sources
to answer complex questions [64]. In practice, OpenAI
Deep Research [43] and Gemini Deep Research [11] offer

multi-step web investigation and source-linked reports, while
open efforts such as DeepResearcher [65] and Tongyi Deep-
Research [53] advance research on sustained information
seeking. Meanwhile, benchmarks from GAIA’s real-world
reasoning and tool-use questions [39] to BrowseComp’s chal-
lenging web information-seeking tasks [58] evaluate agents’
ability to gather and integrate evidence.
For users applying these capabilities to sensitive tasks,
keeping an agent’s reasoning and decision-making on-device
offers greater local control, a motivation reflected in privacy-
oriented personal assistants [2]. However, deploying these
agents on users’ devices requires managing both local com-
putational resources and external tool-use costs. Memory
and compute constraints favor smaller language models [4],
while tool-call fees make repeated searches and page re-
trievals an additional deployment expense [34].
Under these constraints, training small models specifi-
cally for research is a natural way to improve agent perfor-
mance. However, collecting training data and optimizing
each model require additional resources, making repeated
adaptation costly for resource-constrained deployments. In-
stead, inference-time methods offer an alternative by improv-
ing agent decisions without updating the deployed model.
Specifically, output-level refinement [56, 57, 63] selects
or revises completed answers but cannot redirect actions
already executed. Decision-level evaluation [28, 59, 60, 66]
guides intermediate choices through search or learned critics,
but evaluating branches through real execution incurs tool
costs even for discarded candidates. Model-based looka-
head [5, 21, 24] reduces this overhead by evaluating pre-
dicted consequences before execution. To avoid shifting
adaptation costs to supporting components, such guidance
should transfer across agent models without retraining. Yet
approaches relying on the agent’s model for prompted pre-
diction and scoring remain bounded by its capabilities [55].
Figure 1 summarizes these limitations. The challenge is
therefore to provide reusable guidance that strengthens small
agents without model-specific retraining or the real execu-
tion of discarded candidates.
We introduce Seer, an inference-time framework provid-
ing local guidance in deep research. Our key idea is to decou-
ple action proposal from tool-response prediction and value

contributions are threefold.
1. We propose Seer, an inference-time framework that decou-
ples action proposal from shared tool-response prediction
and history-level value estimation across agents. This de-
sign enables reusable guidance for frozen agents without
any model-specific retraining or the real execution of the
discarded candidates.
2. We develop a shared training pipeline that collects real
trajectories across agents and tasks through Monte Carlo
branching on existing datasets. The resulting large-scale
training corpus provides complementary supervision: ob-
served call–response pairs for the simulator and history
prefixes labeled with discounted terminal correctness for
the value estimator.
3. Experiments with different models across extensive
benchmarks demonstrate substantial gains for small
agents, with benefits extending to larger-model settings.
Ablations and analyses reveal the trade-off between can-
didate coverage and imperfect prediction and scoring.
2. Related Work
Research agents and tool use.
Research agents cou-
ple a language model’s parametric knowledge with infor-
mation acquired through tools: WebGPT [41] combines
browser-assisted question answering with human feedback,
ReAct [62] interleaves reasoning with actions, and Tool-
former [48] learns when to invoke APIs from self-supervised
examples. Within this setting, the task determines which
observations matter: an agent must judge which query or
goal-conditioned page visit is likely to reveal useful evi-
dence, which creates two deployment costs—the local com-
pute to run the model and the external tool calls to gather
evidence. Quantization [32], context management [55], and
tool budgeting [34] address deployment costs, while compact
research agents [6, 30, 54] target research capability. Distil-
lation [1, 20] and reinforcement learning [27, 65] strengthen
the agent’s model but require optimization for each adapted
model. Seer instead trains reusable decision models, com-
plementing these efficiency-oriented approaches without up-
dating or retraining the deployed agent.
Inference-time guidance. Guidance can act on com-
pleted outputs or intervene at intermediate decisions, and the
distinction matters because producing a candidate answer
may already consume substantial tool calls. Output-level
methods select or revise solutions: self-consistency [57]
aggregates independently sampled reasoning paths, Self-
Refine [38] alternates generation with feedback, and learned
verifiers [12] score candidates, with asymmetric verifica-
tion [63] allocating a modest verification budget to select
2


among expensive search trajectories. These methods cannot
recover tool calls already spent on discarded trajectories,
as our BoN@4 and AV@4 baselines illustrate. Decision-
level methods intervene earlier: Tree of Thoughts [61] ex-
plores intermediate reasoning states with self-evaluation,
Reflexion [50] uses verbal feedback from earlier attempts,
LATS [66] and web-agent tree search [28] combine search
with environmental feedback, and AgentRM [59] learns
a transferable reward model. Process-supervision evalua-
tors [9, 31, 36, 60] further supply learned ranking signals,
but branches explored through real interaction still incur tool
calls even when discarded, and prompted self-evaluation
depends on the agent’s judgment.
Model-based lookahead. A learned or prompted world
model can instead predict aspects of a candidate’s outcome
before execution. Dyna [52] integrates real experience with
model-based planning, MuZero [49] learns latent dynamics
together with planning-relevant predictions, and Dreamer-
style agents [23, 26] train behavior from imagined rollouts;
for language agents, DreamGym [8] and DynaWeb [14] use
predicted experience to train the agent itself. At inference
time, RAP [24] repurposes an LLM as a world model within
tree search, while WMA [5] and WebDreamer [21] learn
transition predictions for web navigation; SiRA [13] plans
with natural-language belief states, and MCP-Cosmos [18]
couples world models with tool interfaces. However, pre-
dicted experience in these works either trains the agent or
guides a single navigation decision, and avoiding real candi-
date execution does not by itself ensure that both prediction
and evaluation transfer across agent models without retrain-
ing. To enable this reuse, Seer separates call-level response
prediction and history-level evaluation from the agent model,
training the evaluator on discounted terminal correctness
and reusing both checkpoints across agents. It executes only
the selected action and retains real responses as evidence,
trading additional local inference for candidate comparison
without tool calls on discarded candidates.

4. Our Approach
The response approximation in §3 leaves two questions:
what might a candidate action reveal, and how useful is the
resulting history for completing the task? Seer addresses
them with a tool-response simulator Wϕ and a value esti-
mator Vθ supervised by observed responses and discounted
terminal correctness, respectively, while the agent’s frozen
model proposes actions (Figure 2). We refer to these two
components jointly as the decision models, and individually
as the simulator and estimator.

5. Experiments
5.1. Experimental Setup
Training Setup. Frozen agents collect real trajectories by
sampling questions round-robin from DeepDive [35], Miro-
Verse [40], Nemotron RL [42], OpenSeeker [15], and RED-
Searcher [10], excluding evaluation questions. This yields
0.7M call–response pairs for the simulator and 1M training
samples for the estimator. All methods share two exter-
nal tools: search returns titles, links, and snippets, while
visit retrieves pages and extracts goal-relevant evidence.
Extraction in visit is performed by a shared Qwen3.5-
397B-A17B service, so its cost applies equally to all com-
pared methods. Both models are initialized from Qwen3.5-
9B [45] and use LoRA [25] at a learning rate of 10−4, with
simulator/estimator ranks of 64/32 and context limits of
16k/128k tokens. The estimator learns discounted terminal
correctness (γ = 0.95) through a fully trainable HL-Gauss
head [16] with 201 bins over [−1, 1] and a Gaussian standard
deviation of 0.75 bin widths. Both checkpoints are reused
unchanged across agents.
Evaluation Setup. We report final-answer accuracy on
GAIA [39], BrowseComp-200 (BC-200) [58], BrowseComp-
ZH (BC-ZH) [67], SEAL-0 [44], and XBench-DeepSearch
(XBench-DS) [7], following each benchmark’s evaluation
protocol. The main comparison covers Qwen3.5-4B/9B/35B-
A3B [45], Qwen3.6-35B-A3B [46], and Gemma-4-e2b-
it/e4b-it [19]. For each model, all methods share the frozen
agent and tools and use the model’s recommended sam-
pling settings.
Base Agent runs one ReAct-style trajec-
tory [62], whereas BoN@4 and AV@4 run four and select
by normalized-answer majority voting [57] and prompted
outcome verification [63], respectively, without gold-answer
access. We also implement RAP@4 [24], but omit it from
the main table after prompted simulation and self-evaluation
yield little benefit on 4B/9B agents, as illustrated in Table 1.
Each trajectory is limited to 100 committed agent rounds,
128k input tokens, and 9000 s, with a direct-answer attempt
upon reaching a limit. Speculative proposals do not count
toward the round limit. Seer uses depth-one imagined search
with candidate width k ∈{4, 8, 16, 32}, reporting k = 4
and k = 32 as low-width and scaled settings in the main
comparison. These widths are not compute-matched to four
full trajectories, whose real tool calls are all incurred, while
Seer executes only selected actions. In tables, Base, BoN,
and AV abbreviate Base Agent, BoN@4, and AV@4, re-
spectively; Sk denotes Seer with candidate width k, labeled
Seer@k in figures. In all result tables, bold and underlined
values indicate the best and second-best results within each
comparison group, respectively, including ties.

inference rather than execution of discarded candidates. Di-

of eight models with one tie, echoing WebDreamer [21]: an
action’s value hinges on content invisible at decision time,
such as the page behind a search result, which only simulated
responses can reveal before selection.

Scaling Candidate Width: Across all agents and bench-
marks (Table 5), k = 32 falls below the best tested width
in 14 of 40 settings, although it achieves the highest accu-
racy, including ties, on all five benchmarks for Qwen3.5-
4B/9B. Optimal widths depend on both agent and task:
Qwen3.6-35B-A3B peaks at k = 16 on GAIA and k = 8 on
BrowseComp-200, whereas Gemma-4-e2b-it favors k = 32
and k = 4, respectively. Table 6 further probes ranking reli-
ability by comparing the estimator’s action choice with that
of a GPT-5.5 oracle given the ground-truth answer for this di-
agnostic. Agreement generally decreases with k, though not
monotonically on BC-200, consistent with greater sensitivity
to estimator noise when selecting among more candidates.
Together, these results suggest a trade-off between candidate
coverage under simulator noise and ranking reliability under
estimator noise. Wider search offers more opportunities to
find useful actions despite imperfect predictions, without
reducing per-call simulator noise, but also increases the risk
of selecting overvalued branches, potentially compounded
by such errors. This trade-off motivates selecting a specific
candidate width rather than simply maximizing k.
6. Conclusion
We presented Seer, an inference-time framework for local
guidance in deep research that separates agent proposals
8


from learned tool-response prediction and value estimation.
Trained with complementary supervision from a shared tra-
jectory corpus, its simulator and estimator guide different
agent models without retraining, using imagined search but
only real responses as evidence. Experiments show sub-
stantial small-agent gains and transfer across model families
and scales, with tool-call costs close to those of Base Agent
without search guidance in the measured comparisons. We
believe Seer suggests a path toward capable, locally con-
trolled research agents through reusable guidance rather than
model-specific adaptation or costly external exploration.

B. Extended Background and Related Work
We begin with the research-agent setting and its resource constraints, then expand the three directions reviewed in §2 with
additional methods and details not covered in the main text. Efficient research agents address the cost and capability of the
deployed model, while inference-time guidance improves how its proposals are selected. Within inference-time guidance,
model-based lookahead addresses a further question: how to evaluate candidate consequences without executing every
alternative. These directions are complementary, not mutually exclusive.
B.1. Research Agents and Tool Use
Research agents combine a language model’s parametric knowledge with information acquired through tools. WebGPT [41]
combines browser-assisted question answering with human-feedback training, while ReAct [62] interleaves reasoning and
actions so that subsequent decisions incorporate environmental observations. Toolformer [48] learns when and how to invoke
APIs through self-supervised examples. Together, these approaches establish the interaction pattern underlying research agents:
selecting tools, observing their outputs, and using the resulting information to guide subsequent reasoning. InternAgent-
1.5 [17] extends this pattern to scientific discovery by coordinating generation, verification, and evolution across computational
and empirical tasks.
Within this setting, the task determines which observations are useful. WebArena [68] evaluates functional task completion
in interactive websites, whereas the benchmarks used in this study assess answers assembled through search and page retrieval
(§5.1). Research agents must therefore decide which query or goal-conditioned page visit is likely to reveal useful information.
This creates two deployment concerns: the local resources needed to run the model and the external tool calls needed to gather
evidence. The following subsections consider improving the deployed model first, then guiding its decisions without updating
the deployed model.
B.2. Efficient Research Agents
One approach is to reduce the cost of the deployed model or improve its capabilities through training. AWQ [32] reduces
weight storage and inference costs through activation-aware quantization. MiniLLM [20] distills larger language models into
smaller ones, while generalized knowledge distillation [1] uses teacher feedback on student-generated sequences to address
the mismatch between training and inference. These methods make model compression and capability transfer central to
efficient deployment. Beyond language models, STORM [37] reduces visual tokens while preserving spatial structure in visual
state space models, whereas Ferret [69] adapts model partitioning and pipeline execution to changing memory budgets during
online continual learning. Both illustrate how efficiency also depends on the representation and execution of computation,
complementing methods that improve an agent’s decisions.
Reinforcement learning further targets the agent’s tool-use behavior. Search-R1 [27] trains models to interleave reasoning
with retrieval using outcome-based rewards, and DeepResearcher [65] optimizes research agents through real web interactions.
AgentCPM-Explore [6] extends this direction to long-horizon research with an edge-scale model. Alongside reinforcement

B.3. Inference-Time Guidance
Inference-time guidance can act on completed outputs or intervene at intermediate decisions. This distinction matters for
research agents because generating a candidate answer may already require substantial tool use.
Output-level refinement. Learned verifiers [12] score candidate solutions, self-consistency [57] aggregates independently
sampled reasoning paths, and Self-Refine [38] alternates generation with feedback and revision. For deep research, asymmetric
verification [63] allocates a relatively modest verification budget to selecting among expensive search trajectories, while
DeepVerifier [56] uses test-time rubric-guided verification in an iterative refinement loop. These methods can select an existing
answer or inform a subsequent revision, but cannot recover tool calls already spent producing the candidates. For example, the
BoN@4 and AV@4 baselines used in our experiments execute all four trajectories before selecting an answer (§5.1). This cost
motivates guidance before the next real action, rather than only after a trajectory has been completed.
Decision-level evaluation. Tree of Thoughts [61] explores intermediate reasoning states through search and self-evaluation,
while Reflexion [50] uses verbal feedback from earlier attempts to guide later behavior. For interactive agents, LATS [66]
combines tree search, value estimation, and reflection with environmental feedback, and Koh et al. [28] perform search in the
actual web environment.
Learning reusable evaluators. Search determines which alternatives to explore, while evaluation determines which appear
promising. Rather than relying only on prompted self-evaluation, a separately trained evaluator can provide this ranking signal.
Step-level process supervision [31] trains evaluators from intermediate feedback, and OmegaPRM [36] automates process-
supervision collection through Monte Carlo search for mathematical reasoning. AgentPRM [9] uses Monte Carlo reward
targets in an actor–critic framework to train agent policies, rather than restricting guidance to inference time. AgentRM [59]
learns a reward model for test-time guidance that transfers across tasks and policy models, while RAG-Gym [60] studies
transferable search critics for information seeking.
These methods separate the model that proposes actions from the component that evaluates them. However, changing the
evaluator does not by itself remove the cost of obtaining candidate observations. Branches explored through real interaction
still incur tool calls even when discarded. This remaining cost motivates model-based lookahead, which supplies predicted
observations for evaluation before execution.
B.4. Model-Based Lookahead
Model-based lookahead addresses how candidate consequences are obtained. Instead of executing every alternative, an agent
can use a learned or prompted world model to predict aspects of the resulting observation or state. The distinction between
using such predictions for policy learning and for inference-time planning clarifies Seer’s relationship to this literature.
Foundations of world modeling. Dyna [52] integrates real experience with model-based planning, while Ha and
Schmidhuber [22] learn compact representations of environment dynamics and train a controller within a learned model.
MuZero [49] learns latent dynamics together with planning-relevant reward, policy, and value predictions. DreamerV3 [23]
learns behavior from imagined trajectories, and Model-Based Policy Optimization [26] uses short model-generated rollouts
branched from real data to limit the effects of model bias. These approaches establish the use of internal simulation, with
different choices of predictive representation and learning objective.
World models for agent training. Language-based and synthetic environments extend this idea to learning interactive
policies. Dynalang [33] learns a multimodal world model that predicts future text and image representations and trains a
policy from imagined rollouts. DreamGym [8] generates transitions and feedback for agent reinforcement learning, and
DynaWeb [14] combines rollouts in a learned web environment with real expert trajectories. Qwen-AgentWorld [72] learns
language world models across multiple agent environments and studies their use both as simulators for reinforcement learning
and as a foundation for agent training. In these settings, predicted experience supports learning the agent itself.
Language world models for inference-time planning. Predictions can also guide a fixed agent without updating its policy.
RAP [24] repurposes an LLM as both a reasoning agent and a world model, combining predicted transitions with Monte Carlo
tree search and task-dependent rewards. For web navigation, WMA [5] learns transition-focused natural-language descriptions
of changes between observations rather than reproducing full HTML states. WebDreamer [21] combines simulated action
outcomes with value-based selection and trains specialized world models using synthesized interaction data. SiRA [13] uses
natural-language belief states for goal-oriented simulative planning, while MCP-Cosmos [18] integrates world models with
MCP tools to support planning before execution. These methods bring language-based prediction into candidate comparison
rather than requiring real interaction for every alternative.

C. Seer Search Algorithm
Algorithm 1 describes one round of imagined search followed by real execution, as introduced in §4.3. Search begins at
the committed history and explores branches with separate imagined continuations, up to a depth cap dmax. During each
simulation, selection follows Eq. (12), and the estimator scores the endpoint to assess its potential for task completion. This
value is then backed up along the traversed path, updating the mean value of each edge. After search, the action on the most
visited edge from the root is selected for real execution according to Eq. (13).
Note that no gold-answer judgment is queried during search, including for terminal candidates. After a tool-using action is
selected, its actual responses replace the predicted continuation rather than being appended to it. Thus, each subsequent round
starts from a history containing only committed actions and real tool observations.
D. Tool-Response Simulator Implementation Details
The simulator Wϕ approximates the information returned by the shared tools, rather than the full state of the web. Its modeling
unit is an individual call c: a tool name together with its arguments. As in Eq. (6), predictions for a multi-call action are
assembled in the original call order. This factorization makes response prediction independent of the agent architecture while
preserving the format expected by the value estimator.
D.1. Supervision from Observed Tool Responses
The real trajectories collected in §4.2 provide call–response supervision for both search and visit. Search outputs contain
titles, links, and snippets; visit outputs contain page content extracted for the specified retrieval goal. We normalize equivalent
call representations, remove infrastructure exceptions, and deduplicate repeated call–response pairs. Empty results and
inaccessible pages are represented consistently, while substantive returned content is retained. These steps reduce variation
16


caused by the collection infrastructure without filtering examples according to whether the final answer was correct. An
unsuccessful research trajectory can still supply a valid observation of what a tool returns.
The resulting simulator corpus contains 0.7M pairs, as reported in §5.1. Validation is separated by normalized call identity
so that repeated observations of the same call do not appear on both sides of the split. This differs from the estimator’s
question-disjoint split: the simulator learns a call-level conditional distribution, whereas the estimator learns to assess
question-dependent histories.

D.3. Configuration and Use in Search
Following §5.1, we initialize the simulator from Qwen3.5-9B and train it with LoRA rank 64, a learning rate of 10−4, and
a context limit of 16k tokens. Each training example must retain response tokens within this limit, so examples that leave
no tokens for response supervision are excluded. After training, the same checkpoint is reused across agent models without
further updates.
At inference, candidate calls are grouped for simulation, and each predicted response is attached only to its corresponding
imagined branch. Proposals that submit a final answer contain no tool calls and therefore require no simulator output. These
predictions support candidate comparison but do not serve as evidence. Once an action is selected, its tool calls are executed in
the real environment, and only the resulting observations enter the next root history. This procedure allows comparison before
execution while keeping subsequent reasoning grounded in real tool responses.
E. Value Estimator Implementation Details
The estimator Vθ complements call-level prediction by assessing a history in the context of its research question. Its
supervision associates intermediate histories with observed task outcomes and their distance from completion. It provides an
outcome-supervised scalar ranking signal for candidate histories, rather than predicting tool responses or generating critiques.

different frozen models, the learned score reflects this supervision rather than representing a calibrated success probability for
a particular deployment policy. These semantics are preserved when the estimator ranks imagined histories at inference.
E.2. Gaussian-Smoothed Value Classification
We replace the language-modeling head with a fully trainable HL-Gauss classification head [16]. As specified in §5.1, the
head has K = 201 uniform bins over [−1, 1]. Their width is βb −βb−1 = 2/201, and the smoothing standard deviation is
σ = 0.75(2/201). Although the supervision targets lie in [0, 1], the classification support is the wider interval [−1, 1]; the
support should not be confused with the range of observed labels.
For each target, Eq. (10) integrates a Gaussian over the bins and normalizes its mass over the bounded support. This
gives neighboring bins similar supervision for nearby target values, rather than treating two targets on opposite sides of a bin
boundary as unrelated classes. Training minimizes soft-label cross-entropy, and inference returns the expectation of bin centers
under the predicted distribution, as in §4.2. The scalar expectation is the value used for search and backup; no text-generation
prompt or gold-answer comparison is required to obtain it.
E.3. Causal Prefix Training and Shared Deployment
The estimator corpus contains 1M training samples derived from the real trajectories (§5.1). We split training and validation by
question, keeping every trajectory and labeled prefix for the same question together. This prevents closely related continuations
from leaking across the split. Each trajectory is processed in one causal forward pass, with supervision read at the endpoints of
its labeled prefixes. An endpoint representation can attend only to its own history, so later actions and the final submission are
unavailable when scoring an earlier prefix.
Shared-prefix training requires each shorter history to have exactly the same tokens as the corresponding prefix of the full
trajectory. We exclude trajectories beyond the context limit rather than changing the evidence available to earlier prefixes
through truncation. These conditions preserve the intended history–target pairing while avoiding the repeated encoding of
shared context.
The estimator is initialized from Qwen3.5-9B and uses LoRA rank 32, a learning rate of 10−4, and a 128k-token context
limit, together with the fully trainable classification head. Its checkpoint is reused unchanged across agents. At deployment, it
scores the history formed by appending a candidate action and its predicted responses; final-answer candidates are scored
with an empty response. The experiments use depth-one imagined search, so each candidate extends a real root by only one
imagined action before evaluation.
F. Simulator Case Studies: Predicted and Real Tool Responses
We compare predictions from the tool-response simulator Wϕ with the corresponding real tool responses. Unlike the trajectory
comparisons in Appendix G, these cases examine the content supplied to the value estimator, not final-answer accuracy or
realized tool savings. The shared interface has two tools: search returns result lists, and visit retrieves pages and extracts
goal-relevant content (§5.1). Page reading is therefore covered by visit, not a separate read tool, for which no examples
are available here.
The boxes distinguish call arguments, predicted responses, and real responses. Queries and outputs are condensed where
indicated, and descriptive summaries are not verbatim tool logs. Any research context is provided for the reader: Wϕ receives
only the call and its tool-specific instruction, not the enclosing conversation (Appendix D). We examine agreement and
discrepancies in response content rather than treating textual similarity as a measure of factual correctness. These comparisons
provide a qualitative analysis rather than an estimate of average prediction accuracy.

of transient tool failures. The training pipeline excludes infrastructure failures (§4.2), so this diagnostic example should not be read as
evidence that timeout messages were retained as training targets. No downstream reformulation or avoided call is established by the
response pair alone.

Case 1: Turning a retrieved source into an identity check

Setting. BrowseComp-200; Qwen3.5-397B-A17B.
Question. Identify a scenic hiking location formed during the last ice age, with a body-part-related name, a fall from its viewpoint
between 2000 and 2021, and a 2014 search-and-rescue count related to the Grand Canyon’s 2022 count by a factor of 84.5. Labeled

Seer’s committed trajectory: 17 searches. Early searches consider directions including Angels Landing, the Grand Canyon, and

not imply that Seer retains a persistent tree of location hypotheses: at depth one, each real round starts a new imagined search from the
committed history. The example shows that a large frozen agent can also benefit from guidance over its proposals.

G.2. Fewer Real Calls at the Same Answer Quality
Case 5: Submitting once the comparison is supported
Setting. GAIA; Qwen3.5-4B.
Question. How many more encoder blocks or layers does BERT-base have than the encoder in Attention Is All You Need? Labeled
answer: 6.
Seer’s committed trajectory: 2 searches, 3 rounds. One search establishes that BERT-base has 12 encoder layers, and another
establishes that the original Transformer encoder has 6. Seer then submits 12 −6 = 6; the submission is a committed round but not a
real tool call.
Comparison. Base Agent also obtains the two layer counts early, but continues checking arXiv, Wikipedia, Hugging Face, and course
material before returning the same answer. It uses 5 searches and 6 visits over 12 rounds, for 11 real calls in total. Thus, its real-call
count is 5.5× Seer’s despite identical final correctness.
Interpretation. The visible difference is the timing of final submission, not the arithmetic. In Seer, a final-answer action is evaluated
by Vθ with an empty tool response and competes with further tool-using actions (§4.1). This supplies a mechanism for selecting
submission once the history supports an answer, without a separate stopping threshold. The example is consistent with this mechanism,
but does not establish that the estimator is calibrated to evidence sufficiency.

H. Limitations and Future Work
Seer’s scope is cost reduction on the tool-call axis, not context management: it reduces how often external services must be
called, while the interaction histories it reads still grow with every round. This section reports the limitations that follow from
the design and evaluation choices, states why the current work does not cover each of them, and outlines the corresponding
follow-up studies, grouped into five directions: the relation to context compression, model coverage, the question-answering
focus, fixed search computation, and deployment cost accounting.
Relation to context compression. Seer is not a context-management method and does not compete with context
compression or summarization. The two operate on different cost axes: context compression [55] shrinks what the model reads,
whereas Seer reduces how often external services must be called. Seer keeps full committed histories by design, because the
estimator is trained on complete real histories and retaining real responses is what preserves the separation between predicted
content and citable evidence. For the same reason, we did not include a context-compression or summarization baseline in
this work: such a method changes the input distribution that the frozen agent sees, so a controlled comparison would require
either retraining the agent for compressed histories or verifying that a compression module preserves decision quality on every
benchmark, either of which is a study on its own. Combining Seer with context compression on the same tool interface is a
natural direction, as the two would reduce both cost axes at once.
Model coverage. Our evaluation covers Qwen and Gemma, currently among the most widely used open-source model
families, with both decision models initialized from Qwen3.5-9B. The restriction is deliberate rather than incidental: both
decision models are trained once on real trajectories and then shared by every evaluated agent, so extending the study to an
independent family such as DeepSeek or GLM means collecting new training trajectories and retraining both decision models
on that family, a training-scale effort that would break the controlled setup in which every agent is guided by the same decision
models. The Gemma agents already provide first evidence that the guidance itself transfers across families; whether the shared
9B decision models become a bottleneck for stronger agents, and whether the gains persist when the agent is stronger than the
models guiding it, are left to future work.
From question answering to research reports. We focus on question answering because terminal correctness provides a
clear, verifiable training signal and a consistent basis for comparing agents. Correctness alone does not capture the quality
of a full research report, where coverage, citation support, and synthesis also matter. We did not evaluate report-style tasks
in this work because their supervision requires rubrics whose construction is itself an open measurement problem; adopting
one would tie both the estimator’s training signal and the evaluation to a contested metric. Extending Seer with rubric-based
supervision would preserve the separation between proposal, prediction, and evaluation while broadening what the estimator
learns to assess.
Search depth and adaptive allocation of computation. We use fixed candidate widths and a search depth of one to
compare the effect of candidate coverage under a common search procedure; deeper search couples width and depth effects
and would confound this comparison. This controlled setting leaves open how much computation each decision needs, and
the estimator’s discount factor is likewise fixed at γ = 0.95 throughout, with its sensitivity left uncharacterized. We did
not run an adaptive-computation study because such a policy must commit its budget before observing the outcome, which
requires a calibrated uncertainty signal that the current estimator does not provide. Future work could vary candidate width
and simulation effort according to decision uncertainty and the remaining budget, building on adaptive agent scaling [29, 34].
The aim would be to spend more computation where additional candidates are useful and less where they add little, evaluated
under the same resource constraints as the fixed settings studied here.
Deployment cost accounting on local devices. By accounting for model parameters and real tool calls separately, we
study the cost of guidance without tying the comparison to a particular device. These measures do not, however, establish
peak memory use, energy consumption, or latency on actual hardware. We did not take hardware measurements, because
they depend on a specific device profile, and single-device numbers would not transfer across the heterogeneous edge devices
23


this work targets. We instead report a device-independent analytical estimate: each decision proposes k candidates and runs
the simulator and estimator once per candidate, so local inference time scales roughly with k. Quantizing the agent and both
decision models, sharing representations between prediction and evaluation, and tailoring batching to the device could reduce
the added local cost. Evaluating these changes on a fixed device profile would show whether the savings preserve decision
quality and translate into practical deployment benefits. This design exchanges local computation for performance without
agent-side training, and the two decision models share the same 9B base weights with separate LoRA adapters, so the added
memory is dominated by the shared base rather than by the adapters.
Progress along these directions could make reusable guidance practical for a wider range of research tasks and devices,
accelerating access to research assistance while preserving users’ control over their agents’ reasoning and decisions.
