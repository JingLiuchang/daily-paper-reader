ABSTRACT
Personal agents help users complete everyday work across digital applications.
Many standard benchmarks assume that users provide sufficient information to
solve a task. However, agents often encounter incomplete, distracting, or inconsis-
tent information in practice. To systematically study agent inefficiency under such
imperfect information, we introduce Efficient Action (EA)-Bench, which evalu-
ates both tool calls and user interactions in work-related tasks. To our knowledge,
it is the first benchmark to jointly evaluate tool-call efficiency and user-interaction
efficiency under controlled missing, redundant, and conflicting information. We
construct 63 base-task instances adapted from existing work-related benchmarks.
To guide perturbation design and quantify task requirements, we represent work-
flows as directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) and examine node-level dependencies to
identify where information can be withheld, contradicted, or supplemented with
distractors. We use a two-stage validation process combining AI-assisted checks
with expert screening to select 232 conditions from 792 initial candidates. We
organize these tasks into Easy and Hard tracks by task complexity. Experiments
show that imperfect information can reduce accuracy and increase tool-use and
user-interaction costs. More concerning, several models obtain apparently correct
outcomes by guessing user-specific values without confirmation, revealing poten-
tial execution unreliability in current models under imperfect user information.

INTRODUCTION
Language models are increasingly used for software engineering, document preparation, and spread-
sheet analysis (Anthropic, 2026, OpenAI, 2026). As agents, they combine tool operations with di-
alogue to obtain information and complete tasks (Trivedi et al., 2024, Yao et al., 2022). The user
supplies goals and preferences, while the agent accesses records, makes decisions, and changes the
environment. Reliable execution depends on how the agent handles the information available at each
step, including when it needs to ask the user for clarification. However, user requests often leave
essential details unstated. For example, a request such as “Return the shirt I bought last week” may
refer to several purchases. Incorrect actions taken without confirming user intent can cause substan-
tial financial loss. An effective personal agent must therefore efficiently recover missing facts, filter
irrelevant material, and resolve conflicting information before acting.
Existing interactive benchmarks examine aspects of the efficiency and control challenges introduced
by user involvement. τ-bench measures task completion, policy adherence, and reliability across re-
peated trials with simulated users (Yao et al., 2024). In τ 2-bench, both agents and users can operate
tools in a shared environment, making communication and coordination part of task execution (Bar-
res et al., 2025). AppWorld-UL introduces controlled perturbations and user-held knowledge across
multi-app tasks, and reports interaction recall, precision, and cost (Chen et al., 2026). These bench-
marks make evaluation more realistic, but a remaining challenge is to systematically quantify agents’
ability to detect information imperfections and resolve them efficiently.
Our design considers three cases (Figure 1): (1) Missing information. An agent may skip an un-
stated constraint and still produce a result that happens to satisfy it. Although the task appears

complete, this success does not show that the agent recovered the missing fact and may conceal
a mismatch with the user’s intended goal. (2) Redundant information. An agent may repeatedly
invoke tools to inspect files that appear relevant but contribute little to the task. Such unnecessary ex-
ploration increases tool calls and completion time without advancing the user’s goal. (3) Conflicting
information. When available evidence cannot establish which statement is authoritative, the agent
must ask the user for clarification before choosing between incompatible actions. Evaluation should
measure whether it detects the conflict and resolves it with minimal interaction overhead.
EA-Bench evaluates these questions about user experience using quantifiable measures of tool-use
and user-interaction efficiency. First, we adapt work-related scenarios from JobBench, AppWorld-
UL, and WorkBench (Li et al., 2026, Chen et al., 2026, Styles et al., 2024), drawing on tasks already
curated by these benchmarks. Within each task, we represent its workflow as a directed acyclic
graph (DAG) and examine node-level dependencies. We use DAG-guided counterfactual reasoning
to construct candidate missing, redundant, conflicting, and combined conditions while preserving
the business objective.
We use a two-stage validation process combining AI-assisted checks with expert screening to select
232 conditions from 792 initial candidates. The AI-assisted checker examines each candidate against
its public inputs, DAG dependencies, and task requirements. It checks whether omitted facts remain
inferable, whether conflicting statements have a resolvable authority ordering, and whether redun-
dant additions preserve the authoritative facts and required outcome. Counterfactual checks test
whether changing an unresolved fact affects task-relevant decisions. Executable replay and finite-
world checks support consistency and solvability checks where the task protocol permits them.
Human experts then assess whether the introduced omissions, inconsistencies, and distractions plau-
sibly reflect mistakes or communication patterns of real users. They further screen out contrived
perturbations that do not represent realistic user behavior.
The final benchmark retains 63 base-task instances and 169 imperfect variants, together with one
complete-information baseline per task. To separate efficiency from underlying task difficulty, we
organize the instances into Easy and Hard tracks by task complexity. Grounded and ungrounded
scores distinguish task or rubric credit supported by the necessary acquired information from credit
obtained through unsupported assumptions. For efficiency, Et and Ei measure signed differences
between recorded tool calls or information requests and task-specific references within the declared
protocols. We compute these gaps for successful and failed attempts where validated references
exist, and retain actual resource measurements for open-ended Hard tasks. We evaluate nine main-
stream open- and closed-weight model services across all 232 conditions.
Our contributions are:
1. EA-Bench quantifies efficiency under imperfect instructions. To our knowledge, EA-
Bench is the first benchmark to jointly compare recorded tool use and information requests
with explicit, protocol-scoped task references under missing, redundant, and conflicting
instructions.
2


2. EA-Bench provides a reusable construction procedure for imperfect information.
DAG dependencies and counterfactual checks identify task-specific action and information
requirements. A two-stage checker combines AI-assisted validation with expert screening
for perturbation validity and realism.
3. EA-Bench reveals inefficient recovery from imperfect instructions. Across nine eval-
uated services, imperfect variants reduce Easy accuracy by 5.3–24.5 percentage points
relative to their task-matched Full baselines. Moreover, under imperfect variants, 22.7% of
successful Easy outputs receive ungrounded credit.

RELATED WORK
Execution and efficiency evaluation. Since early frameworks such as ReAct linked reasoning with
external actions (Yao et al., 2022), agent evaluation has increasingly focused on effective tool use
and task execution. BFCL evaluates function calling (Patil et al., 2025). WebArena, SWE-bench,
and AppWorld check executed outcomes in realistic environments (Zhou et al., 2024, Jimenez et al.,
2024, Trivedi et al., 2024). Efficiency benchmarks additionally measure the resources consumed
by successful solutions. EffiBench measures code runtime and memory against canonical programs
(Huang et al., 2024), and OSWorld-Human compares computer-use trajectories with human refer-
ences (Abhyankar et al., 2026). AgentBoard tracks intermediate progress, supplies golden actions
for tool subsets, and reports runtime estimates (Ma et al., 2024). These references support differ-
ent notions of necessary execution. They should not all be interpreted as theoretical optima. Joint
accuracy and cost reporting is also motivated by AI Agents That Matter (Kapoor et al., 2024).
Information and user interaction. User interaction is central to clarifying task requirements and
aligning agent behavior with user intent (Subramonyam et al., 2024). τ-bench, τ 2-bench, ToolSand-
box, and UserBench make communication part of agent evaluation (Yao et al., 2024, Barres et al.,
2025, Lu et al., 2025, Qian et al., 2025). AppWorld-UL additionally controls user-held knowledge
and measures coverage of expected questions and interaction burden (Chen et al., 2026). Context
benchmarks examine complementary abilities. CL-bench studies applying supplied knowledge (Dou
et al., 2026), RULER and HELMET test long-context use (Hsieh et al., 2024, Yen et al., 2025), and
IFEval and FollowBench test verifiable instruction constraints (Zhou et al., 2023, Jiang et al., 2024).
Simulated-user results remain conditional on the proxy’s validity (Seshadri et al., 2026). EA-Bench
brings execution references and necessary-information accounting together with separate missing,
redundant, and conflicting-policy variants. Table 1 compares these selected dimensions.

BENCHMARK CONSTRUCTION AND EFFICIENCY MEASURES
4.1
TASK SOURCES AND INSTRUCTION VARIANTS
To preserve the solvability of the original work-related tasks, we adapt work-related scenarios from
three mainstream work-related benchmarks, JobBench, AppWorld-UL, and WorkBench (Li et al.,
2026, Chen et al., 2026, Styles et al., 2024), and construct Missing, Redundant, Conflict, and com-
bined variants through DAG-guided counterfactual analysis.
Furthermore, we use a two-stage checking process to ensure the validity and realism of the perturba-
tions. First, AI-assisted checks assess missing-fact inferability, conflict authority, and preservation
of task requirements and facts under redundancy. We use counterfactual and executable checks to
test validity. Second, human experts screen for plausible user communication. We ultimately retain
232 of 792 candidate conditions across 63 base tasks, comprising 169 imperfect variants and 63 Full
baselines. The task catalog appears in Appendix B.
Task difficulty and scoring.
To separate task complexity from the additional burden of imperfect
information, we divide the base tasks into Easy and Hard tracks. Easy contains 33 tasks and 147
conditions, primarily structured application and office workflows. Hard contains 30 tasks and 85
conditions involving databases, multi-file analysis, and complex artifacts. The division follows task
structure and deliverables, not observed model accuracy.

EFFICIENCY MEASURES AND LOWER BOUNDS
We measure signed tool-call and information-request gaps, Et and Ei, against task-specific refer-
ences.
Information-request efficiency.
Let Q be the number of requested atomic facts and Iref = c(w)
the minimum certificate identifying the required output in the finite checked world w. We define
Ei = Q −Iref.
(5)
We determine Iref from output differences across finite counterfactual worlds. One answer may
make other facts irrelevant to the decision, so this reference need not equal the initial number of
missing or conflicting facts.
Because a single user interaction may request multiple information items, we measure information-
request cost at the item level. To support this accounting, the simulator returns answered fact iden-
tifiers and values in a structured format, while request parsing separately records the queried items.
This distinguishes the requested set Rt from the returned set Ft, allowing us to count total informa-
tion requests as Q = P

Tool-call efficiency.
For the closed-state protocol, we determine K when defining the DAG by
mapping mandatory reading, artifact-generation, and submission operations to nodes Vreq, giving
K = |Vreq|. Since dependency checks enforce every mandatory operation, completing the task
in this protocol requires at least K operations. Given the necessary facts, topological reference
execution attains this bound.
For each attempt, let A count recorded tool calls, including user requests. We retain A for both
successful and failed attempts, so budget-exhausted runs also contribute their actual cost. To dis-
tinguish required work from extra execution cost, we compare A with a reference for the same task
condition. Since necessary facts can be requested together,
Aref = K + 1[Iref > 0],
Et = A −Aref.
(6)
We calculate each gap from the same episode counts used in the actual-cost figures, then average
the paired differences. Et uses all closed-state attempts with an action reference; Ei uses all Easy
attempts with complete request accounting and a finite information reference. A negative gap means
fewer calls or requests were recorded than the reference, which can occur when execution stops
early or information remains unresolved. Since positive and negative gaps can cancel, Figure 5
shows their contributions separately. Hard lacks a validated global reference, so its gaps remain
unavailable while its actual costs are retained.
4.3
MODELS AND CONFIGURATION
We use a custom runner to call model APIs and evaluate nine mainstream open- and closed-weight
model services across all 232 conditions. Instructions, files, and user-facing tool messages are in
English. Each attempt permits at most 100 actions and 16,384 output tokens per model response.
The knowledge-bounded user simulator follows the design of AppWorld-UL (Chen et al., 2026). In
our experiments, the user simulator and artifact judge both use GPT-5.5 at medium effort.

EXPERIMENTAL RESULTS
This section addresses four complementary questions:
1 How does imperfect information af-
fect agents’ task completion?
2 How does imperfect information affect agents’ execution effi-
ciency?
3 How do task difficulty and perturbation severity affect model performance and efficiency?
4 What are the main reasons for model failures under imperfect information?
1 Task completion under imperfect information.
To expose this distinction, we report total
and grounded credits separately. On the one hand, imperfect variants in the Easy track reduce
grounded credit by 5.9–40.4 percentage points and total credit by 5.3–24.5 percentage points rel-
ative to task-matched Full baselines, so information imperfections affect both completion and the
reliability of successful outputs. More notably, 22.7% of task completions in the Easy track ex-
hibit ungrounded credit. Specifically, models submit correct answers without fully reviewing all
task-related dependency files. Although their outputs can pass the outcome checks, the agents still
engage in guessing.
2 Execution efficiency under imperfect information.
To measure requests introduced by im-
perfect conditions, we average the perturbation-column means in Figures 3 and 4 and compare them
with each model’s Full-column mean, including successes and failures. The results show that tool
calls increase by 14.4–71.5% across models on Easy. Information requests under imperfect
instructions increase from approximately 0.0 to 1.4 on the Easy track.
Furthermore, Figure 5 separates Et and Ei by task outcome. Failed attempts have negative mean
Ei for all nine model rows, consistently below their successful counterparts, indicating fewer infor-
mation requests than the reference requires. Mean Et varies across models in both groups, showing
that failure can occur with either additional tool use or fewer operations than the reference.

3 Task difficulty and perturbation severity.
For harder tasks, limits in model capability can
reduce the apparent sensitivity to imperfect instructions. Even under Full instructions, some models
cannot complete any Hard task (Table 3). Because their scores are already near the lower bound,
there is little room for further degradation. A small perturbation penalty can therefore reflect a
performance floor effect. Tool-call cost is also constrained by the action budget. For example,
Gemini 3.5 Flash averages 13.35 actions on the Easy track and 99.28 on the Hard track, close to
the action limit. By contrast, imperfect information can markedly reduce completion on easier tasks
that are otherwise solvable under Full instructions.
Regarding perturbation severity, Figure 6 shows instances of lower grounded scores at higher
perturbation levels for both missing and conflicting information, while mixed conditions further
amplify the score decline. In contrast, redundant information preserves all authoritative facts needed

CONCLUSION
In this paper, we introduce EA-Bench, a benchmark that evaluates how personal agents complete
work-related tasks when user instructions contain imperfect information in the form of missing, re-
dundant, or conflicting facts. To construct effective and realistic perturbations, EA-Bench selects
workflows from existing mainstream work-related benchmarks and abstracts their task dependen-
cies into DAGs. This allows us to identify the information needed for task-relevant decisions and
isolate facts to construct plausible imperfect variants. A two-stage process of AI-assisted checks and
expert screening further selects 232 conditions from 792 candidates across 63 base-task instances.
We divide EA-Bench into Easy and Hard tracks according to the tasks’ inherent difficulty. For
evaluation, we go beyond task success rates by introducing grounded credit to assess whether agents
have reviewed all task-relevant facts, while also measuring the efficiency of tool calls and user in-
teractions.
Our experiments show that imperfect information can substantially reduce task completion and in-
crease action costs by 14.4–71.5% on the Easy track, while increasing the mean number of re-
quested information items from approximately 0.0 to 1.4. More concerning, 22.7% of successful
Easy task completions receive ungrounded credit, indicating that models guess facts to complete
tasks. Overall, EA-Bench provides a useful evaluation setting for improving the efficiency of future
personal agents.
