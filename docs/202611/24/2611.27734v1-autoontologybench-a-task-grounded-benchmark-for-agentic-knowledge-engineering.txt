ABSTRACT
Foundation-model agents are increasingly extending beyond software engineer-
ing (SWE) into autonomous scientific research, where acquiring and integrat-
ing domain knowledge remains a critical challenge. Recent industrial advances
have increasingly adopted knowledge graphs and ontology-based semantic lay-
ers to organize heterogeneous knowledge into reusable representations. We ask:
Can autonomous agents construct ontologies that help them solve real tasks,
and do they offer measurable benefits over alternative knowledge represen-
tations? Existing benchmarks evaluate ontology correctness or agent task suc-
cess in isolation, leaving the value of autonomously acquired knowledge unre-
solved.
We therefore introduce AutoOntologyBench, a task-grounded bench-
mark comprising 19,947 audited tasks grounded in 18 real-world knowledge
sources across 7 domains, with source-native verification.
Spanning Appli-
cation, Repair, Organization, and Construction, AutoOntologyBench compares
nine frontier-model agents under matched knowledge representations. Layered on
retrieval, agent-built ontologies add 6.1 points of task success, yet trail an expert-
derived reference by 13.0, identifying autonomous knowledge acquisition as the
bottleneck to scalable scientific research. Code and resources are available at
https://github.com/VinyehShaw/AutoOntologyBench.

INTRODUCTION
Foundation-model agents have made rapid progress in software engineering, where access to
source code, executable tools, and automated tests enables iterative problem solving and verifica-
tion Jimenez et al. (2024); Yao et al. (2023). These advances have inspired AutoResearch, extending
autonomous experimentation beyond software engineering toward scientific discovery and other
knowledge-intensive domains Karpathy (2026). Comparable reliability has not followed: recent
benchmarks report large gaps in scientific reasoning, experimental exploration, and the execution of
domain-specific research workflows Chen et al. (2025); Jansen et al. (2024); Liu et al. (2026). A
central difficulty lies in the nature of domain knowledge. Unlike software repositories, where task-
relevant information is often directly inspectable and executable, scientific and industrial knowledge
is distributed across heterogeneous databases, literature, technical specifications, and independently
maintained rules. Solving a task may require reconciling inconsistent identifiers, recovering im-
plicit constraints, or integrating relationships across multiple sources. Consequently, extending au-
tonomous research beyond software engineering requires agents not only to reason and act, but also
to acquire, organize, and reuse domain knowledge that is not explicitly available in their immediate
context.
Knowledge graphs and ontologies are a long-standing way to organize such knowledge. Knowledge
graphs represent entities and their relationships, while ontologies make domain concepts, relations,
and constraints explicit, providing a semantic structure for consistent interpretation and reuse Hogan
et al. (2021); Gruber (1993); Studer et al. (1998). Recently, NVIDIA Auto Ontology has illustrated

growing industrial interest in automatically constructing ontology-based semantic layers from en-
terprise metadata, enabling AI agents to access structured domain knowledge NVIDIA (2026a).
Related developments from Palantir and Amazon Web Services (AWS) further reflect the adoption
of ontology-driven knowledge integration and agent interaction Palantir (2026a); Amazon Web Ser-
vices (2026). However, the practical adoption of ontology-based systems does not establish that
general-purpose agents can construct them reliably. More importantly, ontologies are only one way
to support knowledge-intensive agents. Alternative approaches include direct reasoning and tool
interaction Yao et al. (2023), retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) Lewis et al. (2020), and writing
knowledge directly as code or domain-specific rules Wang et al. (2024b). Each involves different
trade-offs in knowledge acquisition, semantic expressiveness, and reuse, and they are not mutually
exclusive: an ontology can organize what retrieval finds rather than replace it. This raises the ques-
tion: Can autonomous agents construct useful ontologies from incomplete evidence, and when does
doing so provide measurable advantages over alternative knowledge representations? Existing on-
tology evaluations primarily measure artifact correctness, consistency, and semantic alignment Li
et al. (2026); Ontology Alignment Evaluation Initiative (2025), whereas agent benchmarks em-
phasize downstream task completion Jimenez et al. (2024); Yao et al. (2024); Yuan et al. (2026).
Neither connects the quality of autonomously acquired knowledge to its contribution to verified task
outcomes, so the value of agent-generated ontologies remains unmeasured.
We introduce AutoOntologyBench, a task-grounded benchmark for evaluating agentic knowledge
engineering through downstream utility, as illustrated in Fig. 1, which follows one task from a
pinned source (1) through the task factory (2), its regime (3), the knowledge representation (4),
and execution (5) to the native verifier (6). Grounded in 18 real-world, versioned knowledge sources
spanning seven domains, AutoOntologyBench comprises 19,947 audited task instances derived from
4,511 base goals and 325 task families across 16 native-verifiable task environments. The bench-
mark encompasses knowledge application, ontology repair, cross-source organization, and ontology
construction, with task outcomes independently evaluated using source-specific native oracles. We
evaluate nine frontier models under matched knowledge representations, measuring native-verified
task success, mapping quality together with its scoring coverage, cost per valid success, and the
effect of controlled semantic interventions. AutoOntologyBench lets us test whether, when, and why
autonomously constructed knowledge improves downstream agent performance, including whether
an agent-built ontology adds value on top of retrieval. Our contributions are threefold:
• A task-grounded benchmark for agent-built ontologies. To our knowledge, AutoOntol-
ogyBench is the first benchmark to evaluate agent-built ontologies through independently
verified downstream task success, moving beyond artifact correctness to measure their ac-
tual utility for autonomous agents.
• Establishing a source-grounded benchmark for agentic knowledge engineering. We
construct AutoOntologyBench from real-world, versioned domain knowledge, comprising
19,947 audited tasks across 16 verifiable environments. Native oracles, each validated by an
admission audit before release, provide reliable evaluation across knowledge application,
repair, organization, and construction.
• Revisiting the value of structured knowledge for autonomous agents. We compare
agent-built ontologies with direct tool use, retrieval, and lane-native DSL artifacts, both
alone and layered on retrieval, across nine frontier models and seven domains. Seman-
tic interventions and per-domain analysis show when structured knowledge improves task
performance and when alternative representations remain preferable.

Knowledge representations for agents.
Agents can keep knowledge in the context window
through tool use Yao et al. (2023); Schick et al. (2023), fetch it at query time Lewis et al. (2020),
or write it down as code Gao et al. (2023); Wang et al. (2024b;a). Our Native DSL baseline keeps
the last idea but restricts it to each source’s declarative language, so the artifact cannot read hid-
den state. Graph-based retrieval builds entity graphs over a corpus Edge et al. (2024); Sarmah
et al. (2024); Peng et al. (2025), and LLM-based ontology learning extracts terms, taxonomies, and
schema-conformant knowledge bases Babaei Giglou et al. (2023); Caufield et al. (2024). These
works score answers against text or score the extracted structure itself. Our RAG + Ontology con-
dition shares GraphRAG’s premise that structure helps retrieval, but judges the ontology only by
whether a native verifier accepts the downstream answer.
Ontologies and knowledge graphs for agents.
Ontologies make concepts, relations, and con-
straints explicit and shareable Gruber (1993); Studer et al. (1998), and knowledge graphs ground
language-model reasoning Pan et al. (2024); Sun et al. (2024). EvoOntology evolves an ontol-
ogy layer for data agents Chong et al. (2026), OaK refines task-oriented ontologies during exe-
cution Zhang et al. (2026), NVIDIA’s Auto Ontology Eval compares schema-only with ontology-
grounded Text-to-SQL NVIDIA (2026b), and commercial platforms deploy ontology agents Mi-
crosoft (2026); Palantir (2026b). None of these isolates a privileged verifier, compares against lane-
native and retrieval baselines on the same tasks, or tests whether a specific semantic fault causes
a specific failure. Artifact measures such as OWL/SHACL validity and OAEI alignment Ontology
Alignment Evaluation Initiative (2025) serve here only as diagnostics.

AUTOONTOLOGYBENCH
AutoOntologyBench turns real, versioned domain knowledge into tasks that each source’s own en-
gine can grade. Figure 1 follows one Gatekeeper task, repairing the owner labels of two Kubernetes
namespaces, through six steps; this section covers steps 1 to 3 (where tasks come from and what
they look like) and Sec. 4 steps 4 to 6 (how an agent is run and scored).
Sources and lanes. Every task starts from a source snapshot, one upstream revision pinned by com-
mit or release (step 1 of Fig. 1). In the figure the snapshot is the Gatekeeper policy library Open
Policy Agent contributors (2026), whose rule that every namespace needs an owner label is written in

Rego, the policy language of the Open Policy Agent (OPA) The Open Policy Agent Authors (2026),
and executed by OPA. AutoOntologyBench uses 18 snapshots in seven domains: Policy (Gate-
keeper, Kubernetes Common Expression Language (CEL) The Kubernetes Authors (2026)), Work-
flow (Camunda Decision Model and Notation (DMN) Camunda contributors (2026), PM4Py Berti
et al. (2019)), Chemistry (Rhea, ChEBI Rhea (2026); EMBL-EBI (2026a), RDKit RDKit contribu-
tors (2026), ChEMBL EMBL-EBI (2026b)), Biology (Gene Ontology Gene Ontology Consortium
(2026), BioModels Malik-Sheriff et al. (2020); Hucka et al. (2003), Reactome Reactome Pathway
Knowledgebase (2026), UniProt The UniProt Consortium (2026)), Clinical (Clinical Quality Lan-
guage (CQL) Clinical Quality Framework contributors (2026), World Health Organization (WHO)
SMART antenatal care guidelines (SMART-ANC) World Health Organization (2021)), Home tasks
(BEHAVIOR-1K, OmniGibson Stanford Vision and Learning Lab (2026); Li et al. (2022)), and Web
APIs (APIs.guru APIs.guru contributors (2026)). A lane connects one or two snapshots, for example
Rhea with ChEBI, to a public task interface and a native verifier. This gives 16 lanes, numbered L01
to L16 in the supplementary material.
Task factory. The factory (step 2) runs three stages per source. A source plugin fetches and pins
the snapshot. A family plugin turns a task family, a reusable template with its verifier branch, into
concrete tasks: it chooses entities and an initial state, computes a reference answer with the native
tools and the private source rules, and splits the result into a public task (goal, initial state, public
evidence, permitted tools, and budgets) and a private oracle OT (the reference answer, rules, and
hidden tests). The agent sees only the public task, which starts unsolved; the answer exists only
inside the factory. Native qualification then keeps a task only if the lane’s native verifier accepts
the reference answer and the oracle can be recomputed from the pinned snapshot (see below). The
native verifier VT (z; OT ) ∈{PASS, FAIL} checks a submitted answer or final state z against OT
by running the lane’s native executor Ew on the pinned snapshots w. Tasks are counted at three
levels. A base goal is one objective inside a family, and a task instance binds a base goal to concrete
entities, initial state, and parameters; instances of one base goal share the goal text but differ in
entities, initial values, and oracle. The 19,947 instances therefore cover 4,511 distinct objectives in
325 families. For example, the Gatekeeper label-repair family has 56 instances spanning 15 base
goals, and the six instances of one of them differ in namespace names and initial labels. An LLM
phrases each goal in natural language, and any new goal structure it proposes is re-instantiated and
checked against its source. Deduplication, solvability, nontriviality, and leakage checks run before
the final split, which groups tasks by source mechanism rather than wording. Where a lane supports
it, budgeted probes let the agent query the native executor.

that has not run counts as not passed. Writing Q for the gates that apply to the candidate’s lane,

Baselines. Direct answers the task directly, with no research phase and no stored artifact. RAG only
retrieves from a fixed index over the same public evidence with pinned chunking, retriever, reranker,
and top-k. Native DSL writes the knowledge in the lane’s own declarative language: Rego or CEL
policies, DMN tables, CQL, the Systems Biology Markup Language (SBML) Hucka et al. (2003),
BDDL, OpenAPI schemas OpenAPI Initiative (2026), SPARQL queries W3C (2026b) for the RDF
lanes, or the closed query DSL of the RDKit lane. Unlike an ontology, which organizes typed
identities, relations, constraints, and provenance behind a stable interface, a Native DSL artifact
carries none of these. Ontology conditions. Ontology only builds O from the public evidence and
probes. The key condition, RAG + Ontology, retrieves evidence as RAG only does and organizes it
into O, so that retrieved facts are linked and constrained within the task; it tests whether a structured
semantic layer adds value on top of retrieval. Index construction and ontology building are both
charged to cost. References. The expert-derived reference installs the curated rule layer from which
each lane’s oracle is derived (ontology and shapes for the RDF lanes, the source’s own rule set
elsewhere) in place of the agent’s artifact; it measures the pipeline with correct knowledge and is a
ceiling, not a competitor. The namespace-only stub keeps the same consumer but installs an empty
ontology that declares only namespaces, which isolates the ontology’s content from its interface.
The stub is the initial submission of Organization and Construction tasks and is not run on Repair,
where every condition starts from the defective artifact. Interventions. Gold, Corrupt, and Repaired
pre-install the expert-derived reference artifact, a copy with one certified defect, or that copy with
the defect fixed. A task is governed if its solution depends on the corrupted element and unaffected
otherwise.

5.3
RESULTS BY TASK REGIME
Under Direct, every model succeeds most often on Repair and least often on Organization (Fig. 3a).
Averaged over models, Repair reaches 56.0%, followed by Construction (43.1%) and Application
(28.3%), while Organization reaches only 21.3%. GPT6-sol leads Application (38.9) and Repair
(67.4), Claude Opus 5.5 leads Organization (36.0), and Gemini 3.5 Flash leads Construction (57.2).
Construction separates the models most (32.2 to 57.2).
5.4
KNOWLEDGE-MAPPING QUALITY
Mapping quality is scored on Common-7, seven mapping families from five lanes (RDKit, Gene
Ontology, BioModels, PM4Py, UniProt) with 362 tasks, where a model writes cross-source corre-
spondences that are compared with curated gold mappings. Under Direct, quality is highly uneven
across families (Fig. 3b). Every model reaches an F1 of at least 0.63 on Gene Ontology aspect an-
notation, and all but the two Qwen models reach 0.67 on RDKit molecule sets, whereas no model
exceeds 0.12 on UniProt Enzyme Commission (EC) to Rhea catalyst mapping or 0.21 on the two
BioModels families. Differences in macro-F1 therefore come mainly from the two PM4Py relabel-
ing families, where Claude Opus 5.5, GPT6-sol, and Gemini 3.5 Flash reach 0.19–0.49 and no other
model exceeds 0.16. GLM 5.3 reaches a macro-F1 of 0.29 but returns a scorable mapping for only
29% of eligible tasks, whereas Qwen 3.8-max parses 99% with a macro-F1 of 0.24, so the two must
be read together.
5.5
KNOWLEDGE REPRESENTATIONS
Averaged over the nine models, TSR rises from 36.0 (Direct) to 38.7 (RAG only), 41.9 (Ontol-
ogy only), and 44.8 (RAG + Ontology) (Tab. 2). The key contrast, RAG + Ontology over RAG
only, is +6.1 points task-weighted and +4.1 with every lane weighted equally (Eq. (6)), and both
lane-clustered intervals exclude zero, whereas Ontology only over RAG only (+3.2 task-weighted)
shrinks to +1.4 with an interval that includes zero once lanes are weighted equally. Native DSL
(39.0) is level with RAG only, and the expert-derived reference (57.8) leaves 13.0 points of head-
room above RAG + Ontology. On the 13,847 non-Repair tasks the namespace-only stub reaches
21.0, below Direct (27.2) and Ontology only (33.7), so the gain comes from what the ontology
contains. The ontology layer raises the cost per valid success from 44.3k to 53.7k tokens, and com-
mitted policy violations keep V-TSR (Eq. (5)) within 0.4 points of TSR in every condition. In a
sample of 200 runs the agent-built ontologies are small, with a median of 3 concepts, 11 relations
and properties, and about 1.0k tokens (see the supplementary material).
9


The gain follows mapping ability, not model size. Every model gains from the ontology layer,
from +3.2 (GLM 5.3) to +7.6 (GPT6-sol) points over RAG only (Fig. 3c). The two models with
the lowest Common-7 coverage under Direct, GLM 5.3 (28.7%) and Kimi k3 (53.6%), gain least,
and for GLM 5.3 an ontology without retrieval adds only +0.2. Qwen 3.7 Flash, last overall but
with 95.0% coverage, gains +7.4, close to the leaders. An agent benefits from an ontology to the
extent that it can write the mappings the ontology stores.
Where the ontology layer pays off. By regime the increment of RAG + Ontology over RAG only
is largest on Organization (24.1 to 35.7, +11.6), where the cross-source mapping the agent writes
is the artifact itself, and smallest on Application (30.9 to 33.8), where the source already states the
rule. By domain it is largest in Chemistry (+11.9) and Biology (+7.1), led by the Rhea–ChEBI
(+14.3), ChEMBL (+11.8), and UniProt (+10.9) lanes, whose tasks join independently curated
identifiers. It is small in Clinical and Workflow (+1.9 each) and slightly negative in Home tasks
(−1.1), Policy (−0.5), and Web APIs (−0.9). Native DSL is the best autonomous condition in
Policy and Web APIs, where the rule is already executable in its own language. In Chemistry RAG
+ Ontology reaches 30.4% against 60.0% for the expert-derived reference, so the open problem there
is acquiring the crosswalk. Per-lane values are in the supplementary material.
5.6
SEMANTIC-ERROR INTERVENTIONS
A paired cohort of 2,400 tasks per model, drawn from Repair and Organization tasks with a curated
rule layer, corrupts one semantic element and then repairs it under a frozen consumer. Over the nine
models, a certified defect costs 23.7 points on the 1,500 governed tasks, whose solution depends
on the corrupted element (Gold 79.1 to Corrupt 55.5) and repair recovers 17.9, whereas the same
corruption moves the 900 unaffected tasks by 0.6 points; only the governed intervals exclude zero,
so the damage is specific to the corrupted element. Damage ranges from 19.6 (Kimi k3) to 27.8
(GPT6-sol) points, and recovery is not ordered by strength: Qwen 3.7 Flash recovers 87% of its loss
and Kimi k3 67%. Details are in the supplementary material.
5.7
LANE HETEROGENEITY AND COST
Per-lane Direct success ranges from near zero to 96 (Fig. 3d). Rhea–ChEBI, ChEMBL, and UniProt
stay below 37% for every model, and Rhea–ChEBI and ChEMBL alone hold 5,000 of the 19,947
tasks, which caps every model’s overall rate. Single models lead individual lanes: Kimi k3 on CQL
(31.5 against at most 16.4 for the rest), DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash on PM4Py, Gemini 3.5 Flash on the
OmniGibson rollouts, and Qwen 3.8-max on APIs.guru (95.3); conversely GLM 5.3 trails on RDKit
(8.7) and DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash on Reactome (57.3).
Under Direct, accuracy and cost per valid success are not aligned (Tab. 1). The three Flash models
are the cheapest per valid success (16.4k–19.2k tokens), and Gemini 3.5 Flash pairs the lowest wall-
clock time (36 s) with the third-highest TSR, whereas GLM 5.3 is both the most expensive (77.9k
tokens) and the slowest (627 s). Claude Opus 5.5 pays 2.7 times the cost of Gemini 3.5 Flash per
success for 7.7 more points (see the supplementary material).
6
CONCLUSION
AutoOntologyBench examines whether agents can turn acquired domain knowledge into reliable
semantic structures that improve downstream task performance. Across nine frontier models, our
results show that agent-built ontologies complement retrieval, improving task success by 6.1 points,
particularly in cross-source organization and multi-source scientific domains. However, the 13.0-
point gap to the expert-derived reference reveals a persistent knowledge acquisition bottleneck, while
controlled semantic corruption confirms that faithful knowledge directly determines downstream
reliability. These findings suggest that the central challenge for autonomous research is not merely
to generate structured knowledge, but to acquire the right knowledge and make it reliably actionable.
AutoOntologyBench provides a task-grounded foundation for evaluating this capability.
Limitations and future work. Our evaluation is bounded by source-native executors: clinical
rule execution does not establish treatment outcomes, and SBML results remain in silico. Regime
and domain effects may reflect source composition, costs are measured in tokens, and ontologies
10


are built per task under fixed budgets. Future work will explore persistent cross-task knowledge,
broader source coverage, and agents explicitly trained for autonomous knowledge acquisition.

verifier, ancestry, and public/private interface. Files, rules, mechanisms, templates, goals, instances,
paraphrases, and runs are counted separately.

REPRESENTATION COMPARISON AND DETAILED RESULTS
Knowledge representations.
Seven conditions share tools, budget, and one frozen downstream
consumer, and all but the namespace-only stub run on every task (Sec. 4.2 of the main paper).
Table 2 of the main paper gives pooled, per-regime, and per-domain task success and cost; Tab. S8
repeats Table 1 of the main paper for each autonomous condition and Tab. S9 every condition per
lane. RAG + Ontology leads every autonomous condition when pooled and in Chemistry, Biology,
and Clinical; Native DSL is best in Policy and Web APIs, where the rule is already executable in
the lane’s own language, Ontology only in Workflow, and RAG only in Home tasks. The gap to the
expert-derived reference is largest in Chemistry (29.6 points), concentrated in the Rhea–ChEBI and
ChEMBL crosswalks. On the 13,847 non-Repair tasks where it is defined, the namespace-only stub
falls below Direct in every regime, so the ontology’s benefit comes from its content rather than from
the consumer interface. Per model, the gain of RAG + Ontology over RAG only ranges from +3.2
to +7.6 points and is smallest for the two models with the lowest Common-7 coverage.

Task success requires native confirmation of the terminal goal, and valid suc-
