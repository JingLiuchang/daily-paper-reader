Abstract—Graph-based indexes enable efficient approximate
nearest neighbor search at scale by linking similar objects.
In applications such as product retrieval and rental listing
search, similarity depends on several components whose relative
importance varies across queries. The challenge is that the index
must select neighbors during construction, but the query weights
that determine which objects are closest are not yet known. A
straightforward approach is to index each component separately.
However, merging the results of these searches can miss the
nearest neighbor under the weighted distance. A joint graph
allows a search to follow links across components, helping to
avoid such misses, but selecting neighbors separately within each
component can leave redundant edges, and keeping component
hierarchies synchronized limits parallel construction. To address
these limitations, we propose the Skyline Joint Graph (SJG).
Although query weights can change the ranking of candidate
neighbors, the dominance relation among them is independent
of those weights. SJG exploits this property by pooling candidates
across components and retaining the Skyline for each node,
thereby enabling efficient approximate nearest neighbor search
under different query weights while supporting parallel graph
construction. Extensive experiments on six real-world datasets
demonstrate that SJG achieves up to 27.9× the query throughput
of baseline methods at 90% Recall@10, while accelerating single-
threaded index construction by up to 201×. Its index requires at
least 36% less space than the smallest baseline index.
I. INTRODUCTION
Similarity search is a fundamental operation in modern
data systems. Recommendation engines, retrieval systems,
and vector databases all need to retrieve objects that rank
highly in similarity to a given query object. Over the past
decade, approximate nearest neighbor search (ANNS) has
made this task tractable at scale, and graph-based indexes such
as HNSW [1], NSG [2], and Vamana [3] are widely used
in large-scale ANNS [4], [5]. By organizing objects into a
proximity graph, they achieve high recall while visiting only
a small fraction of the collection [6]. During construction, each
object first gathers a set of candidate neighbors and then retains
a compact, well-connected subset. Most graph-based ANNS
indexes construct and traverse the graph using the same fixed
distance function.

no longer well described by a single vector. For example,
a rental listing carries a location, a name, a price, a guest
capacity, and a set of amenities; a product carries an image, a

Existing methods address this challenge in different ways.
Composite methods build one graph per component and merge
the results during query processing. Since each graph selects
neighbors using only its own component distance, the query
weights are not needed during construction. During search,
the system queries the component indexes and combines the
retrieved candidates according to the specified weights. Mil-
vus [7], for example, supports this pattern for multiple vector
fields. Uemura et al. [8] use intermediate search results to
select a starting node for the next component graph, reducing
redundant node visits.
However, simply taking the top candidates from each com-
ponent and merging them can miss the nearest neighbor under
the requested weight combination. As illustrated in Fig. 2,
object C lies outside the top two under both location distance
and amenities distance, yet it becomes the nearest neighbor
when the two components receive equal weight. Even if both
component searches return their exact top two objects, their
union excludes C, and weighted reranking cannot recover it.
Cross-graph guidance can reduce such misses when compo-
nents are correlated, but it does not eliminate them in general.
Although longer candidate lists may include such objects,
they also increase the cost of traversal and reranking. Joint
graph indexing takes a different approach, allowing queries to
explore neighbor relationships across components and discover
objects that separate component searches may miss.
The Hierarchical Joint Graph (HJG) [9] implements this
approach by building a hierarchical graph for each component
and synchronizing the levels of corresponding nodes across
these graphs. However, this joint organization introduces two
limitations. First, HJG selects edges separately within each
component, which can leave redundant edges in the joint graph
and increase both index size and search cost. Second, syn-
chronizing the hierarchies creates dependencies among graph
updates, making parallel construction difficult and increasing
construction cost.
Taken together, these limitations reveal two related but dis-
tinct challenges in constructing a joint graph for queries with
unknown weights. The first is how to select effective neighbors
when the relative importance of different components is not
known in advance. The second is how to facilitate parallel
joint graph construction without the dependencies introduced
by synchronized component hierarchies.
Challenge I: How to select effective neighbors without
knowing the query weights
Directly combining neighbors selected within individual
components introduces substantial redundancy, inflating graph
size and search cost. Conventional graph indexes control graph
density through neighbor selection, typically using a fixed
distance function to determine which edges to retain. With
per-query weights, however, the usefulness of a neighbor
may change across different distance combinations. Select-
ing neighbors according to any particular weight assignment
may therefore compromise the graph’s effectiveness for other
queries, while retaining all candidates leads to excessive
redundancy. We thus need a strategy that neither depends on
a fixed weight assignment nor requires maintaining weight-
dependent edge information. To address this challenge, we
propose the Skyline Joint Graph (SJG), which uses weight-
independent Skyline pruning to remove dominated candidates
while preserving promising neighbors across different query
weights.
Challenge II: How to facilitate parallel joint graph con-
struction
Effective neighbor selection is only half of the problem.
The construction process must also avoid costly dependencies
among graph updates. In HJG [9], each node must keep the
same level across all component graphs. During insertion, a
larger neighborhood may promote a node to a higher level,
which in turn requires updates across the graphs. These up-
dates can affect neighboring nodes and trigger further promo-
tions, creating cascading dependencies. As a result, insertions
cannot proceed independently, making parallel construction
difficult and expensive. SJG avoids these dependencies by
separating candidate acquisition from joint neighbor selection.
The component graphs are built independently, and each node
then selects its final neighbors without modifying the adja-
cency of others. This separation enables parallel construction
without hierarchical synchronization or cascading updates.
The main contributions of this paper are summarized as
follows.
• Effective graph indexing with joint neighbor selec-
tion. We propose the Skyline Joint Graph (SJG), which
integrates candidates from multiple component-specific
graphs and performs joint neighbor selection through
Skyline-based pruning. This weight-independent selec-
tion reduces redundant edges and yields a compact graph
that supports efficient weighted ANNS. The resulting ad-
jacency preserves the minimum weighted distance within
each candidate set for every nonnegative weighting.
• Efficient and parallel graph construction. We develop a
two-stage construction strategy that separates component-
specific candidate acquisition from joint neighbor selec-
tion. Candidate graphs are built independently, and each
node selects its final neighbors from its own candidate
set. This design removes hierarchical synchronization and


cascading updates, enabling efficient parallel construc-
tion.
• Extensive experimental evaluation. We evaluate SJG
under seven weight settings on each of six real-world
datasets and examine its scalability up to 7.75 million
objects. SJG outperforms the fastest composite baseline
in 36 of the 42 settings, with a median speedup of 2.05×
and a maximum of 27.9×. It builds up to 201× faster than
HJG and produces graph indexes at least 36% smaller
than all evaluated baselines.

II. RELATED WORK
This section reviews three areas of related work: graph-
based ANNS on single-component data, multi-component ex-
act retrieval, and multi-component approximate retrieval.
A. Graph-Based ANNS on Single-Component Data
Graph-based ANNS indexes represent objects as vertices
and search for near neighbors by following edges toward the
query [10]. A natural starting point is a k-nearest neighbor
graph, in which each object is connected to its closest neigh-
bors. NN-Descent [11] constructs an approximation to this
graph by iteratively comparing objects that occur in one an-
other’s neighborhoods. Such a graph captures local proximity,
but several neighbors of a vertex may lead a search into much
the same region. The choice of edges therefore matters even
when good candidate neighbors are already available.
HNSW [1] organizes its edges across multiple layers. Search
uses the sparse upper layers to move toward the query and
then descends to a denser neighborhood to refine the result.
NSG [2] builds a single-layer graph from an approximate k-
nearest neighbor graph. For each vertex, it gathers candidates
through graph search, prunes redundant edges, and then checks
that every vertex is reachable from the entry point. Vamana [3]
also searches the graph to obtain candidates, but applies a
tunable pruning rule that controls when a selected neighbor
can rule out another candidate. Other work addresses the cost
of building these indexes through candidate edge pruning [12],
graph refinement [13], and merging existing indexes [14].
Graph search can accommodate a variety of distance func-
tions, as illustrated by NMSLIB [15]. In the methods above,
however, the distance function used to select neighbors is
fixed during construction. In multi-component retrieval, two
candidates can exchange their order of proximity to the same
vertex when the query weights change. An edge pruned under
one weighting may therefore lead to a neighbor preferred
under another.
B. Multi-Component Exact Retrieval
Exact retrieval over multi-component data primarily relies
on distance bounds to exclude objects that cannot be query
results. Since different queries may assign different weights
to individual components, indexes must retain component-
specific distance information from which bounds can be de-
rived for the given query weights.
Existing studies have explored different ways to organize
this information. Unified indexes, such as M2-tree [16] and
M3-tree [17], [18], organize multi-component information
within a common structure, with M3-tree retaining component-
specific covering information to support queries under varying
weights. Another approach organizes distance information
through component-specific indexes or reference objects, as
explored by PM-Tree and RR*-Tree [19]. DESIRE [20] uses
a cluster-based index that supports query filtering through clus-
ters and their associated distance information. Beyond single-
machine indexing, OneDB [21] investigates multi-metric sim-
ilarity search in distributed environments.
Unlike these exact methods, which rely on distance bounds
for candidate pruning, graph-based approximate retrieval
guides search through pre-established neighbor relationships.
C. Multi-Component Approximate Retrieval
Existing multi-component approximate methods differ in
how component information is incorporated into candidate
discovery, graph construction, and query traversal.
One approach maintains separate indexes for individual
components and combines their search results. Milvus [7]
supports searching different vector fields independently, fol-
lowed by weighted aggregation and reranking. Since reranking
operates only on returned candidates, this approach may miss
objects that rank poorly within individual components but
are close under the weighted distance. To exploit information
across component searches, Uemura et al. [8] investigate coor-
dinated graph search, reusing previously retrieved candidates
to select entry points for subsequent component searches and
evaluating candidates using the weighted distance.
Component information can also be fused during graph
construction rather than coordinated only during search.
MUST [22] learns modality weights to construct fused rep-
resentations and builds a proximity graph using similarities
between weighted concatenated representations for target-
modality search. Because the graph is constructed using
weighted fused representations, its neighbor selection depends
on the fusion weights rather than being independent of arbi-
trary query weights.
DEG [23] combines greedy Pareto frontier search for can-
didate acquisition with dynamic relative neighborhood graph
(RNG) pruning to construct a weight-aware graph for two-
component vector search. Its pruning mechanism assigns each
edge an active weight interval, allowing queries to traverse
edges according to their weights. However, this interval-based
pruning mechanism relies on the one-dimensional weight
space of two components and cannot be directly extended
to an arbitrary number of components. Moreover, DEG ac-
quires candidates by searching a partially constructed graph
and updates its adjacency during incremental insertion, in-

IV. THE SJG INDEX
A multi-component graph index must select the outgoing
neighbors of every node before the query weights are known.
A future query can use its weights to change how objects are
evaluated and expanded, but it can discover further objects
only through edges established during construction. Our design
therefore pursues two related goals. Each node should have a
neighbor list that serves queries with different weights, and
final neighbor selection should proceed independently across
nodes once candidate relationships have been established.

A. SJG Framework
The Skyline Joint Graph (SJG) organizes neighbor rela-
tionships from different components into a static, single-layer
directed graph G = (O, E). Each object v ∈O is a node.
The edge set E contains a directed edge (v, u) for each final
outgoing neighbor u ∈N(v).
Combining neighbor relationships from different compo-
nents does not, by itself, require synchronized hierarchies. We
therefore examine the effect of the hierarchy on search by flat-
tening a fully constructed HJG [9] while retaining its original
neighbor relationships. The effect on search cost varies across
datasets. Flattening reduces the cost on some datasets and
increases it on others (Section V-C), so the hierarchy does not
provide a consistent search benefit. Maintaining synchronized
levels, however, couples updates across component graphs and
can trigger further updates to neighboring nodes. Taken to-
gether, these observations motivate a single-layer organization
that retains cross-component connections without the cost of
maintaining synchronized hierarchies.
With the single-layer structure in place, construction can
be organized around two tasks. It must first obtain possible
neighbor relationships and then decide which of them should
become final outgoing edges. As illustrated in Fig. 3, can-
didate acquisition obtains approximate neighbor relationships
separately from each component and pools them into a joint
candidate set C(v) for every node v. Joint neighbor selection
then compares each candidate’s distances across all compo-
nents and determines the final adjacency using the Skyline as
its basis. The key is to exploit component-wise dominance,
which allows candidates to be compared without committing
to a predefined weighting. In this way, the component graphs
serve as sources of candidates, whereas queries traverse the
single joint graph formed from the selected neighbors.
B. Candidate Acquisition
The single-layer organization removes the need to synchro-
nize hierarchical levels, but a separate issue remains, namely
how to obtain candidates. A single-layer graph could still be
built incrementally. If objects are inserted one at a time by
searching a partially constructed joint graph, the candidates
available to a new node depend on the adjacency produced
by earlier insertions. For a static dataset, however, candidate
neighbors can be obtained independently from individual
components without relying on a partially constructed joint
graph. This allows us to acquire candidate relationships in
advance and determine each node’s final adjacency without
depending on an evolving joint graph.
The neighbors of individual components can serve queries
with different weight preferences. When all the weight is
assigned to component i, the weighted neighbor ordering
is determined entirely by di. The neighbors under each
component therefore provide candidate connections relevant
to queries that emphasize that component. To obtain these
relationships, SJG uses NN-Descent [11] to construct an
approximate K-nearest-neighbor graph for each component,
yielding a neighbor list Li(v) for object v. Here, K controls
the component neighbor-list size and differs from the number
k of results requested by a query. These component graphs
can be constructed independently because each uses only its
corresponding distance function di.
These neighbor lists, however, do not necessarily capture
every relationship involving v. The K-nearest-neighbor re-
lation need not be symmetric, even when the neighbors are
computed exactly. An object u may select v as a neighbor
without appearing in v’s own list. If we used only Li(v), such a
relationship would be lost before the joint selection stage. SJG
therefore forms the component-specific candidate set Ai(v) in
Eq. (3) from both the forward and reverse neighbors of v.
Ai(v) = Li(v) ∪{u ∈O : v ∈Li(u)}.

C. Skyline-Based Joint Neighbor Selection
Given the joint candidate set C(v), the remaining task is to
decide which candidates should become outgoing neighbors.
Since the query weights are unknown, retaining every candi-
date may seem like a straightforward way to avoid excluding
neighbors preferred by future queries. However, this leaves
more neighbors to examine whenever a node is expanded.
At the other extreme, selecting edges under a predefined
weighted distance would favor one particular combination of
components. We therefore need a selection rule that reduces
unnecessary neighbors without fixing the query’s weighting in
advance.
Although the weights prevent us from fixing a complete
ordering of the candidates, they do not make every com-
parison uncertain. Fig. 4 illustrates the distinction. Changing
the weights switches the nearest candidate from a to f, so
their relative preference cannot be determined in advance. By
contrast, g is no farther from v than h in every component.
Its weighted distance to v therefore cannot exceed that of h,
regardless of how the nonnegative weights are assigned. Thus,
while some comparisons vary with the weights, others remain
valid for all admissible weightings. These weight-independent
comparisons provide a basis for selecting neighbors before the
query weights are known.
To compare two candidates, SJG examines their distances
from v in each component, writing di(v, u) for the distance
between v(i) and u(i).

Scale Invariance. The construction steps described above,
including candidate acquisition, dominance testing, and rank-
based supplementation, rely only on distance orderings within
individual components. A strictly increasing transformation
of the distances in any component therefore preserves the
comparisons used by these steps. Provided that the randomized
choices, processing order, and tie-handling rules remain con-
sistent, the constructed adjacency is unchanged. This invari-
ance applies to graph construction rather than query rankings.
During search, weighted distances are evaluated using the
specified distance functions and weights, so the ranking under
fixed weights may still change when the distance values
change.

E. Query Processing
Once construction is complete, the graph adjacency remains
fixed, while query weights determine how encountered objects
are evaluated and prioritized for expansion. Given a query
(q, w, k), SJG applies the weighted distance defined in Eq. (2)
to each encountered object u. Only components with positive
weights require a distance evaluation.
SJG uses best-first search (Algorithm 2) to explore promis-
ing neighbors while retaining alternative directions of expan-
sion. A min-heap H manages candidates awaiting expansion,
while a max-heap W retains at most ef potential results and
determines the distance threshold for admitting new candi-
dates. The parameter ef ≥k controls search breadth, and a
visited set V avoids repeated evaluations. Search begins from
the fixed entry node v0.
During traversal, the closest candidate in H is expanded,
and its unvisited neighbors are evaluated using Dw. A neigh-
bor is admitted to both heaps if W is not full or its weighted
distance is smaller than the farthest distance in W. Importantly,
removing an object from W does not remove it from H, so it
may still be expanded.
Search terminates when H is empty or the closest candidate
awaiting expansion is farther than the farthest object in a full
W. The k closest objects in W are returned. Throughout this
process, query weights affect candidate evaluation and expan-
sion order, while the graph adjacency remains unchanged.
V. EXPERIMENTS
We first compare SJG with existing methods under different
query weights. We then examine how the hierarchy, candidate
sources, and neighbor selection affect search performance and
how sensitive SJG is to its construction parameters and the
number of requested results. Finally, we compare construc-
tion time and index size across methods and evaluate query
performance and construction cost on larger datasets.
A. Experimental Setup
Datasets. We evaluate SJG on six real-world datasets, with
their statistics summarized in Table II. Corel [34] contains four
visual features, including a 32-dimensional color histogram, a
32-dimensional color layout histogram, 9-dimensional color
moments, and 16-dimensional co-occurrence texture. NUS-
WIDE [35] contains five visual features, including a 64-
dimensional color histogram, a 144-dimensional color cor-
relogram, a 73-dimensional edge direction histogram, 128-
dimensional wavelet texture, and 225-dimensional block-wise
color moments.
For Tiny ImageNet [36], we use the training images and
extract five features: a 256-dimensional HSV color histogram,
9-dimensional color moments, 32-dimensional Gabor texture,
324-dimensional HOG, and 2048-dimensional ResNet-50 [37]
representations. For COCO [38], we use images and captions
from the 2017 training set. Its eight components include four
visual features, CLIP [39] and ResNet-50 image representa-
tions, and MPNet [40] and CLIP text representations. Multiple
captions of each image are encoded and aggregated into one
text representation. The deep representations in Tiny ImageNet
and COCO are L2-normalized.
Food and Airbnb contain heterogeneous components beyond
vectors. Food is derived from Open Food Facts [41] and
contains 40,000 objects, each with five nutritional values per
100 grams, a food category, and a 768-dimensional MPNet
representation of its product name. Airbnb uses listing snap-
shots from 119 locations in Inside Airbnb [42], dated between
September 11, 2025, and June 19, 2026. Each listing contains
its location, name, price, guest capacity, and a 64-dimensional
multi-hot vector over the 64 most frequent amenities.

To evaluate how SJG performs under varying query weights,

receives weight 1/m. We also sample three weight vectors
per dataset from each of two symmetric Dirichlet distributions,
giving seven weight settings per dataset and 42 settings across
six datasets. All seven settings on each dataset use the same
prebuilt index. With α = 1, sampling is uniform over all
nonnegative weight vectors whose entries sum to one. With
α = 0.2, sampled vectors are more likely to concentrate
most of the weight on a few components. We denote the j-th
sampled weight vector by Dir(α)-j. Due to space limitations,
Fig. 5 presents five weight settings on four datasets.

composite baseline in 36 of the 42 settings, achieving a median
speedup of 2.05× and a maximum speedup of 27.9×. Against
HJG, SJG is faster in all 42 settings, with a median speedup
of 21.4×. Under equal weights, SJG is the fastest on all
six datasets. It is 1.6–4.8× faster than the fastest composite
method and 12–28× faster than HJG. The conclusion holds

in 34 of the 40 comparable settings. Under equal weights, the

89.0 and 83.9 QPS.

Figs. 8(a) and 8(b) show that SJG has higher query through-
put than C-HNSW at every scale, although its lead narrows as
the datasets grow. At Recall@10 = 0.90, SJG is 2.3× faster
than C-HNSW on YFCC at 1M–3M and 1.3× faster at 5M.
On Amazon, the speedup decreases from 4.2× at 1M to 1.3×
at 7.75M. At the largest scale, Figs. 8(c) and 8(d) show that
SJG maintains higher QPS than C-HNSW for recall between
0.6 and 0.9.
Notably, on Amazon, the highest recall that SJG reaches
decreases as the dataset grows. With ef = 4096, the recall of
SJG decreases from 0.975 at 1M to 0.923 at 7.75M, and that
of C-HNSW decreases from 0.960 to 0.945. At 5M and above,
SJG does not reach Recall@10 = 0.95 within this budget. On
YFCC, both methods reach a recall above 0.97 at every scale.
Figs. 8(e) and 8(f) show how construction time grows with
dataset size. On YFCC, C-HNSW builds faster at all four
scales. As the dataset grows from 1M to 5M objects, its
construction time rises from 5.2 to 32.2 minutes, while SJG’s
rises from 6.8 to 40.5 minutes. On Amazon, SJG builds faster
at every scale, and its lead grows from about 3 minutes at 1M
objects to 28 minutes at 7.75M.
VI. CONCLUSION
In this paper, we present the Skyline Joint Graph (SJG), a
graph index for multi-component approximate nearest neigh-
bor search with per-query weights. To address the chal-
lenge of selecting neighbors before query weights are known,
SJG pools component-specific candidates and applies weight-
independent Skyline pruning to construct a compact joint
graph. By separating candidate acquisition from joint neighbor
selection, SJG also enables parallel graph construction without
hierarchical synchronization or cascading updates. Extensive
experiments on six real-world datasets demonstrate the ef-
fectiveness of SJG. At Recall@10 = 0.90, SJG outperforms
the fastest composite baseline in 36 of 42 dataset–weight
combinations, achieving a median speedup of 2.05×. Its graph
index is at least 36% smaller than the smallest baseline
index, while its single-threaded construction is up to 201×
faster than HJG. In future work, we will investigate how to
preserve efficient graph navigation while reducing the number
of retained Skyline neighbors.
