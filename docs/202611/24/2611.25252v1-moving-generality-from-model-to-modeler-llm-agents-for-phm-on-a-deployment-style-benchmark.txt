Moving Generality from Model to Modeler:
LLM Agents for PHM on a Deployment-Style
Benchmark
Zhenling Chen, Zhiguo Zeng, and Anne Barros
Abstract—Coding agents built on large language models
(LLMs) now help develop many models published as general
solutions. Should generality instead reside in the agent that builds
a model for each new task? We examine this question in prog-
nostics and health management (PHM), where every model must
work on unseen equipment. Our deployment-style benchmark,
NextUnit-Bench, covers 24 public datasets split into 58 folds that
hold out units or operating conditions; each agent has two hours
to deliver an executable predictor, then run on the hidden units.
First, frontier agents outperform AutoML, which reaches less
than half the best agent’s score, and faithful implementations of
published methods on four datasets, of which only 6 of 30 beat
the strongest agent’s own design; published accuracies, where
reproducible, rest on the papers’ own conventions, and a single
convention, capping the remaining life of test engines, can halve
the reported error. Second, the agents’ advantage is a design
made on each machine’s data; once made, a plain-text plan of a
few hundred words carries most of the gap between two frontier
agents, and given a weaker agent’s plan, the strongest agent falls
to that agent’s level, often to identical predictions. Before touching
the data, however, it matters little whose plan an agent carries
out, and domain toolkits appear to help neither: what sets the
strongest apart is how much it learns from the data. Third, such
designs reach only implementers that can execute them: given
the strongest agent’s designs, a locally deployable open-weight
model delivers half as often, even when they are lightened, and
scores no higher than without them. Generality, we argue, is not
disappearing but moving, from the model to the modeler.
Index Terms—Prognostics and health management, LLM
agents, benchmark, reproducibility, knowledge transfer, deploy-
ment.
I. Introduction
Coding agents built on large language models (LLMs) have
quietly become part of how models are developed in science
and engineering. Researchers and engineers use them to build
models faster, and often better, and then publish the resulting,
seemingly superior general models as papers. This practice
raises two questions. The first is about credit: in such a
development process, how much of the contribution comes
from the human developer, and how much from the agent? The
second is about generality: once the contribution is published
as a general model or algorithm, how much of its performance

together, they point to a more radical question. If a state-of-
the-art agent can already build, for each new task, a model
as good as a human developer’s, and do so faster, should
generality reside in the agent, the modeler, rather than in an
ever-growing stream of narrow, task-specific models?
Prognostics and health management (PHM) is a natural
place to put this question to the test, because every PHM
model eventually meets a machine it has never seen: the next
bearing, an engine that has not yet failed, an air-handling unit
in another building. A general model earns its name only if
it keeps working there. We therefore examine agents the way
models are actually used. An agent receives a task and the data
of a set of machines, builds a model on its own within two
hours, and delivers an executable predictor, which we then run
on machines it has never seen. On 24 public PHM datasets,
we pit frontier and locally deployable agents against faithful
implementations of published methods and against AutoML.
The first result surprised us: the agents win. Published meth-
ods, implemented faithfully, and AutoML pipelines both fall
behind what a general-purpose agent builds for the equipment
at hand; of 30 implementations of 20 published methods on
four datasets, only 6 beat the strongest agent’s own design. If
an agent can out-model the literature on equipment it has only
just met, where does its advantage come from? Is it a better
programmer, or does it make better decisions?
To find out, we write down the design an agent delivered
as a plain-text plan of a few hundred words, with no code,
and hand it to another agent to implement from scratch. The
answer is striking. Given a weaker agent’s plan, the strongest
agent becomes the weaker agent: its scores fall to the weaker
agent’s level, often down to identical predictions, even though
it writes every line of code itself. Given the strongest agent’s
plan, the weaker agent moves toward it, dataset by dataset. The
advantage is a design made for this particular machine, tried
on its data down to the hyperparameters; a published method
is such a design too, only chosen for other machines. Little
of it lies in heavy machinery: stripped of neural networks,
ensembles, and specialized packages, the strongest agent’s
designs keep most of their value. Could the strongest agent
have written those words without the data? Asked to plan each
task from its description, its documentation, and the layout of
its files, without seeing a single value, its plans were worth
only about a point and a half more than the weaker agent’s to
the same implementer, against 6 to 13 points for plans written
on the data, and toolkits of domain knowledge appeared to

help neither. What sets the strongest agent apart is not what it
knows but how much it learns from the data in front of it.
These few hundred words, however, reach only those who
can carry them out. Handed the strongest agent’s plans, a
locally deployable open-weight model delivers a working
predictor only half as often as on its own and ends up no
better than with no plan at all; even lightened, the plans remain
beyond it. Yet what it does deliver is no worse than what it
builds on its own. What a local model lacks is not knowledge,
but the capacity to act on it. And because the value is made
on the data, which in many plants may not leave the site, what
local deployment needs is a capable modeler beside the data,
not more knowledge from outside.
These findings bear on a question that PHM has rarely
asked. Transfer learning and domain adaptation are among the
most popular topics in the field, and generalization receives
a great deal of attention, yet we seldom ask what we really
need it for. Is it to improve accuracy, or to save cost? The
history of the field hints at an answer. PHM moved from signal
processing to machine learning and then to deep learning, and
each step promised to spare experienced engineers the work
of hand-crafting features for every machine. Had there been
countless such engineers, enough to tailor feature extraction to
every machine, would this progression have happened at all?
LLM agents are beginning to make that counterfactual real.
Systems built on LLMs and agents keep getting cheaper, while
their accuracy and capability keep growing, so much so that we
can imagine an agent writing custom models and algorithms
for every individual physical asset. Do we then still need
generalization in the traditional sense? Our results suggest
that generality is not disappearing but moving: from a model
expected to work everywhere to a modeler that builds the
right model for each machine. We make this case through four
contributions:
1) NextUnit-Bench, an open deployment-style benchmark
(https://github.com/JialingRichard/nextunit-bench)
that
evaluates agents the way models are used: built on one set
of machines, delivered as a program, and run on machines
never seen;
2) evidence that, on this benchmark, frontier agents out-
perform AutoML averaged over 24 datasets, that the
strongest of them outperforms faithful implementations
of published methods on average on three of four datasets,
though the best of those methods beat it on two, and that
published accuracies depend on the papers’ evaluation
conventions;
3) plan transplant and plans written without the data, which
show that the agents’ advantage is a design made on each
machine’s data that, once made, fits in a plain-text plan of
a few hundred words and travels between agents able to
execute it; that before touching the data, it matters little
whose plan an agent carries out; and that neither heavy
machinery nor domain toolkits are its source;
4) a diagnosis of locally deployable agents, which struggle
to execute such designs even when they are lightened, yet
build them no worse when they do: capability buys the
probability of completing a design, not its quality.
II. Related Work
A. LLM Agents for Machine-Learning Engineering
LLM agents that build machine-learning pipelines end to
end are now measured on benchmarks drawn from Kaggle
competitions, such as MLE-bench [1], and built on scaffolds that
search over candidate code, such as AIDE [2]. MLE-Dojo [3]
provides interactive environments for training such agents,
TimeSeriesGym [4] extends the setting to time-series tasks, and
recent work finds that, with a strong model, a minimal single-
session coding agent does as well as elaborate scaffolds [5],
which is why we use plain command-line agents. These
benchmarks score predictions on fixed splits of competition-
style data. None of them asks whether a delivered pipeline keeps
working on physical units it has never seen, the question on
which PHM stands or falls.
B. LLMs and Agents in PHM
In PHM, LLMs have so far been evaluated mostly as assistants
rather than as modelers. PHM-Bench [6] scores large models on
domain knowledge, algorithm generation, and task optimization
across PHM tasks; PHMForge [7] and AssetOpsBench [8] have
agents call tools that wrap published algorithms or orchestrate
agents for asset operations; agents have been asked to diagnose
a simulated appliance step by step from its sensors [9]; LLMs
placed above existing diagnostic tools fail to exploit them
consistently [10]; and a multi-agent framework fuses bearing-
domain knowledge into an LLM’s reasoning and selects feature
pipelines by signal characteristics, without updating model
weights [11]. A recent review discusses foundation models for
PHM [12]. In all of these, the models themselves are built
by humans or provided as tools. In ours, the agent builds the
model from scratch, delivers it, and is judged by how it runs on
equipment it has never seen.
C. Reproduction and Evaluation Conventions in PHM
Evaluation conventions are known to shape reported results
in PHM. On CWRU, segments cut from the same recordings end
up in both training and test sets, which inflates accuracy [13], and
remaining-useful-life results on C-MAPSS [14] are customarily
reported against capped labels. Agents have recently been
used to reproduce under-specified methods in machine health
intelligence [15]. We go one step further and let the same agent
implement each published method twice, once under the paper’s
own protocol and once under deployment, which separates the
effect of the convention from that of the implementation.
III. A Deployment-Style Benchmark
Our benchmark, NextUnit-Bench (NextUnit for short), asks
one question of every method: given the data of some machines,
does what you deliver work on machines you have never seen?

B. Protocol
An agent works as an engineer would on a new assignment.
It receives a task description, which states the inputs, the target,
the history that may be used at prediction time, the metric, and
whatever physical information is available, but not the name
of the dataset. It also receives the development units with their
labels, a shell, and a Python environment. It then has two hours
of wall-clock time, its own reasoning included, and may use the
internet as an engineer would, but not to obtain evaluation data,
labels, or existing answers. During development it may submit
its predictor to the evaluator for a check that it runs, which
reports errors such as an empty output but nothing about the
hidden units. At the end it must deliver an executable predictor
with a single entry point. Once the deliverable is sealed, an
evaluator runs it offline on each hidden unit in a fresh process
(Fig. 1), either in streaming mode, where each prediction may
use only the records received so far, or in batch mode. The agent
never sees the hidden labels or the scoring code, and nothing it
reports about itself is taken on trust. A deliverable that does not
run counts as a failure.

small one-dimensional convolutional network on the raw inputs,
neither tuned to the task. With 𝐸the loss of a method and 𝐵

D. Agents and References
We evaluate four frontier agents, models too large to run
on a plant’s own hardware, GPT-6 Astra, Kimi K3, DeepSeek
V4.1-Flash, and GPT-6 Luna, each run through the Codex
command-line agent at its highest reasoning setting, and two
open-weight models small enough for local hardware, Qwen3.6-
35B-A3B and Gemma 4, run through OpenCode. All runs took
place in September and October 2026, through Codex 0.156.1
and OpenCode 1.19.0 (Appendix B); on average, a run of the
strongest agent took 32 minutes of active time and about 2.8
million tokens. As references we use the fixed baseline, two
AutoML systems with and without automatic feature extraction,
and faithful implementations of published methods.
Two independent audits checked the pipeline, one tracing each
result from the paper back to the code and the other following
the data from the raw files to the scores. Both reproduced the
main scores to within 0.01 and the baseline scores to within a
relative 2 × 10−6, confirmed that development and hidden units
do not overlap, and found no leak that changes a conclusion; the
shortcuts they found are reported in Section VII-C.

C. AutoML and Foundation Models Are Not Enough
A second objection is that any automated search would do as
well. It does not. Given the same inputs as the fixed baseline
and a two-hour search target, AutoGluon and FLAML reach 𝑆
of 11.6 and 13.5, closer to the local open-weight agents than
to any frontier one. Their inputs, the baseline’s own, subsample
long recordings and cap the number of training rows, and a
failed fit scores zero, as it does for the agents; scoring a failed
fit as a constant prediction instead raises both by one to two
points. A bank of generic signal features lifts both to 18.7, and
tsfresh features [46] lift FLAML to 28 on seven signal datasets,
where Astra scores 57 on the same folds. AutoML also tends to
overrun: nearly every AutoGluon fit took longer than two hours.
Overruns were allowed and scored; only a fit stopped by the
cluster’s job limit counted as a failure. Generic features close
the gap where the problem is easy, taking CWRU from 0 to
above 90, but not where it is hard: on the hydraulic rig, the IMS
bearings, Paderborn, and SEU, AutoML stays below 15 while
Astra scores between 21 and 100. What the agents add is not
search but judgment about the machine at hand.
Nor does a general model. Time-series foundation models
are the closest PHM has to the general models of language and
vision. We fine-tuned two whose published pretraining corpora


6
contain none of our datasets, MOMENT [47] and Chronos-
2 [48], on every fold, with the same downsampled inputs as
AutoML and one fixed pipeline, the way a general model is
meant to be used; tuning them to each dataset might lift them,
but that is a modeler’s work. They reach 𝑆of 12.5 and 11.2,
level with AutoML without features and far below every frontier

seem to agree: a foundation-model library was installed in their
environment, yet no agent in any of our runs imported one,
although they did use pretrained audio and vision backbones.
The next section asks where the agents’ judgment lives.
V. Where the Agent’s Advantage Lives: Fit, Not Size
Section IV leaves a question open. When an agent’s design
beats published models on a machine it has just met, is the
agent a better programmer, or did it make better choices for that
machine? We answer by transplanting designs from one agent
to another, by having agents design without the data, and by
stripping designs of heavy machinery.
A. Design
After a run, the method an agent delivered on a fold is written
up as a plan by a separate session of GPT-6 Astra, which reads
the delivered code and the commands of the development run
under fixed rules but never the data. A plan has seven short
sections, from task framing and features to model, validation,
and inference, and stays within 900 Chinese characters, about
500 English words. It contains no code and no number fitted to
the data, but it states every choice the agent made, down to the
hyperparameters it selected by trial on the fold’s development
data. A plan is, in effect, a model specification without its
weights: much like the method section of a paper, except that
it was written for one machine (Appendix A). A second agent
receives the plan, read-only, together with the usual task and is
asked to implement it, deviating only where it conflicts with the
task; it is not told whose plan it is. We cross the strongest and
the weakest of the four frontier agents, GPT-6 Astra and GPT-6
Luna, in both directions, so that plans move across the widest
gap; these two carry the later experiments. As a larger and a
smaller model from the same developer, the two likely share
much of their training and style and differ mainly in capability,
which isolates capability for the comparison; Section VI hands
the same plans to a local model. Plan runs use the OpenCode
harness, and their controls are runs without a plan in the same
harness, which score within 1.2 points of the main runs through
Codex (44.2 against 43.0 for Astra, 31.0 against 30.8 for Luna).
B. Fit Travels, Good and Bad
Given Astra’s plans, Luna moves toward Astra (Fig. 4). On
average its score rises from 31.0 to 36.8, closing nearly half of
its gap to Astra, and the pattern across datasets is unmistakable:
on the 18 datasets where Astra was the better modeler, Luna
gains 12.2 points; on the 6 where Astra was worse, Luna loses
13.3, following Astra down.

C. Close Before the Data
Could the strongest agent have written those few hundred
words without the data? We asked each frontier agent to write
a plan for every fold from the task description, the machine’s
documentation, and a card listing the layout of the data (its files,
fields, shapes, and sampling rates) but not a single value. A fresh
session wrote each plan under the same template, with no tools
and no access to files; both agents then carried out every plan
on the data, under the same instructions as before.
Before touching the data, it hardly mattered whose plan an
agent carried out (Table III). Holding the implementer fixed,
Astra scored 29.5 with its own plans written without the data
and 28.0 with Luna’s, and Luna 27.6 with Astra’s and 26.0
with its own: about 1.5 points either way. Second runs give
1.4 for Astra and, against a rerun of Luna’s own plans, 3.8 for
Luna. Plans written on the data were another matter: to the same
implementer, Astra’s were worth 5.8 points more than Luna’s
when Luna carried them out and 12.7 points more when Astra
did. Designing before seeing the data cost Astra 14.7 points
and Luna 5.0; given the same data, the stronger agent made far
more of it. Given to Luna as advice rather than as instructions,
Astra’s plans changed little. A plan written on the data pins the
model down: following Luna’s original plans, Astra reproduced
Luna’s predictions on 16 of 44 folds. A plan written without the
data leaves more to its implementer: two agents carrying out the
same such plan made identical predictions on only 2 to 5 of 41
to 44 folds, although their scores differed by just 2 points.
Knowledge carried the plans furthest where the field knows
the machine. On the 16 best-known datasets, the plans written
without the data came within 8 to 12 points of Astra’s own
designs; on the eight less-known ones, they fell 27 to 30 points
short (15 to 23 without the shortcut sets; Section VII-C).
Knowledge without the data, at about 28, search without
judgment, at 12 to 19 for AutoML, and judgment applied to
the data, at about 44 for the strongest agent, are three different
things. What sets the strongest agent apart is not what it knows
but how much it learns from the data in front of it.

E. Only the Design Moves the Score
If the advantage lies in fit, capability added from outside
should not move it, and it does not. Over the project, we gave
the frontier agents seven toolkits, tried in fifteen agent–toolkit
combinations (Appendix C), each on the datasets it applies

and feature components and validation tools of our own. No
toolkit helped every agent it was given to. On average, they

VI. Only to Implementers That Can Execute Them
Models small enough to run inside a plant are where PHM
agents would be most useful, and they are far behind. Qwen3.6,
running on a single workstation, scores 14.3 in its main run,
against 30.8 for the weakest frontier agent and on a par with
AutoML (Fig. 2); it fails on 17 of 58 runs, almost always by
running out of time. Gemma 4 fails differently: it delivers on 56
of 58 runs, but what it delivers is weak, and its score of 16.5 is
barely higher.
A. Plans Make Them Deliver Less
Handing them the strongest agent’s design does not help.
Given Astra’s plans, Qwen3.6 delivers a working predictor on
only 32% of runs, against 57% without a plan (Fig. 6a), and its
score falls from 12.6, the mean of its four runs without a plan,
to 9.3; two in three of its runs end in a timeout, lost in code,
dependencies, and repair loops. Luna’s simpler plans hurt far
less: Qwen delivers on 53% of runs and scores 14.7 (Fig. 6a).
What matters is the load of a plan, not its author. We index load
by five features of the author’s own implementation: the number
of models, external dependencies, the use of a neural network,
compute-heavy time, and lines of code. On light and middle
plans, Qwen delivers as often on Astra’s as on Luna’s, and on
light plans at least as often as with no plan at all; but Astra’s plans
pile up in the heaviest third, 64 of 116 against 12 of Luna’s, and
there Qwen delivers on only one run in five. Nor does lightening
rescue it. Given the lightened plans of Section V-D, which it
read in almost every run, Qwen still delivered on only 31% of
runs and scored 9.6. The trouble is not hardware either: the drop
persists on folds whose source run used no GPU.
B. Capability Buys the Probability of Completion, Not Its
Quality
Yet what Qwen does deliver is no worse than what it builds on
its own: on folds where it delivers both with and without Astra’s
plans, its scores differ by about one point, and the lightened and
original plans give the same quality. What separates it from the
frontier agents is whether it completes a design, that is, delivers
a working implementation within the two hours, not how well.
Success is also nested: Qwen lands a plan, delivering it and
reaching its author’s result, almost only on folds where a frontier
implementer lands it too (Fig. 6b). Capability, in other words,
buys the probability of completing a design, not the quality of
what is completed.
C. Nothing Else Closes the Gap
Nothing we tried closed the gap of about 18 points between
Qwen3.6’s standard runsandthe weakestfrontieragent (Fig.5b).
Higher-precision weights, from 4-bit to 8-bit to full 16-bit,
moved the score by a few points in directions that did not repeat.
Giving the agent a GPU changed little, because it barely used
one. Doubling the time to four hours cut failures from 23 to 12
of 58 runs, against a run made at the same time, but left the
score where it was. A workflow skill that guides the agent step
by step helped by 6.4 points in one run and not at all in its repeat.

needs is not more knowledge but the capacity to act on the data
in front of it.
VII. Discussion
A. What Counts as a Contribution in the Agent Era
The worry that researchers overfit their designs to the nominal
test sets of benchmarks is decades old [53], [54], [55]. In the
agent era it becomes acute. Picture a researcher who wants better
numbers on a few datasets. With an agent at hand, trying one
more popular module, and then another, costs minutes rather
than weeks; sooner or later some combination improves the
results on the researcher’s own test split, and yet another XXX-
Net is published as a general model. Such a model is overfitted,
not to its training data but to a protocol, and every one of them
makes it harder for the field to see which ideas carry over to
the next machine. Our results show how thin the line can be: a
single, ordinary convention, capping the remaining life of test
engines, can separate published C-MAPSS errors from errors
roughly twice as large on the very same engines (Section IV-B).
To treat an agent as a researcher is to make research itself
the object of study. An agent can be run again, denied the data,
handed another agent’s plan, or given a toolkit, and every choice
it makes leaves a trace; no such experiment can be run on the
researchers whose methods fill our journals. What the agents
did, we therefore read as a magnified picture of the field. They
recognized the famous datasets and knew their literature, yet the
published methods did not travel; they fitted whatever they were
given, the way the data were collected included; and their own
validation could confirm only what their data allowed them to
see. These are not the agents’ failings alone. They are the field’s,
made visible and measurable.
Agents have greatly multiplied what a researcher can do. Yet
researchers, like the agents themselves, have little sense of how
good the things they make really are. This is not a personal
failing but a structural one: a researcher’s own split, like an
agent’s own validation, can only confirm what its data allow it
to see. It is also why benchmarks exist; they supply the self-
knowledge that neither researchers nor agents possess on their
own.
In PHM, the problem is compounded by a scarcity of data
that is easy to overlook. Industrial data appears abundant, yet
the physical units behind it are few. CWRU, still among the
most widely used datasets, records artificially seeded damage
on a handful of bearings, and its leaderboards passed 99%
long ago [13]. Unlike images or text, which humans perceive
on a common scale, a PHM signal means nothing without its
sampling rate, its sensor, and its machine; two waveforms of
the same shape can mean entirely different things. Each dataset
therefore covers a narrow distribution, and no single dataset can
tell how a model will fare in the field. This is also one reason
why the apparent ocean of industrial data has not yet yielded a
foundation model that does for PHM what large language models
did for text. What the field needs is a common benchmark
that spans many distributions: shared, so that differences in
evaluation no longer masquerade as differences in methods, and
diverse, so that generality is actually put to the test. Such a
benchmark can test the generality of a model or, as in this paper,
of a modeler.
What if the domain knowledge built into frontier models
already rivals, or exceeds, that of most human experts? We
did not set out to measure this, but our results point that way.
Before touching the data, the strongest and a weaker frontier
agent designed within a few points of each other (Section V-C),
as if both stood on nearly the same plateau of domain knowledge;
domain toolkits and skills written by people added nothing to
either on average (Fig. 5a); and the agents recognized most
datasets from their documentation alone. Much current work
measures the PHM knowledge of general models or adds to
it [6], [11]. For frontier models, that knowledge may already be
in place, and further skills may no longer help. The plateau has
an edge, however: knowledge carried furthest on well-known
machines and much less far on unfamiliar ones. What is scarce
is not knowledge but what the strongest agent did better than the
rest, learning from the data of one machine, and the means to
tell whether what it learned was right.
Our results echo Sutton’s bitter lesson [56]: in the long run,
methods that leverage computation beat methods built on human
knowledge. Knowledge written in by people did not move the
agents. Toolkits left their scores where they were, published
methods lost to their designs, and plans written from knowledge
alone, without the data, scored about 28. What set the strongest
agent apart was what it found in the data. AutoML is no exception
to the lesson: it searches, but over a menu that people wrote.
At the level of the model, the opposite lesson holds. The no-
free-lunch theorems state that, averaged over all problems, no
optimizer beats another, so any advantage comes from matching
the structure of a problem [57], and designs fitted to each
machine did beat the general ones. The agent is a general method
whose products are specialized ones. Moving generality from
the model to the modeler lets both lessons hold at once.
For researchers, this changes what is worth contributing.
When an agent can produce a new “what”, a new architecture or
a new model, in an afternoon, the contributions that move a field
forward are the “why” and the “how”: why a method works on
one machine and fails on the next, and how to tell the difference
before it is deployed. A gain on one’s own split entertains its
author; an explanation that holds on the next machine moves
the field. Building a model for each machine is not the same
as overfitting it: overfitting tunes a model to the very data on
which it is judged, whereas the designs here were chosen on


10
development units and judged on units no one had seen.

meets exceeds its strength [58]; in item response theory, a person
answers an item correctly when ability exceeds difficulty [59].

C. Fitting the Machine, or the Dataset
One dataset revealed something the score alone could not.
In the AHU dataset, faults were introduced in all air-handling
units on the same schedule, so the timestamp alone predicts the
fault. Most frontier agents found this regularity and used it, and
it travelled from one agent to another inside their plans. Using
it is not cheating: the task allowed it, and no general model
could have known it. It is customization at full strength. But
the regularity belongs to how the data were collected, not to the
machine. Held-out units cannot expose it, because they share the
schedule; a real building would expose it on the first day. When
we ran the sealed predictors again with each unit’s clock shifted
by a random offset, scores of up to 86 fell to about 18 or less,
and the gap between frontier and local agents on this dataset
all but vanished. Nor must the shortcut be sought: plans written
without the data that added the hour of the day and the day of
the week, as building engineers routinely do, scored up to 37 on
this dataset, against at most 16 for plans without them. Two less-
known datasets carried subtler traces of how they were made.
In Petrobras 3W, our benchmark requested predictions only for
the labeled rows of each instance, so where the first request falls
within an instance, together with the instance’s length, identifies
one class; in CARE, the length of the observed window separates
anomalies within a wind farm. Astra’s designs turned both into
features. Agents, like researchers, find and use whatever traces
the making of a dataset leaves behind, and these traces belong to
the benchmark, not to the machines. Without the three datasets,
the same implementer gains about 2.5 points from Astra’s plans
written without the data and 6.1 to 11.6 points from its plans
written on the data, against 1.5 and 5.8 to 12.7 with them; on
the less-known datasets, plans written without the data fall 15
to 23 points short of Astra’s own designs rather than 27 to 30.
No ranking or comparison changes direction.
Generality forces a model to rely on what holds everywhere;
locality lets a modeler use whatever holds where it works. That
freedom is the point of customization, but a correlation can hold
here because of the machine’s environment, as when a building’s
schedule drives its load, or because of how the data were made,
as with the shared fault schedule above. A customized model fits
whatever it is given, the machine and the making of its data alike,
and only knowledge from outside the data can tell the two apart.
We keep these datasets in the benchmark as hidden indicators
of that limit: a high score on them, earned through such traces,
signals a fit to how the data were made that a held-out test cannot
see.
Our own documentation held one shortcut too. For Paderborn,
a table of each bearing’s operating history, keyed by the
identifiers used at deployment, revealed which bearings were
healthy; one of 81 delivered predictors used it, without gain,
and removing Paderborn changes none of our comparisons.
D. Limitations
Our evidence has limits. Most configurations were run once;
aggregate results are stable across complete re-runs, but single
datasets are not, so per-dataset contrasts should be read as
descriptive, and our intervals reflect variation across datasets,
not across runs. The datasets are public and may have been
seen during pretraining: the agents were not told which dataset
they faced, but they recognize most of them from the task
documentation, and the reasoning of two of them cannot be
inspected, so memorization cannot be ruled out entirely. That
the published methods were implemented faithfully rests on
a single LLM judge. Agents could also use the internet during
development, as a practitioner would: public documentation, pa-
pers, code, and pretrained models were allowed, while obtaining
evaluation data, labels, or existing answers was forbidden but
not technically blocked. Scanning the commands and web-tool
calls of every development run reported here, we found one
violation: Kimi looked up the duration of a hidden IMS test
and hard-coded a wrong value, which lowered its score on that
fold. A few searches for the benchmark’s own interface and
error messages returned nothing; all other network use installed
packages, downloaded generic pretrained weights, or consulted
documentation, code, and public descriptions of the datasets,


11
and none retrieved evaluation data or labels. The scan sees only
addresses written in commands or printed in their outputs. The
agents ran in their usual harnesses, Codex for the frontier agents’
main runs and OpenCode for the local agents and the plan runs,
so what we compare are agents as deployed, not bare models.
The local models in the main experiment ran on CPUs only,
although runs with GPUs changed little. Finally, our plans are
specifications written after the agent’s own trial and error, within
900 characters; they transfer a design chosen on the data. The
plans were written in Chinese, which all implementers handle,
and all transplanted plans, Luna’s included, were written up
from the delivered code by a session of Astra; if anything, this
should favor Astra as an implementer, yet given Luna’s plans,
Astra fell to Luna’s level. The plans written without the data were
run days to weeks after the runs they are compared with, and
Luna wrote each of its plans in about a minute and a half where
Astra took about ten; if anything, this should favor Astra’s plans,
yet Astra’s plans led by only 3.5 to 6.2 points. Whether plans
travel as well between frontier models from different developers
we have not tested, and the closeness of the two agents before
touching the data may partly reflect their shared origin. Above
all, our setting assumes labeled data from other units of the same
kind of machine. When a new type of machine arrives with
no labels at all, the cold start that motivates much of transfer
learning, there is nothing to fit, and whether a modeler can still
help there remains open; our plans written without the data show
only what knowledge contributes before any data is seen, more
on well-known machines and less on unfamiliar ones.
E. Future Work
Three directions follow. The first is planning without data for
local implementers. With frontier implementers, a plan written
without the data was worth about what a weaker agent achieves
on its own; whether such a plan helps a local model on site, in the
setting that motivates it, is open, and so is how far newer local
models or longer budgets move the threshold that stops them.
The second is reuse across tasks: whether the decisions written
for one machine help on another, which would turn a modeler’s
experience into something like a foundation and offer a route
toward the cold start. The third is self-knowledge: whether an
agent can tell a good plan from a bad one, or its best attempt
from the rest, without a benchmark to tell it. A laboratory test
rig whose data have never been published would offer a lockbox
test for all three.
VIII. Conclusion
We asked whether, in the age of agents, generality should
reside in the model or in the modeler. Evaluated the way models
are deployed, general-purpose agents outperformed AutoML
averaged over 24 PHM datasets and, for the strongest of them,
faithful implementations of published methods on average on
three of four datasets. Their advantage lay less in the size of
their models than in fit: decisions made on each machine’s data
that fit in a plain-text plan of a few hundred words, decisions
that travel to any implementer able to carry them out, but not
yet to the models that fit on local hardware. Before touching the
data, it mattered little whose plan an agent carried out; what set
the strongest apart was how much it learned from the data in
front of it. PHM, we believe, is moving from publishing models
to producing decisions. The evolution of its methods has not
reached an end; it has turned a corner. Its next step returns to
building a model for every machine, only now the builder is a
general modeler. One route to generality, in other words, is not to
build a general model, but an agent that adapts methods to each
setting. In studying agents as researchers, we found ourselves
studying research itself.
Appendix A
An Example Plan
Plans were written in Chinese, within 900 characters; we
translate one here. It describes the method Astra delivered on
the second fold of PHM 2010, where the task is to track the
flank wear of a milling cutter cut by cut. The plan states every
decision and every hyperparameter Astra selected, but no fitted
coefficient: the implementer must write the code and fit the
models itself. Given this plan, Luna reproduced Astra’s score
exactly, and Qwen3.6 came within a tenth of a point in the
second of its two runs; in its first run, it delivered a predictor
worse than the baseline.
Task framing. Stream-wise regression per cutter; each cut is one sample; predict
the flank wear averaged over the three flutes after the cut, in µm; the history uses
only cuts already received.
Data and preprocessing. Use the three vibration axes only. Take 20%–80% of
the current record (bounds floored, keeping at least one point); subsample with
step ⌊points/20000⌋, minimum 1. Read in single precision, compute in double;
set non-finite values to zero, clip to ±106, and subtract each axis’s mean. Raise
an error on empty signals.
Features. (1) Difference amplitude: population standard deviation of (𝑋−
𝑍)/
√
2. (2) Projection amplitude: project onto the unit directions (1, −1, 0)/
√
2
and (1, 1, −2)/
√
6 and take the square root of the smallest eigenvalue of the
two-dimensional population covariance. (3) Minor-axis amplitude: square root
of the smallest eigenvalue of the unbiased three-axis covariance (zero for a single
point); negatives are set to zero before the square root. Each model takes two
inputs: the amplitude raised to an inner power, accumulated over the cutter’s
cuts and raised to an outer power; and a run-in term 1 −exp(−cut/scale).
Model and hyperparameters. A weighted average of five ridge regressions
with intercept, configured as (amplitude; inner power, outer power, run-in scale,
regularization base, weight): difference; 3, 1/4, 10, 3, 1/4. Difference; 2.5, 1/3,
10, 3, 1/4. Projection; 2.5, 1/3, 10, 3, 1/4. Minor axis; 3, 1/4, 10, 3, 1/8. Minor
axis; 2, 1/2, 5, 1, 1/8.
Training and validation. Sort by cutter and cut and join the development
labels. Hold out whole cutters for validation, infer in causal order, and compare
configurations and blends by the cutter-averaged MAE. In each fold, compute the
input mean and standard deviation on the training part only; the regularization
strength is the base times the number of training samples divided by 315. Finally,
refit on all development samples and fold the standardization into the coefficients
and intercept.
Inference and output. When cuts are skipped, fill the missing accumulation
with the mean inner-power amplitude of the neighboring endpoints already seen;
do not backfill on the first call. Reuse the output for a repeated cut; reset when
cut numbers do not increase, and across cutters. Non-finite predictions fall back
to the previous value, and on the first call to the mean first-cut label of the
development data. Clip to [0, 1000] and take the maximum with the previous
prediction. Output finite predictions in the original order and identifiers.
Compute. Feature extraction for training uses four processes; inference uses
one CPU and no GPU, with reading cost linear in record length and a constant
history state; about 1 s to start and 0.1 s per cut, with peak memory of 256 MB.
