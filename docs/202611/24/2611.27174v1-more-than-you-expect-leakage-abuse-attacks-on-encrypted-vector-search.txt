Abstract—Vector
databases
support
semantic
search
and
retrieval-augmented generation, but the embeddings, queries,
and record labels they handle may contain sensitive infor-
mation. Encrypted vector search aims to protect this infor-
mation. However, some encrypted vector search schemes fail
to hide which records are retrieved together across queries.
We identify semantic neighborhood leakage, showing that co-
retrieval relationships derived from access patterns can reveal
the semantic categories of encrypted records and queries
without exposing their underlying contents.
We present SAGE (Semantic Access-pattern Graph Ex-
ploitation), a passive and persistent leakage-abuse attack that
infers record labels and query categories from access patterns
by exploiting semantic neighborhood leakage. SAGE constructs
a weighted co-retrieval graph and develops two independent at-
tack variants under distinct auxiliary-knowledge assumptions.
SAGE-Dist matches anonymous spectral groups to semantic
categories using estimated class proportions and refines record
labels via optimal transport, without requiring known record
labels. SAGE-Known combines graph-based label propagation
with anchor voting over spectral groups, using a small set of
known record labels.
We evaluate SAGE on four datasets spanning text and
images. With only 1% known record labels, SAGE-Known
achieves up to 84.3% mean record-label accuracy and con-
sistently outperforms the strongest corresponding baselines
across all four datasets. Using only estimated class propor-
tions, SAGE-Dist achieves 66.9% record-label accuracy and
68.1% query-category accuracy on AG News, exceeding the
strongest corresponding baselines by 21.8 and 41.9 percentage
points, respectively. However, its effectiveness varies across
datasets. Experiments with a real-world DCPE implementation
(Fuchsbauer et al., SCN’22) further show that semantic leakage
persists under encrypted approximate retrieval. Our findings
highlight the privacy risks posed by semantic neighborhood
leakage, even when vector contents remain protected.

accessed. We refer to the set of returned record identifiers
for each query as its access pattern. Some encrypted vector-
search schemes hide retrieval accesses through PIR or obliv-
ious computation [18], [19], [20], [21], but these approaches
introduce additional computation, communication, or de-
ployment costs. Other schemes prioritize efficient retrieval
and expose record identifiers [22], [23], [24], [25]. Such
exposure also occurs in practical systems, as demonstrated
by our experiments with Quiver [26], a vector search sys-
tem supporting distance-comparison-preserving encryption
(DCPE) [27]. Appendix A further examines identifier expo-
sure in practical vector search systems. Following Hoover
et al. [28] and Sun et al. [29], we consider a passive
and persistent server-side adversary who observes returned
record identifiers but cannot access plaintext record contents,
embeddings, queries, or semantic labels. Although these
identifiers need not carry semantic meaning themselves,
repeated observations reveal overlaps across query results.
Such overlaps can reveal semantic relationships among
encrypted records. We say that two records are co-retrieved
when they appear in the result of the same query. Since top-k
retrieval reflects proximity in an embedding space, repeated
co-retrieval can expose latent semantic neighborhoods. In
Figure 1, the returned sets {1, 3, 4} and {3, 4, 7} share
records 3 and 4. By aggregating such relationships across
queries, an adversary can recover anonymous groups of
semantically related records. With limited auxiliary knowl-
edge, such as estimated class proportions or a few known
labels, these groups can further reveal record labels and
query categories. We refer to this category-level exposure
as semantic neighborhood leakage. In the clinical-record
example, an adversary may associate a recovered group with
cardiology, thereby exposing the diagnostic categories of
protected records and the medical topics of private queries.
This inference does not require reconstructing record con-
tents or embeddings, nor does it require every co-retrieved
record to share the same label.
The possibility of inferring hidden semantic information
raises the question of whether existing leakage-abuse attacks
can exploit such leakage. Prior work has demonstrated some
inference attacks against encrypted search [30], [31], [32].
These attacks exploit numerical ordering and statistical rela-
tionships to infer protected information. However, existing
techniques do not directly address semantic-category infer-
ence from high-dimensional top-k similarity search.
1.1. Limitations of Existing Attacks
Order-based inference. Existing attacks on encrypted nu-
merical databases exploit ordering constraints exposed by
range and nearest-neighbor queries [33], [34], [35]. In one-
dimensional k-NN search, returned records form consecutive
intervals in a common sorted order, allowing adversaries
to reconstruct their relative positions from overlapping ac-
cess patterns. Prior work has also extended reconstruction
to multidimensional range queries, including attacks that
exploit co-occurrence relationships and graph drawing to
recover geometric structure [36], [37], [38], [39]. However,
high-dimensional top-k similarity search does not expose
a global record ordering or the axis-aligned constraints of
range queries. Its returned sets instead reflect local neigh-
borhoods in an embedding space, making existing recon-
struction techniques difficult to apply directly. Moreover,
recovering geometric relationships does not identify the
semantic categories of records or queries.
Statistical matching attacks. Attacks on encrypted key-
word and equality search exploit frequencies and co-
occurrence statistics to match observable leakage with can-
didate plaintext values [29], [30], [31], [40], [41]. These
techniques typically rely on corresponding auxiliary statis-
tics for candidate keywords or values. In contrast, vector
similarity search retrieves records according to embedding
proximity rather than exact predicate matches. Queries from
the same semantic category may retrieve different records,
while queries from different categories may return overlap-
ping sets. Consequently, co-retrieval relationships provide
noisy evidence of semantic similarity rather than a direct
correspondence to plaintext keyword or value statistics. Fur-
thermore, even if anonymous record groups are recovered,
access patterns alone cannot identify their semantic cate-
gories, since permuting the hidden category names leaves
the observed access patterns unchanged.
1.2. Our Contributions
We present SAGE (Semantic Access-pattern Graph Ex-
ploitation), a passive and persistent leakage-abuse attack that
exploits semantic neighborhood leakage to infer record la-
bels and query categories from access patterns in encrypted
vector search. Our contributions are as follows.
• Characterizing semantic neighborhood leakage. We
investigate how repeated top-k retrievals expose semantic
relationships among encrypted records through observable
identifiers. We construct a weighted co-retrieval graph
to capture these relationships and examine the semantic
structure revealed by spectral grouping. We distinguish
the recovery of anonymous record groups from the iden-
tification of their semantic categories.
• Two attacks with distinct auxiliary knowledge. We
develop two independent inference attacks. SAGE-Dist
uses estimated class proportions to associate anonymous
groups with semantic categories, followed by optimal-
transport refinement of record labels. SAGE-Known com-
bines graph-based label propagation, class-mass normal-
ization, and anchor voting over spectral groups using a
small set of known record labels. Both attacks infer query
categories from their predicted record labels.
• Evaluation
and
privacy
implications. We evaluate
SAGE on four datasets spanning text and images under
varying observation workloads and auxiliary-knowledge
settings. With 1% known record labels, SAGE-Known
achieves mean record-label accuracies of 84.3%, 64.9%,
47.0%, and 27.8% across the four datasets. Using only
estimated class proportions, SAGE-Dist achieves 66.9%
on AG News and 36.1% on Yahoo Answers, although its
effectiveness varies substantially across datasets and runs.

2. Related Work
Leakage-abuse attacks on encrypted search. Prior work
has demonstrated that search, access, and volume patterns
can reveal sensitive information even when database con-
tents and queries are encrypted [30], [31], [32], [42]. At-
tacks against encrypted numerical databases exploit ordering
and response overlaps to reconstruct values and geometric
relationships [33], [36], [37], [38], [39], [43], [44], [45].
Kornaropoulos et al. [34], [35] further investigate recon-
struction attacks against one-dimensional nearest-neighbor
search. In encrypted keyword search, IHOP [40] and Jig-
saw [41] exploit statistical relationships and auxiliary knowl-
edge to recover protected queries. Hoover et al. [28] investi-
gate leakage-abuse attacks against structured encryption for
SQL, while Sun et al. [29] develop incremental inference
attacks that exploit correlations and joint distributions across
encrypted SQL attributes. Other attacks similarly leverage
frequency and co-occurrence statistics [46]. While these at-
tacks exploit ordering constraints or statistical relationships
in structured search, SAGE investigates semantic-category
inference from co-retrieval relationships in high-dimensional
top-k similarity search. The limitations of existing attacks
in this setting are discussed in Section 1.1.
Privacy-preserving vector search. Some private nearest-
neighbor schemes employ oblivious data structures, private
information retrieval, or other privacy-preserving mecha-
nisms to protect retrieval patterns [18], [19], [20], [21].
Other approaches support efficient server-side search over
protected vectors. Early schemes employ scalar-product-
preserving encryption or locality-sensitive hashing for en-
crypted nearest-neighbor and similarity search [24], [25].
More recently, Liu et al. [22] combine encrypted distance
comparisons with HNSW-based approximate search, while
Wang et al. [23] extend this approach to range-filtered
retrieval and explicitly allow final result identifiers to be ex-
posed. SAGE targets deployments where the server can ob-
serve and link the selected record identifiers across queries,
without requiring plaintext embeddings, distance values, or
index traversal traces.
Privacy attacks on embeddings and retrieval systems.
When plaintext embeddings are accessible, embedding in-
version attacks can reconstruct information about their
source inputs [15], [16], [17]. Other studies investigate
membership inference against retrieval-augmented genera-
tion systems through interactions or externally observable
outputs [47], [48], [49], [50]. In contrast, SAGE requires
neither plaintext embeddings nor retrieved content or gen-
erated answers. It exploits co-retrieval relationships derived
from observable access patterns to infer the semantic labels
of database records and the categories of user queries.

3.2. Threat Model and Leakage
Adversary. Following prior leakage-abuse attacks [28], we
consider a passive and persistent server-side adversary that
observes legitimate retrieval executions without interfering
with them. The adversary has no decryption keys and can-
not access plaintext record contents, database embeddings,
semantic labels, or query contents and embeddings, except
for independently obtained auxiliary knowledge. It neither
injects queries nor modifies the database or retrieval results.
Access-pattern leakage. Suppose the server processes M
legitimate queries. For each query i ∈{1, . . . , M}, the
adversary observes the unordered set Ii of selected record
identifiers defined in Eq. (2). The accumulated access-
pattern leakage is
LAP = (I1, I2, . . . , IM).
(3)
The adversary only needs to recognize repeated identifiers
across queries. Thus, opaque or encrypted identifiers do not


prevent this leakage if they remain linkable. SAGE uses
neither similarity scores nor retrieval rankings. Our attack
applies to deployments exposing cross-query-linkable result
identifiers.
Semantic neighborhood leakage. Similarity search re-
trieves records based on embedding proximity. As seman-
tically related records often occupy nearby regions of the
embedding space, they may repeatedly appear together in
query results. For each record rj, we define its retriever set
as the indices of observed queries that return its identifier:
Qj = {i ∈[M] | idj ∈Ii}.

expose the database’s latent semantic structure, giving rise
to semantic neighborhood leakage. The exposed structure
does not necessarily identify semantic categories without
auxiliary knowledge.

4. Technique Overview
SAGE exploits semantic neighborhood leakage to infer
record labels and query categories from observable access
patterns. It first constructs a co-retrieval graph that cap-
tures relationships among records retrieved together across
queries. These relationships reveal structural groups without
identifying the semantic categories of their records. SAGE
then uses one of two forms of auxiliary knowledge to
infer record labels from the observed structure. Finally, both
attack variants infer query categories from their predicted
record labels.
4.1. Co-retrieval Graph Framework
Key observation. An individual returned identifier does not
directly reveal its record label. Across queries, however,
records belonging to the same semantic category may be
retrieved together more frequently than records from dif-
ferent classes. Their co-retrieval relationships can therefore
expose semantic structure even when contents, embeddings
and labels remain encrypted.

Anonymous grouping. SAGE applies spectral analysis to
the normalized co-retrieval graph to obtain structural rep-
resentations of observed records. Spectral grouping identi-
fies anonymous groups whose members exhibit similar co-
retrieval relationships. These groups need not correspond
exactly to semantic categories, and their members’ record
labels remain unknown.
The central challenge is therefore to infer record labels
from anonymous graph structure using auxiliary knowledge.
SAGE addresses this challenge through two distinct infer-
ence strategies. Section 5 presents the graph construction
and spectral procedures.
4.2. SAGE Attack Overview
The co-retrieval graph reveals structural relationships
among records but does not directly reveal their semantic
categories. SAGE develops two attack variants that exploit
this structure using distinct forms of auxiliary knowledge,
as illustrated in Figure 3.
SAGE-Dist. SAGE-Dist assumes estimated class propor-
tions ˆπ, without knowing any individual record labels. It first
partitions the co-retrieval graph into C anonymous groups
using spectral embedding and KMeans. It then matches
the relative group sizes to the estimated class proportions
using the Hungarian algorithm to assign semantic classes to
the groups. To account for imperfect grouping, SAGE-Dist
further refines individual record-label predictions through
Sinkhorn-based optimal transport, which constrains the soft
assignment masses according to the estimated class propor-
tions.
For example, consider the two anonymous groups in
Figure 2. If cardiology is estimated to be more prevalent
than dermatology, the larger group can be associated with
cardiology and the smaller with dermatology.
SAGE-Known. SAGE-Known instead assumes a small set
of known identifier-label pairs Ψ. Known records present in
the observed graph serve as anchors. SAGE-Known propa-
gates their labels through weighted co-retrieval connections
while keeping anchor labels fixed. The propagated scores are
normalized by class-wise mass to reduce class imbalance.
In parallel, spectral grouping provides anonymous groups in
which known anchors contribute smoothed class votes. The
two sources of evidence are combined to infer the labels of
other records.
For example, if record 2 is a known cardiology record
and record 6 is a known dermatology record, their labels
provide evidence for labeling neighboring records through
graph connections and group memberships. Section 6 details
both procedures.
Query category inference. After completing record-label
predictions, both variants infer each observed query’s cat-
egory by majority voting over the predicted labels of its
retrieved records. For instance, query 5 retrieves {4, 5, 6}.
If record 4 is predicted as cardiology and records 5 and 6
as dermatology, the inferred query category is dermatology.
This inference estimates the query’s semantic category. The
completion and voting procedures are specified in Section 6.

resulting anonymous groups need not coincide with se-

coverage, and fragmented graph connections may produce
inaccurate or missing group assignments. These groups
therefore provide structural evidence rather than recovered
record labels.

7. Evaluation
We evaluate semantic leakage from access patterns
across four datasets. We first examine co-retrieval structure
and attack effectiveness, then assess the impact of algo-
rithmic components, auxiliary knowledge, and observation
volume. Finally, we study robustness under real encrypted
retrieval.
7.1. Experimental Setup
Datasets and embeddings. We evaluate four datasets
spanning text and image retrieval: AG News, Yahoo An-
swers, MedMCQA, and CIFAR-100, with 4, 10, 21, and
100 semantic categories, respectively. We encode text us-
ing BGE-base-en-v1.5 (768 dimensions) and images using
OpenCLIP ViT-B/32 (512 dimensions). All embeddings are
ℓ2-normalized. Database, query, and auxiliary pools are
disjoint, and sampling is performed without replacement.
Ground-truth labels are used only for evaluation and the
specified auxiliary knowledge.
Retrieval workloads. Unless otherwise stated, we observe
M = 2,000 queries, each returning the exact inner-product
top-k records with k = 100. Database sizes depend on
the dataset. We use Zipf class distributions with exponents
1.0 for AG News and Yahoo Answers and 0.5 for CIFAR-
100, while MedMCQA retains its natural class distribution.

isolate the contribution of group-level voting from the prop-

Query-category inference. SAGE-Known achieves query-

Grouping versus semantic identification. Table 2 reports
grouping diagnostics to distinguish structural recovery from
semantic label inference. On CIFAR-100, spectral grouping
achieves an NMI of 0.607, while the grouping selected by

These results indicate that anonymous groups can retain
semantic information even when the attack fails to identify
record categories accurately. However, oracle alignment uses
ground-truth labels unavailable to the adversary, and the gap
also reflects subsequent optimal-transport refinement. Thus,
structural recovery alone does not guarantee successful se-
mantic identification.

We examine whether the main components of SAGE-
Dist and SAGE-Known contribute to record-label inference
under their respective auxiliary-knowledge settings. Within
each attack, the compared variants share the same access
patterns and auxiliary knowledge. Figure 5 reports mean
record-label accuracy and standard deviation over ten runs.
Exact results and fusion-weight sensitivity are given in
Appendix C.3.
SAGE-Dist components. Figure 5(a) compares a single
KMeans candidate named by class-proportion matching with
two successive additions: selection among six candidates
and Sinkhorn-based record-level refinement. Both additions
use the same spectral embedding dimension and auxiliary
prior. On AG News, mean accuracy increases from 57.6%
to 66.0% and then 66.9%. Yahoo Answers improves from
32.1% to 34.2% and then 36.1%, while MedMCQA shows
smaller gains. On CIFAR-100, the full method reaches 1.9%.
These findings show that the added stages can improve cat-


egory assignment, but they cannot reliably resolve category-
name ambiguity from class proportions in every dataset.
SAGE-Known components. Figure 5(b) compares clamped
label propagation with class-mass normalization (LP-only,
γ = 0), spectral group-level anchor voting without propaga-
tion scores (Group-only, γ = 1), and their fusion in SAGE-
Known. The full method achieves the highest mean record-
label accuracy among these variants on all four datasets.
Relative to LP-only, its gains are 2.3, 5.2, 1.7, and 0.3
percentage points on AG News, Yahoo Answers, MedM-
CQA, and CIFAR-100, respectively. Group-only also under-
performs the fused method, indicating that group-level votes
are complementary to propagation rather than a sufficient
replacement. The full γ sweep in Appendix C.3 further
shows that the contribution of group voting depends on its
weight.

7.6. Robustness in Encrypted Vector Retrieval
We evaluate SAGE with Quiver’s encrypted vector re-
trieval using four retrieval settings. Exact performs local
plaintext exact top-k search. Quiver Plain uses the server’s
plaintext HNSW index. Simulated DCPE applies local
scale-and-perturb noise followed by exact retrieval. Quiver
DCPE combines client-side DCPE encryption with Quiver’s
HNSW retrieval. In all settings, the adversary uses only
the returned record identifiers. We use M = 2,000 queries
and k = 100 throughout. The full protocol is described in
Appendix C.4.
Attack effectiveness. At β = 1.6, Quiver DCPE achieves
only 68.8–76.9% Recall@100, yet SAGE-Known maintains
record-label accuracies of 84.2%, 64.7%, 46.9%, and 27.1%
across the four datasets, close to its exact-search results.
Thus, retrieval errors do not necessarily eliminate the co-
retrieval relationships exploited by the attack. SAGE-Dist is
less stable, with 61.7%, 32.8%, 5.4%, and 1.9% accuracy,
respectively. Its limited performance on MedMCQA and
CIFAR-100 also occurs under exact retrieval.
Perturbation sensitivity. We further vary the DCPE per-
turbation strength β ∈{0.02, 0.1, 0.4, 1.6}. As β increases,
Quiver DCPE Recall@100 decreases from approximately
99% to 68.8–76.9%, while SAGE-Known accuracy changes
only slightly. SAGE-Dist shows greater variation without
a consistent trend. Results for both Quiver DCPE and the
simulated perturbation are given in Appendix C.4.
Record linkability. We further examine idealized identifier
rotation, where record pseudonyms cannot be linked across
query windows. With windows of 50–500 queries, SAGE-
Dist falls below the majority baseline on AG News and
Yahoo Answers. SAGE-Known also degrades substantially
when known-label anchors can only be recognized in the
first window, whereas access to anchors in every window
preserves much of its accuracy. Unlike Quiver DCPE, which
retains linkable record identifiers, this experiment removes
cross-window linkability by assumption. It quantifies the
importance of persistent record linkage rather than demon-
strating a deployable defense. Further results are given in
Appendix C.5.

8. Discussion and Limitations
Scope of the attack. SAGE targets encrypted vector-search
systems with record identifiers that remain linkable across
queries. It requires neither plaintext embeddings or queries
nor similarity scores or retrieval rankings. Its effectiveness
depends on the co-retrieval relationships exposed by legiti-
mate workloads, a known semantic taxonomy, and limited
auxiliary knowledge. SAGE infers record labels and query
categories rather than reconstructing plaintext contents. Sys-
tems that hide retrieval accesses fall outside our threat
model.
Limitations of semantic inference. Recovering anonymous
record groups does not guarantee identifying their seman-
tic categories. SAGE-Dist performs better on AG News
and Yahoo Answers but remains limited on MedMCQA
and CIFAR-100, where ambiguous class proportions, mixed
neighborhoods, and imperfect grouping complicate cate-
gory assignment. SAGE-Known achieves stronger results
but depends on the availability and category coverage of
known labels. Both variants may be affected by changes
in query distributions, embeddings, and retrieval workloads.
Our findings do not imply universal label recoverability.
Deployment implications and defenses. Experiments with
Quiver DCPE demonstrate that semantic inference remains
possible under encrypted approximate retrieval. Fixed identi-
fier pseudonymization does not prevent co-retrieval analysis
when identifiers remain linkable. Identifier rotation may
reduce accumulated leakage if cross-window linkage is pre-
vented, although other metadata may still enable record link-
age. Such defenses can introduce additional system costs.
These findings motivate protecting semantic relationships
alongside encrypted vector contents.
9. Conclusion
We identify semantic neighborhood leakage in encrypted
vector search, showing that co-retrieval relationships can
expose sensitive semantic information even when record
contents and embeddings remain protected. We develop
SAGE-Dist and SAGE-Known to infer record labels and
query categories using distinct forms of auxiliary knowl-
edge. Experiments across four datasets and encrypted re-
trieval settings demonstrate the feasibility and limitations
of these attacks. Our findings highlight the need to protect
semantic relationships beyond encrypting vector contents.

tolerance of 10−5 or after 500 iterations.
Completion and query voting. Unassigned records are

tion 6.3. If no labeled record is available, the fallback selects
class index 0. Completed record labels are then used for
query-category majority voting. The same completion pro-

C.5. Identifier Rotation
We evaluate idealized identifier rotation by partition-
ing 2,000 queries into windows with independent record
pseudonyms. At a window size of 50 queries, SAGE-Known
achieves 76% accuracy on AG News when known anchors
can be matched in every window, compared with 29% when
anchors are restricted to the first window. The corresponding
results on Yahoo Answers are 45% and 19%. These findings
suggest that preventing cross-window record linkage can re-
duce semantic inference, although other persistent identifiers
or metadata may still enable linkage.
