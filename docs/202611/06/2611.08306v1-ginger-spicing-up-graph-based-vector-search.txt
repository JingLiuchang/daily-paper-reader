## ABSTRACT 

We present Ginger, a state-of-the-art open-source C++ library for graph-based approximate nearest neighbor search (ANNS). We developed Ginger at Google to push the speed and memory effi-ciency of graph search on large and high-dimensional embedding datasets. Ginger surpasses existing ANNS libraries with regards to query throughput and indexing time while matching the memory footprint of the most space-efficient competitors. Ginger builds upon recent advances in proximity graph con-struction (PiPNN) and vector quantization (TurboQuant). We in-troduce Nested TurboQuant (NTQ), a two-level quantizer which supports low precision graph navigation and high precision rerank-ing in a single code. This representation achieves near floating point precision with 2 .6× less memory. We also compress the size of our proximity graphs by up to 2 .5× without impacting query through-put. We combine graph reordering with a new lossless compression codec called AdaptiveGap, which builds upon StreamVByte. At 95% recall, Ginger speeds up query throughput by an average of 1 .8×

over each dataset’s fastest competitor and by at least 2 .3× over any single library. This lead grows to 2 .9× over compact libraries on high-dimensional embeddings. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

The rapid progress in deep learning has placed Approximate Nearest Neighbor Search (ANNS) at the center of information retrieval and data analysis [31, 49, 50, 62, 72, 88, 93]. Today’s workloads are marked by a sharp increase in both the number of indexed entities and the dimensionality of the embeddings used to represent them. Proximity graphs have been shown to perform well in very high dimensions, and are thus the dominant technique for ANNS indexing whenever high recall is paramount. A proximity graph represents each entity as a vertex and connects it to nearby and further directionally diverse neighbors. Queries route through the graph via greedy beam search , which iteratively expands the closest unexplored vertices until converging to the query neighborhood. Prominent proximity-graph algorithms include HNSW [57], Va-mana (DiskANN) [41], NSG [26], and PiPNN [73, 74]. Compared to cluster-based inverted-file (IVF) indexes such as Faiss [22] and ScaNN [37, 81], proximity graphs achieve high recall with many fewer distance evaluations on modern embeddings. Still, graph indexes face three bottlenecks in practice: (1) mem-ory bandwidth at search time, (2) memory footprint to host the index, and (3) indexing time. • Nested TurboQuant (Section 3). We introduce Nested Turbo-Quant (NTQ), an extension of TurboQuant in which a single code per vector serves both graph navigation and reranking. • Fast graph search (Sections 3 and 4). We contribute an ex-tremely fast SIMD kernel for one-to-many scoring of the 4-bit codes. • Graph compression (Section 5). On low-dimensional datasets, the graph adjacency lists of prior libraries consume as much memory as the quantized vectors themselves. We combine this codec with graph partitioning to assign nearby IDs to neighboring vertices. Together, these techniques shrink the graph by an average of 2 .1× (up to 2 .5×) relative to an un-compressed CSR layout without slowing down queries. 

• Extensive evaluation (Section 6). We compare Ginger with seven state-of-the-art graph and IVF libraries on 14 public bench-marks with up to one billion vectors and 4096 dimensions. Gin-ger achieves the highest query throughput at 90% and 95% recall on 13 of the 14 benchmarks. At 95% recall, Ginger outperforms the fastest competitor by 2 .0× on average (up to 2 .7×) on high-dimensional data and by 1 .45 × (up to 2 .1×) on low-dimensional data. At 90% recall, the corresponding speedups are 1 .8× (up to 2.3×) and 1 .55 × (up to 2 .0×). The complete Ginger index for the billion-point Deep dataset occupies approximately 288 GB of memory with 12-bit NTQ (Figure 5). Ginger’s modular design and portable SIMD kernels make it easy to integrate state-of-the-art ANNS into new search and data management systems. Our results demonstrate that we can simul-taneously deliver state-of-the-art memory footprint, search perfor-mance, and indexing time through a mix of algorithmic improve-ments and performance engineering.

## 2.1 ANNS Indexes 

Proximity Graphs and Search. A graph-based ANNS index or-ganizes vectors into a proximity graph, with a vertex for each vec-tor and directed edges connecting vectors. Neighbors are selected heuristically during graph construction based on closeness and directional diversity. Queries explore this graph using beam search (Algorithm 1). Starting from an entry vertex 𝑠 , the search maintains a beam 𝐵 of at most 𝐿 candidates and a set 𝑉 of expanded vertices. It repeatedly expands the closest vertex in 𝐵 \ 𝑉 , adds its neighbors to the beam, and retains the 𝐿 closest candidates, stopping when every vertex in the beam has been expanded. Implementations of graph search differ widely in their candidate and visited-set data structures, stopping rules, filtering, batching, and prefetching. They may also navigate using quantized vectors and rerank a larger can-didate set using full-precision vectors or more accurate codes. These choices affect memory traffic, query throughput, and recall.

Graph Construction. The Vamana [41] index constructs a prox-imity graph iteratively via beam-search traversals on the partial index and an 𝛼 -Relative Neighborhood Graph ( 𝛼 -RNG) pruning heuristic ( RobustPrune ). This rule enforces directional diversity and injects long-range edges, achieving shorter search paths than hierarchical structures like HNSW [57]. Many other proximity-graph construction algorithms have been proposed [21, 24–26, 38, 40, 56, 63, 86, 90, 91, 94]. In practice, Vamana and HNSW are the most widely used. However, search-driven graph construction suf-fers from a fundamental “search bottleneck” [74] requiring millions

of random-access memory lookups and thus incurring long build times. 

PiPNN (Pick-in-Partitions Nearest Neighbors) [74] speeds up in-dex construction by replacing searches during construction with a partitioning-based build paradigm. Instead of global beam searches, PiPNN recursively decomposes the dataset into overlapping clus-ters via randomized ball carving and generates candidate edges using cache-friendly many-to-many top-𝑘 kernels. This achieves an order-of-magnitude faster index construction across CPUs and accelerators while maintaining equivalent graph quality. In Gin-ger, we offer both PiPNN and Vamana index builds to give users flexibility and choice. 

Inverted Indexes. Inverted-file (IVF) indexes partition vectors into clusters and store each cluster in an inverted list. A query probes a subset of the clusters and scores the vectors in their lists, often using quantized representations followed by higher-precision reranking. 

Quantization. Quantization replaces floating-point vectors with compact codes representing regions of the vector space, reducing both index memory and the bytes fetched during distance evalua-tion [1, 42]. The codes must support fast scoring while preserving enough accuracy to identify the nearest neighbors. Scalar quanti-zation encodes each coordinate independently using 𝑏 bits to select one of 2 𝑏 representative values. Product quantization partitions a vector into subvectors and encodes each with an index into a learned codebook; distances are estimated by combining per-subvector ta-ble lookups [32, 42]. Rotation-based scalar quantizers first apply a shared random orthogonal transformation, then encode the trans-formed coordinates. Examples include DRIVE/EDEN [8, 84, 85], RaBitQ [27, 28, 30], and TurboQuant [92]. The representation and scoring kernel must also suit the search access pattern. Graph tra-versal scores scattered candidates, whereas IVF search scans lists of vectors. Quantization has a rich literature, and we refer readers to the excellent surveys [36, 60] for background on its foundations and applications to nearest-neighbor search. Section 3 presents our nested quantizer and SIMD scoring kernels for graph navigation and reranking. 

## 2.2 Prior Libraries 

We focus on a representative selection of widely used libraries and high-performance ANNS methods. Our selection emphasizes methods that are widely adopted and/or very high performance while covering graph-based and inverted-file search. 

DiskANN. DiskANN [41, 46] is Microsoft’s widely used library for in-memory and SSD-backed search using the Vamana proximity graph. Its SSD-backed design enables billion-scale search on a single machine by keeping compressed vectors in memory and fetching graph neighborhoods and full-precision vectors from disk. 

ParlayANN. ParlayANN [59, 66] is a highly optimized parallel graph-based ANNS library built on the ParlayLib [12] runtime. It provides deterministic implementations of proximity-graph al-gorithms, including Vamana, designed to avoid synchronization bottlenecks and scale to large datasets on shared-memory machines. 

SVS. Intel’s Scalable Vector Search (SVS) [1] combines Vamana-based search with highly optimized SIMD kernels and compressed vector representations. Its LVQ codec uses per-vector scaling and scalar quantization to reduce memory traffic, while LeanVec [83] combines dimensionality reduction with quantization. 

Hnswlib. Hnswlib [58] provides a lightweight, header-only C++ implementation of HNSW [57] with Python bindings and support for custom distance functions. It supports incremental index con-struction, vector updates and deletions, and multithreaded search, with configurable graph connectivity and search effort. 

SymphonyQG. SymphonyQG [27, 35] combines RaBitQ quan-tization [30] with FastScan [4], storing codes on graph edges to score neighbors sequentially. Its layout aligns vertex degrees with 32-edge FastScan batches, trading increased memory usage from replicated codes for fewer random accesses during graph search. 

Faiss. Faiss [22, 43] is Meta’s similarity search library, offering flat, IVF, and graph indexes with scalar and product quantization. Its widely-used CPU implementations support large-scale search, with index and compression choices that provide different tradeoffs between memory usage, query throughput, and recall. 

ScaNN. ScaNN [37, 81] is an existing IVF-based similarity search li-brary developed at Google, combining partitioning with anisotropic vector quantization and optimized scoring kernels for maximum inner-product search. Its two-stage pipeline scores candidates using low-bit representations before reranking a smaller set, reducing the cost of evaluating large candidate pools. 

## 3 NESTED TURBOQUANT (NTQ) 

All production-grade ANNS libraries use quantization . Because distance evaluation performs only a small constant number of arith-metic operations per loaded byte, ANNS is limited by memory bandwidth. Compressing coordinates to low-precision codes re-duces both memory traffic and the memory footprint of the index. Graph beam search imposes a specific access pattern on the quan-tizer: each expansion fetches an arbitrary subset of non-contiguous points in X and scores each point by scanning across its coordinates. Unfortunately, standard PQ [32, 42], as used in IVF indexes, is ill-suited for this task because loading each of the 𝑀 subspace lookup tables is expensive relative to scoring a single point. Uniform scalar quantization methods avoid per-subspace codebooks and support fast coordinate-wise scans, but suffer higher quantization error. Recently, rotation-based quantizers [28, 30, 84, 85, 92] have become popular. They keep the fast coordinate-wise scans of uniform scalar quantizers and need no per-subspace tables, while achieving much lower quantization error. A random rotation makes every coordi-nate approximately Gaussian, so a single scalar codebook serves all coordinates. EDEN [85] and TurboQuant [92] place this codebook non-uniformly at the Lloyd–Max points of the Gaussian [52, 61], which minimizes the mean-squared error per coordinate. Graph search needs two precisions with conflicting goals. Beam search scores thousands of random candidates per query and needs a compact code that is cheap to load from memory and score. Reranking scores only the final beam, but needs the accuracy of floats to achieve very high recall. A standard recipe is therefore to navigate with aggressive quantization and then rerank the con-verged beam with floats or higher-precision codes. Storing a second code for reranking, or maintaining the float vectors, wastes signifi-cant memory. A single nested code avoids this waste, but only if

nesting does not degrade the quality of the codes. We developed Nested TurboQuant (NTQ) to achieve this. 

Overview of Our Approach. NTQ builds on TurboQuant [92] and EDEN [8, 85]. It takes the 4-bit Lloyd–Max codebook and splits each of its intervals into 2 𝑘 finer sub-intervals, which yields a (4 + 𝑘 )-bit code. The 4-bit prefix of every code is therefore a complete Tur-boQuant code for navigation. The full code nearly matches float accuracy for reranking. Search cost stays fixed, while the 𝑘 addi-tional bits per coordinate trade memory for reranking accuracy. We mostly focus on 10- and 12-bit codes ( 𝑘 = 6 and 8). Nesting is nearly free, because the rotation makes every coordinate approximately Gaussian, and Gaussian sources are successively refinable [23]. Both levels of NTQ are therefore close to optimal for the rotated coordinates. We make both levels fast to score by evaluating their codebooks in registers rather than memory. 

The following subsections describe NTQ in detail for in-terested readers. Section 3.1 sets up the TurboQuant encoding that NTQ builds on and points out where we deviate from it. Sec-tion 3.2 introduces the nested refinement, which is the core of NTQ. Sections 3.3 and 3.4 describe fast scoring kernels for 4-bit navigation and (4 + 𝑘 )-bit reranking. All quantization and scoring kernels are implemented in portable SIMD using Highway [87]. For concreteness, we refer to AVX-512 instructions in the text.

## 3.3 Vectorized 4-Bit Scoring for Beam Search 

Each coordinate of a data vector is stored as a 4-bit code for beam search, so the codebook has only 16 entries. To score candidates with fast 8-bit integer dot-product instructions, we round these 16 centroids to int8 values and quantize the query coordinates to int8 as well. Each codebook entry then occupies a single byte, so the entire codebook forms a 16-entry lookup table. We load this table into a vector register. Scoring a candidate looks up each of its 4-bit codes in this register and accumulates the products with the query coordinates.

## 4 SEARCH OPTIMIZATIONS 

Although the basic beam search (Algorithm 1) is simple, ANN sys-tems vary significantly in the details of how the search is imple-mented. In Ginger, we build on our fast 4-bit scoring kernel (Sec-tion 3.3) with search optimizations that reduce memory traffic and the overhead of maintaining the candidate beam. 

## 4.1 Search on Projected Coordinates 

On high-dimensional datasets, fetching and scoring even quantized candidate vectors consumes substantial memory bandwidth. In Ginger, we reduce this cost by projecting database vectors and queries into a lower-dimensional space using Principal Compo-nent Analysis (PCA) [44]. We encode the projected vectors with 4-bit navigation codes and score them using the kernel from Sec-tion 3.3. Each candidate evaluation therefore reads and scores fewer coordinates, reducing memory traffic beyond quantization alone. The database vectors can be projected offline, whereas each in-coming query must be multiplied by the projection matrix 𝑃 before navigation begins. This matrix multiplication is itself memory-bound and competes with graph traversal for memory bandwidth when queries run in parallel across threads. To alleviate this bot-tleneck, we also quantize the rows of 𝑃 to 4 bits, reducing the data fetched during query projection. Our results show that this does not impair recall (Figure 8).

## 4.3 Beam Management 

A naive beam search maintains two priority queues for the closest unexplored vertices and the furthest points in 𝐵 . However, priority-queue operations take 𝑂 (log 𝐿 ) time and have poor cache locality. Following ParlayANN [59] and SVS [1], we instead keep 𝐵 in a single array of size at most 𝐿 sorted by distance. Each entry stores a flag indicating whether it has been explored, and we track the index of the first unexplored entry. Picking the next vertex to expand then takes 𝑂 (1) time. Because 𝐵 is sorted, the furthest element 𝑏 𝐿 sits at the end of the array and provides the distance cutoff for entering 

> 6GINGER: Spicing Up Graph-Based Vector Search

the beam. Inserting new points into the sorted array one at a time would take 𝑂 (𝐿 ) time per point. Instead, when expanding a vertex 

𝑝 ∈ 𝐵 , we keep only the unvisited neighbors that beat 𝑏 𝐿 , sort the survivors, and merge them into 𝐵 in a single linear pass. Later in the search, most neighbors have already been visited or fail to beat 𝑏 𝐿 , so merging after every expansion scans the whole beam just to insert one or two points. Once the initial phase of the search is over, we therefore batch across consecutive expan-sions. We collect unvisited neighbors from several unexplored beam vertices, score them together, and merge the survivors into 𝐵 in a single pass. Batching also exposes a longer stream of candidates, which helps amortize startup latency (due to prefetching) in the TurboQuant scoring kernel. 

## 5 GRAPH COMPRESSION 

In Ginger, we also reduce the memory occupied by the proximity graph itself, which can exceed the footprint of the quantized vectors on low-dimensional datasets. For example, a 96-dimensional vector occupies 144 bytes at 12 bits per coordinate, whereas a flat adjacency list with 64 four-byte slots and a degree counter occupies 260 bytes. We therefore compress the graph losslessly while keeping decoding fast enough to avoid slowing search. Our approach first relabels vertices so that neighbors receive nearby IDs (Section 5.1), then stores each sorted neighbor list as gaps between consecutive IDs. We encode these gaps with a codec that packs small values into fewer than eight bits and decodes four at a time using a few SIMD instructions (Section 5.2). 

Gap encoding. Let 𝑣 1 < 𝑣 2 < · · · < 𝑣 deg (𝑢 ) be the sorted neighbor IDs of a vertex 𝑢 . Gap encoding stores the first ID 𝑔 1 = 𝑣 1, followed by the differences 𝑔 𝑖 = 𝑣 𝑖 − 𝑣 𝑖 −1 for 2 ≤ 𝑖 ≤ deg (𝑢 ). A gap 𝑔 𝑖 needs about log 2 (𝑔 𝑖 + 1) bits, so small gaps mean a small graph. A prefix sum over the gaps recovers the IDs. 

## 5.1 Graph Reordering 

To reduce gaps between consecutive neighbor IDs, we place well-connected groups of vertices into contiguous ID ranges, an ap-proach well studied for web and social graphs [5, 11, 13, 14, 16, 51, 70, 75, 77] and inverted indexes [9, 10, 19, 20, 76, 78]. In Ginger, recursive graph partitioning ( RGP ) uses a parallel multilevel parti-tioner [33, 34] to recursively split the graph into balanced blocks with few crossing edges. We number the small leaf blocks in recur-sion order, assign consecutive IDs within each block, and permute the vectors accordingly. Balanced graph partitioning only works on undirected graphs, hence we symmetrize the graph by adding the reverse of every edge. Reciprocal connections then carry twice the weight, reflect-ing the potential for long gaps in both adjacency lists when their endpoints are separated. RGP yields smaller gaps than recursive graph bisection [19] on all six datasets and is faster to compute on all but the smallest (Section 6.3).

StreamVByte. We build on StreamVByte [48], which removes this dependency by storing lengths separately from gaps. It encodes groups of four gaps using a control byte with four 2-bit length selectors and a separate payload holding the gap values back to back. Each selector specifies a gap length of one to four bytes. To decode a group, StreamVByte loads 16 payload bytes into a SIMD register and uses one byte shuffle ( pshufb ) to distribute the values among four 32-bit lanes. A precomputed shuffle mask for each of the 256 control bytes specifies which payload bytes to copy into each lane, and a SIMD prefix sum recovers the neighbor IDs. However, rounding each gap to whole bytes wastes space; for example, a 9-bit gap requires two bytes. 

AdaptiveGap. AdaptiveGap retains StreamVByte’s single-shuffle decoding while adding adaptive bit widths and optional separate storage of the first neighbor ID. Each neighbor list selects one of 16 

modes , each specifying four bit widths that the 2-bit selectors choose among. Mode 0 uses the widths {8, 16 , 24 , 32 }, which corresponds to StreamVByte, while the other modes use sub-byte widths, such as {4, 12 , 20 , 28 }. For example, StreamVByte stores the gaps 3, 9, 1000, and 5 in 1 + 1 + 2 + 1 = 5 bytes. In the mode {4, 12 , 20 , 28 },they occupy 4 + 4 + 12 + 4 = 24 bits, or 3 bytes, with the same single control byte. of a byte spans 𝑜 + 𝑤 bits, so every gap must satisfy 𝑜 + 𝑤 ≤ 32. In mode 0, all gaps are byte-aligned, so the offset is always 0. ## 6 EXPERIMENTAL EVALUATION 

We compare Ginger with seven widely used ANNS libraries across 14 large-scale datasets ranging up to a billion points and up to four-thousand dimensions. We assess query throughput, memory footprint, and indexing time for each method. Subsequently, we run ablation studies to isolate where our performance gains come from and how key design choices affect performance, including standalone evaluations of NTQ and AdaptiveGap (Section 6.3). 

## 6.1 Experimental Setup 

Machine. We use a Google Cloud c4-highmem-288 instance, a dual-socket server with two 72-core Intel Xeon 6985P-C processors (Granite Rapids) and 2.3 TB of RAM across 6 NUMA nodes. 

Datasets. We evaluate Ginger on 14 public ANNS datasets cover-ing Euclidean and maximum inner-product search (MIPS), summa-rized in Table 1. Six low-dimensional datasets ( 𝑑 ≤ 200) comprise GloVe and five billion-vector benchmarks spanning image, web-search, and cross-modal retrieval. Eight high-dimensional datasets (768 ≤ 𝑑 ≤ 4096) contain up to 101 million vectors and cover text, encyclopedia, scientific, multimodal, and LLM embeddings. Input coordinates are 8-bit integers for MS-SPACEV and BigANN, and 32-bit floats for the remaining twelve datasets. 

Existing Libraries. We compare Ginger against the seven li-braries introduced in Section 2.2: the Vamana implementations in ParlayANN [59, 66], SVS [1], and DiskANN [41, 46]; HNSW [57] from Hnswlib [58]; the IVF implementations in ScaNN [37, 81] and Faiss [22]; and SymphonyQG [35] from the RaBitQ-Library [27]. Together, these libraries form a comprehensive comparison set span-ning both widely adopted industrial systems and highly optimized research implementations. We select SymphonyQG to represent methods that store auxiliary data on graph edges because of its reported query performance and scalable, multithreaded C++ im-plementation [27, 35]. 

Environment. All C++ implementations are compiled with Clang v19.1.7. DiskANN is built with Rust v1.97.0. We use full compile-time ( -O3 ) and link-time optimizations ( -lto ), and native archi-tecture tuning ( -march=native ). To avoid allocator contention and ensure a fair comparison, we link all implementations against 

jemalloc v5.3.0. This change is either neutral or positive on QPS for all methods. To ensure accurate query timings, we perform one warm-up run (discarded) followed by 5 timed runs of the query set for each parameter configuration and report the average through-put. To prevent cache interference across runs, we flush the CPU cache between runs. 

Query Setup. We evaluate query throughput without batching: individual queries are processed in parallel across threads. To ob-tain the Pareto-optimal frontier of throughput at each recall level, we sweep the search beam width 𝐿 search with select values in the range [10 , 2000 ] for graph-based indexes. For IVF-based indexes, we sweep the number of probed leaf clusters 𝑛 probe over select val-ues in [1, 1000 ] and the number of candidates to rerank 𝑛 rerank ∈{2𝑘, 4𝑘, 8𝑘, 16 𝑘, 32 𝑘, 64 𝑘 }. For multi-level ScaNN, we additionally sweep the number of top-level clusters to probe on two-level trees, and for three-level trees we evaluate selected pairs of middle- and top-level probe counts to cover various routing profiles that vary the search cost at each level, which we model by the amount of data loaded. See Appendix A for more details. Table 5 in Appendix A summarizes the vector representations used by each library. On the 8-bit integer datasets (MS-SPACEV and BigANN), all graph libraries operate directly on the native 

int8 /uint8 coordinates without quantization. On high-dimensional float datasets ( 𝑑 ≥ 768), libraries combine dimensionality reduction (PCA, OPQ, or JL prefiltering) or 1–2-bit spherical codes with higher-precision reranking. On low-dimensional float datasets ( 𝑑 ≤ 200), dimensionality reduction degrades accuracy so we omit it.

## 6.2 Comparison with other Libraries 

We evaluate the libraries along three dimensions: query throughput, memory footprint, and indexing time. These three properties jointly comprise the bedrock of index performance. We use geometric means for comparing across datasets. In general: Ginger produces 

smaller indexes which support higher query throughput with less indexing time as compared with all other libraries. 

Query Throughput. Figure 4 shows throughput (queries per sec-ond) with respect to recall. Ginger leads on 13 of 14 datasets across the [90% , 100% ] recall range and in area under the QPS-recall curve (AUC). At 95% recall, its mean speedup over the fastest competi-tor on each dataset is 1 .8× overall and 2 .0× on high-dimensional datasets, with the largest dataset-specific gain of 2 .7× on Wiki-Cohere. The strongest competitors on high-dimensional datasets are SymphonyQG and SVS. The only exception is BigANN, whose byte coordinates we do not encode with TurboQuant; there, SVS is 4% faster at both 90% and 95% recall. Because different competitors are second-best on different datasets, the advantage over any indi-vidual library across completed datasets is larger. In AUC, Ginger achieves a geometric mean gain of 2 .35 × over ParlayANN, 2 .38 ×

over SVS, 2 .42 × over SymphonyQG, and 2 .79 × over DiskANN. The IVF indexes Faiss and ScaNN trail the graph methods across all datasets, with Ginger achieving a geometric mean AUC gain of 10 .3× over ScaNN and 11 .7× over Faiss.

## 6.3 Ablation Studies 

We now analyze the contributions of Ginger’s main components through ablation studies. We first examine search optimizations, then evaluate NTQ accuracy independently of graph traversal, and finally study how reordering and encoding affect graph size and query speed. 

Search Optimizations. Figure 6 breaks down where Ginger’s speedups come from. At 90% recall, navigating on 4-bit TurboQuant codes instead of float vectors is the largest step, speeding up search by 5 .7× in the geometric mean (up to 7 .2×). Searching on PCA-projected coordinates speeds up search by another 1 .8× in the geometric mean (up to 2 .4× on DBpedia-3072, which has the largest reduction in dimension). The prefilters help to a lesser extent at 90% recall. TurboSlice speeds up search by 1 .26 × in the geometric mean (up to 1 .5×). JL speeds up search by 1 .12 × in the geometric mean (up to 1 .4× on DBpedia-3072), but incurs a 15% slowdown on Wiki-All. TurboSlice beats JL on six of the seven datasets. On Wiki-Qwen, TurboSlice is 1 .27 × faster than PCA at 97% recall, and the two are on par at 99% recall. We attribute this to the fact that the projection is expensive on Wiki-Qwen. PCA and the prefilters both cut memory traffic per candidate, so their gains do not stack. Next, we investigate how much time each phase of the search pro-cedure takes. Figure 7 shows how the runtime balance shifts as di-mensionality increases from 768 (Wiki-Cohere) to 1 ,536 (DBpedia-1536) and 4 ,096 (Wiki-Qwen). Beam search dominates on the first two datasets, while projection accounts for roughly half the run-time on Wiki-Qwen at 95% recall. This reflects the growing cost of multiplying each query by a projection matrix whose size in-creases with both the input and projected dimensions. On larger dimensionalities, we can significantly speed up projection without sacrificing recall by quantizing the matrix entries. Figure 8 shows little benefit from quantizing Wiki-Cohere’s projection matrix, whose float representation occupies only 0 .73 MiB and can fit in L2 cache. The larger float matrices for DBpedia-1536 and Wiki-Qwen occupy 2 .16 MiB and 13 .75 MiB, respectively. Quantization reduces their code storage to 0 .27 MiB and 1 .72 MiB, consistent with im-proved cache residency and the substantial throughput gains on these datasets. ## 7 DISCUSSION & CONCLUSION 

Ginger shows that graph-based vector search still has plenty of room to grow. It breaks new ground in query throughput, mem-ory footprint, and indexing time for ANNS. The key is designing every step of the search around reducing memory traffic. Nested TurboQuant lets a single code serve both fast 4-bit navigation and reranking with near-float accuracy. Graph compression shrinks the graph without slowing down queries. PiPNN completes the picture with fast index construction. 

Looking Ahead. While this paper focuses on single-vector dense embeddings, retrieval applications at Google increasingly go be-yond this setting. We therefore designed Ginger to be flexible for more general retrieval settings. Graph construction and beam search operate only through a user-provided similarity function, abstracting away the specific data representation. This makes it easy to search under novel similarity functions like Chamfer on multi-vectors and black-box similarity functions such as learned relevance scorers. Neural scorer inference is more expensive than computing a dot product, increasing the appeal of the few distance evaluations that graph search needs. We are thrilled to share Gin-ger with the research community and look forward to seeing what others build with it.

## A PARAMETER CONFIGURATION 

For each library, we tune index structure, quantization method, and reranking formats for the highest throughput across the recall range. Every configuration we report is built and evaluated on the full dataset. We sweep query search parameters to extract the Pareto frontier of throughput versus recall. Table 5 summarizes the formats we use for each library, and Table 6 lists the selected parameters for all 14 datasets. Below, we first summarize the settings for graph indexes. For the IVF indexes (ScaNN and Faiss), point clustering, routing structure on top of the clusters, quantization, and reranking interact closely, making them hard to tune, so we describe their tuning in detail below. 

Graph Indexes. All graph indexes use maximum degree 𝑅 = 64 and construction beam width 𝐿 = 128, except on GloVe, where they use 𝑅 = 100 and 𝐿 = 250. We tune the pruning threshold 𝛼 

per dataset, with 𝛼 PiPNN for PiPNN graphs and a single 𝛼 Vamana 

shared by all Vamana-based indexes. Table 5 lists the quantization methods and formats of each graph index. SymphonyQG runs out of memory during index construction on the billion-scale datasets, so we evaluate it only on GloVe and the eight high-dimensional datasets. On high-dimensional datasets ( 𝑑 ≥ 768), we initialize the PCA target dimension for Ginger to the smallest dimension that retains roughly 90% of dataset variance, rounded so that the 4-bit NTQ vector and its bundled metadata fill whole cache lines. Starting from there, we reduce the dimension in increments of whole cache lines to find the optimal throughput–recall trade-off against unprojected vectors. For SVS, we use LeanVec4x8 and follow the same tuning procedure, aligning dimensions directly to whole cache lines as it does not embed metadata into the codes. Table 6 lists the chosen dimensions. 

ScaNN. ScaNN partitions the dataset with a 𝑘 -means tree. We evaluate up to three levels, with 𝐶 1 clusters on the bottom, 𝐶 2 in the middle and 𝐶 3 on the top. Within a leaf cluster, vectors are stored as anisotropic product-quantization codes [37] with 16 centers per two-dimensional subspace (AH2-LUT16). The best candidates retrieved from the leaves are rescored against higher-precision vectors. In high dimensions, we configure ScaNN to route and quantize in a PCA subspace while reranking against full-dimensional vectors. We also enable SOAR [81], an optimization that stores each vector in a second leaf, chosen so that the two assignments err in different directions, which delivers recall improvements. We use the default settings for SOAR ( 𝜆 = 1.5), except on GloVe and Wiki-Cohere ( 𝜆 = 1.0), and on DataComp-DR and DBpedia ( 𝜆 = 2.0). For anisotropic vector quantization, we also use the default weight AVQ = 2.5. Because the default noise-shaping threshold NST = 0.2assumes unit-norm vectors, we scale it proportionally to the average vector norm on non-unit-norm datasets (24 .0 on MS-SPACEV, 100 .0on BigANN, 2 .75 on Wiki-Cohere, and 2 .0 on MS-MARCO). We also tune it to NST = 0.1 on the unit-norm datasets OpenAI-arXiv, DataComp-DR, and DBpedia. Because ScaNN builds its hierarchy bottom-up, we first size the leaf clusters 𝐶 1 through target memory budgets of 2-bit AH codes: roughly 10 KB per leaf on billion-scale datasets, 20 to 64 KB in high-dimensional PCA space, and ≈ √𝑛 on small flat indexes. We train this leaf 𝑘 -means once and build the upper routing levels on the fixed leaf centroids without retraining. To balance centroid routing overhead against leaf scanning, we scale the routing depth with dataset size. Small datasets use flat routing, as scanning all leaf centroids directly is fast. Medium datasets use a two-level tree with 𝐶 2 ≈ √𝐶 1 top-level clusters for a balanced branching factor. Billion-scale datasets use a three-level tree with a uniform geometric branching factor 𝐵 ≈ 𝐶 1/31 . Over these fixed leaf partitions, we evaluate different upper-level routing layouts to select the best configuration without reclustering the dataset. For reranking, we choose the most compact format that pre-serves accuracy: int8 suffices on DBpedia-1536, OpenAI-arXiv, and DBpedia-3072, and is lossless on MS-SPACEV. We rerank with 

bf16 on BigANN (where signed int8 caps recall at 88 .5%), on the 

𝑑 = 768 inner-product datasets (where int8 caps recall at 96%), and on the billion-scale float datasets to halve bandwidth with minimal accuracy loss. At query time, we sweep the number of probed leaves, the num-ber of upper-level clusters visited, and the number of reranked candidates to pick the Pareto-optimal configurations. To choose the upper-level clusters visited, we sweep over them directly on two-level trees. On three-level trees, an independent grid over both upper levels is intractable. Instead, because query latency is domi-nated by memory bandwidth, we set the upper-level probes as a function of the leaf probes so that each routing level scans a fixed ratio of the leaf bytes. We evaluate nine routing profiles across three categories: uniform allocations (spending 0 .5×, 1 ×, or 2 × of leaf bytes at both upper levels), mid-heavy allocations (0 .5×–1 ×

at the root and 1 ×–2 × in the middle), and low-routing allocations (0 .05 ×–0 .1× at the root and 0 .25 ×–0 .5× in the middle). 

Faiss. Faiss composes an inverted file (IVF) index from modu-lar pipeline stages [22], listed in Table 6 in factory notation. The dataset is partitioned into 𝑛 clusters using 𝑘 -means. Within each cluster, vectors are split into 𝑚 subspaces and stored as 4-bit product-quantization codes in a cache-aligned fast-scan layout [4] ( PQ 𝑚 x4fs ). To route queries across large numbers of clusters efficiently, Faiss builds an HNSW graph over the 𝑛 centroids ( IVF 𝑛 _HNSW32 ). To balance routing cost against cluster scanning, we sweep the cluster count 𝑛 around recommended baselines: {1,024, 2 ,048, 4 ,096 }

on small datasets, {16 ,384, 32 ,768, 65 ,536, 98 ,304, 131 ,072, 196 ,608, 262 ,144 } on medium datasets, and {524 ,288, 1 ,048 ,576, 2 ,097 ,152 } on billion-scale datasets. Small datasets use flat routing, as scanning all cluster centroids directly is fast. Larger datasets route through an HNSW32 graph over the centroids. To keep 𝑘 -means training tractable for tens of thousands to millions of centroids, we use top-down two-level clustering on datasets with ≥ 10M vectors. An optional OPQ rotation [32] balances the variance across PQ subspaces and can also project vectors to fewer dimensions. We eval-uate OPQ across different target dimensions, but omit it on GloVe where rotation does not improve accuracy. In low dimensions, we evaluate two- and four-dimensional OPQ subspaces, where two dimensions per subspace with centroid residuals ( PQ 𝑚 x4fsr ) per-forms best. In high dimensions, we sweep OPQ target dimensions in {128 , 192 , 256 , 384 , 512 }, and store uncentered vectors because centroid subtraction degrades throughput–recall performance.
