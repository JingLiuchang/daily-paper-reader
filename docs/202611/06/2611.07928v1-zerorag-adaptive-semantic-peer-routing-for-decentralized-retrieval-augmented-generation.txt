## Abstract 

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) is a standard approach to enhance the reliability of large language model (LLM) outputs by retrieving documents relevant to a query and supplying them in the LLM prompt. Today’s RAG systems collect, index, and serve these documents from centralized stores. In practice, however, documents are owned by organizations, devices, and individuals who cannot or will not surrender custody of them to a central party, e.g., because of privacy, copyright or regulatory compliance. Existing modern decentralized retrieval techniques either reintroduce a coordinating authority, or rely on a global data structure that requires continu-ous network-wide coordination ( e.g., DHTs). We present ZeroRAG, a decentralized routing layer for document retrieval in RAG that performs semantic query routing across an overlay of peers with-out global data structures or centralized orchestration. Documents remain with the peers that own them, and each peer keeps only lightweight local state: a bounded set of neighbor advertisements and a cache of recently routed queries. Using these, ZeroRAG steers each query through a best-first walk that progressively rewires the overlay toward semantically relevant neighbors, so recall improves over time as the network self-organizes. We evaluate ZeroRAG using three real-world workloads and two decentralized baselines. We find that ZeroRAG outperforms baselines in terms of recall and required network messages, approaches the recall and LLM answer accuracy of a centralized document store, self-heals after catastrophic peer failures, and scales to 100 000 peers.

## 1 Introduction 

Large language models (LLMs) have transformed natural language processing because of their strong capabilities across tasks such as text generation, translation, and summarization [10, 56]. One of the most popular applications of LLMs is question answering [64]. In this domain, a fundamental challenge is to ensure the reliability of LLM-generated answers. However, LLMs are prone to hallucina-tions where they confidently generate false or misleading informa-tion [4]. This makes it difficult to trust the outputs of LLMs without double-checking factuality against other sources of truth [27]. These reliability concerns are particularly problematic in high-stakes set-tings, such as healthcare, finance, or law, where inconsistent or fabricated answers can have serious real-world consequences [24]. RAG is a popular approach to improving LLM reliability without retraining or fine-tuning the model [14, 28]. Rather than relying solely on parametric knowledge embedded in the model itself, RAG first retrieves relevant documents from an external source based on the user’s query, then includes them in the prompt before gen-erating a response. Both queries and documents are typically repre-sented as high-dimensional embedding vectors capturing semantic meaning, and retrieval involves finding the embeddings nearest to the query, a process known as nearest neighbor search (NNS) [42]. This allows the LLM to ground its answers in up-to-date or domain-specific information, improving factual accuracy and consistency. arXiv:2611.07928v1 [cs.DC] 5 Nov 2026 Middleware ’26, December 14–18, 2026, Tarragona, Spain Neague, et al. 

Existing RAG systems typically assume that documents are col-lected, indexed, and served from a centralized store under unified administrative control [14, 28, 62]. While this simplifies the RAG pipeline and results in low latency for document retrieval, it is not always a viable or desirable architecture. Data may be inherently distributed across organizations, devices, or individuals who can-not or will not surrender custody of their documents to a central party, whether for reasons of privacy, regulatory compliance, data ownership, or operational boundaries [19, 26]. A hospital network, for instance, cannot pool patient records into a shared index with-out additional access control [46, 55]. Similarly, a federation of research institutions may each hold proprietary datasets they are unwilling to freely share with other parties. Beyond ownership con-cerns, centralizing large, heterogeneous, and continuously evolving document collections introduces infrastructure costs and creates a single point of failure [17, 60, 61]. Despite these limitations, no general-purpose decentralized retrieval infrastructure exists for RAG: existing systems either assume centralized control, or dis-tribute retrieval only within tightly managed federated or cloud settings that still require some coordinating authority. We present ZeroRAG, a decentralized routing layer that en-ables document retrieval by routing queries across a decentralized overlay, without requiring global data structures or centralized or-chestration. Documents remain with the peers that own them and peers themselves are in control whether they want to share doc-uments with the party requesting them. Retrieval with ZeroRAG only uses local coordination. Each peer in ZeroRAG maintains only lightweight local state: compact summaries of the content exposed by neighboring peers and a bounded cache of previously-routed queries, and uses these to guide incoming queries to peers likely to hold documents relevant to that query. This is a key departure from prior decentralized nearest-neighbor systems, which route based only on what peers hold : ZeroRAG additionally exploits what peers have recently asked , referring query issuers toward peers whose recent query history is semantically aligned, not only their local inventory. For example, REMINDIN’ [54] only learns from past re-sponses and INGA [33] routes toward previous query issuers using explicit query topics. This design leaves owners in control of which documents they share, while peers route queries using document and query embeddings and peer referrals. The key technical challenge ZeroRAG solves is effective routing and overlay management without global knowledge or coordina-tion. Rather than consulting a centralized directory or broadcasting queries to all peers, a query in ZeroRAG involves a best-first walk over the overlay that adapts as queries flow through the network while also progressively rewiring peer connections toward seman-tically relevant neighbors. The result is a retrieval layer for RAG whose recall improves over time as the overlay organizes, scales gracefully with the number of peers and documents in terms of communication and computation overhead, and remains resilient to peer churn, without requiring network-wide maintenance. We implement ZeroRAG and evaluate its efficiency and effec-tiveness for decentralized document retrieval in RAG pipelines, using three real-world workloads (AOL-IA [36, 43], Amazon Elec-tronics [3], and PubMedQA [25]) and two decentralized base-lines (Semantica [41] and DHT-LSH). Our experimental evaluation demonstrates that ZeroRAG consistently outperforms other decen-tralized baselines while requiring no globally maintained structure. On the AOL-IA workload, ZeroRAG achieves this at one tenth of the message overhead of the strongest baseline (DHT-LSH). Ze-roRAG approaches the recall and end-to-end RAG answer accuracy of a centralized document store, without any global index, and 99% of queries locate the best supporting document in at most 13 peer contacts. Beyond static performance, our ablation studies show that ZeroRAG self-heals after catastrophic failure in which 50% of all peers crash simultaneously, quickly adapts as new topics are introduced, and scales to networks of up to one hundred thousand peers and documents. We make the following contributions: 

• We introduce ZeroRAG, a decentralized routing layer for RAG that enables document retrieval by routing queries across a decentralized overlay without labeled data, shared data structures, or centralized orchestration (Section 3). 

• We evaluate three real-world datasets, comparing against centralized and decentralized baselines on recall, RAG an-swer accuracy, message efficiency, resilience to peer churn, and topic introduction (Section 4). We show that ZeroRAG matches or exceeds the performance of decentralized alter-natives while scaling gracefully to large peer populations. 

## 2 Background and Problem Description 

Our aim is to construct a decentralized document retrieval layer for RAG pipelines. We first describe the standard RAG workflow and then discuss the technical challenges that this work addresses. 

## 2.1 Centralized RAG Workflows 

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) is a standard and popular approach to improve the factual reliability of LLM outputs by sup-plying external context relevant to the user query directly in the prompt [28]. Whereas conventional language models depend en-tirely on parametric knowledge encoded during pre-training, RAG consults an external data source at inference time, yielding more accurate, up-to-date responses and reducing hallucinations without requiring costly retraining or finetuning. We illustrate the RAG workflow in Figure 1, using an example where a user asks a medical question, and explain the individual operations next. The pipeline operates in two phases. In the offline phase, doc-uments, videos, or other source material are preprocessed: each item is divided into chunks, and every chunk is encoded into a high-dimensional vector by an embedding model [45] ➊, with the resulting embeddings stored in a vector database ➋. For presen-tation clarity, we will refer to chunks as documents throughout this work. In the online phase, a user query ➌ is embedded and forwarded to the retriever ➍, which locates the most semantically similar stored vectors ➎ and fetches the corresponding documents from the vector database ➏. The original query and the retrieved context are then jointly passed to the LLM, which produces a re-sponse grounded in that evidence ➐, yielding a more accurate and context-aware answer than generation without retrieval. The vector database is the central component enabling retrieval. Rather than matching exact keywords, it uses similarity search over high-dimensional embedding spaces. ## 2.2 Data Ownership and Availability 

Our objective is to build a decentralized mechanism for the retrieval of relevant documents. A vast body of works exists surrounding peer-to-peer technologies and information retrieval [47]. Our work challenges two commonplace assumptions of these technologies and RAG: (1) that data can be freely ingested regardless of copyright or access control, and (2) that data must be globally available. 

Copyright and access control. Both peer-to-peer retrieval systems and RAG architectures typically assume that information is generally available; RAG systems in particular oftentimes expect to freely ingest any corpus. We argue that this assumption will collapse under scrutiny when it comes to the intended access control or copyright law [48]. LLMs have been shown to be trained on scraped websites, whereas their terms of service prohibit this, and some even on pirated material, completely circumventing access control [37]. There have also been various court cases, that were lost by the companies behind the technology, where ML algorithms generated copyrighted material verbatim [5]. Strict access control is therefore a hard requirement in RAG systems, particularly in domains where data sovereignty is key, like healthcare or finance. By adhering to purely decentralized interactions, ZeroRAG is compatible with existing access control mechanisms and peers can refuse or allow other peers to access their information at their discretion. 

Global data availability. Major decentralized systems, e.g., Bit-coin [40], BitTorrent [44], and IPFS [11], are built around the assumption that data should be available everywhere, and they solve for this by replicating data across nodes. As previously dis-cussed, replication is not always legal or permissible: a hospital, for instance, cannot freely copy patient records to arbitrary peers. Data has to stay with the peer that owns it and peers make no guaran-tees about availability or access. Rather than solving the replication problem, ZeroRAG routes queries to find data where it already resides, using only local queries and data set descriptions, without replicas of the underlying documents in the network. This comes at a cost: because ZeroRAG maintains no global index, it cannot offer the routing-time guarantees that a system like Kademlia [39] provides, and retrieval is therefore best-effort by design. 

## 2.3 Towards Decentralized RAG 

Our routing protocol should, given a user query, locate non-local owners of target or relevant documents to that query under bounded coordination and without centralized control. Forwarding queries to retrieve documents is a promising approach to decentralizing RAG workflows. This allows peers to perform access control on three lev-els: identity-based (or attribute-based) access control, query-based access control, and view-based (or purpose-based, or context-aware) access control. Our innovation therefore is in the Retriever compo-nent in the RAG workflow (step ➍ in Figure 1), which still receives results from the local vector database, but also combines these with results from (remote) peers in an overlay network. This introduces two new challenges: (1) ZeroRAG must quickly find and query peers in the network that hold relevant documents to a given query, and (2) ZeroRAG must adapt to changes in the available documents over time, e.g., due to peers joining and leaving or updating their local stores, causing semantic drift when the topology becomes misaligned with the current distribution of documents and queries. 

Challenge 1: locality-aware peer sampling. Simply querying all peers in a decentralized overlay ensures maximum recall in terms of document retrieval, but cannot work on Internet-scale networks or data volumes. Instead, each peer should maintain connections to a small set of semantically proximate peers, such that relevant documents can be retrieved from currently connected neighbors alone, without sacrificing recall. This is the goal of locality optimiza-tion: steering peer connections toward neighbors whose document collections are likely to satisfy incoming queries [57]. For example, in a network of hospitals with different specializations, two pedi-atric hospitals are more likely to find similar cases in each other’s documents than in those of hospitals with unrelated specializations. However, in semantically homogeneous settings, such as a network of only pediatric hospitals, all peers hold similar documents, so any neighbor is as good as any other and a locality-aware overlay brings no benefit over random connections. Therefore, we assume a heterogeneous setting. However, locality optimization cannot be treated as a one-time configuration: peers may leave the network, and the documents they hold may change over time, shifting what constitutes a relevant neighborhood. The first challenge addressed by ZeroRAG is maintaining temporally adaptive peer connections that remain semantically relevant as the network evolves. 

Challenge 2: connection reconfiguration. Existing decentral-ized retrieval systems that achieve efficient lookup typically do so by maintaining a globally shared index structure, such as a semantic trie [41] or a distributed hash table (DHT) [39]. These structures require continuous network-wide reorganization as peers join and Middleware ’26, December 14–18, 2026, Tarragona, Spain Neague, et al. 

leave and as document collections evolve. In our setting, where coordination must remain strictly local, maintaining such a global structure is not an option. Thus, the second challenge that Ze-roRAG takes on is to reconfigure neighborhood connections directly 

as queries and documents evolve. Our approach is comparable to how border gateway protocol (BGP) routes packets without a global directory: each router maintains only local routing tables that are updated incrementally as traffic flows through the network, and efficient paths emerge from these local updates alone. 

## 3 ZeroRAG Architecture and Design 

ZeroRAG is a decentralized retrieval layer that routes queries to peers that hold relevant documents. In this section, we first discuss the actors and assumptions, and then peers’ local algorithms for neighborhood maintenance and query answering. 

## 3.1 System Model 

Our system model focuses on query routing; we empirically evalu-ate the accuracy of the downstream RAG answer, but do not model the quality of the citation or the reduction of hallucinations. 

Network and communication. ZeroRAG coordinates decen-tralized document retrieval by maintaining a permissioned decen-tralized overlay network and reconfiguring connections between peers when queries are issued. The incoming and outgoing con-nections by each peer form a directed graph. Peers joining the ZeroRAG network can bootstrap into the network by connecting (outgoing) to several random peers, which can be obtained by using existing peer sampling services [18, 23]. Threats targeted at the network level, like Sybil [13] and Eclipse [21] attacks, are beyond the scope of this paper, but we note that peer sampling mechanisms can overcome these after a cold-boot period of several minutes [52]. ZeroRAG only uses primitive request-response messages (pre-sented shortly), does not maintain shared data structures, or require synchronization steps between peers. Therefore, messages may be asynchronous or drop. Likewise, peer connections can drop at any time, e.g., when a peer goes offline or crashes. We assume the peer sampling mechanism can detect dropped connections and that this can be implemented with minimal overhead using a ping-pong scheme, message-reception acknowledgments and timeouts. Be-yond this, we do not investigate Byzantine behavior of peers, like attempts to bias others’ search results or black hole attacks [1]. 

Documents and embeddings. In ZeroRAG, peers exchange embeddings of documents and queries. The embeddings are used for indexing and network reconfiguration. The transfer of the doc-uments corresponding to those embeddings is outside ZeroRAG. Exchanged embeddings need to be stored by peers and each peer operates a local vector database for this. We assume that peers use the same embedding model 𝜙 𝑒 , so embeddings produced by differ-ent peers remain comparable. As embeddings are local, routing does not depend on centrally assigned labels or a global topic taxonomy, which was the common approach in the late 2000’s [32, 50]. Peers are expected to accrue documents with attribution over time, i.e., a verifiable record of which peer a document originates from, as they exchange them after ZeroRAG matches their embed-dings to queries. The attribution itself is out of the scope of Ze-roRAG and can be implemented with a digital signature. Within the scope of the paper we are interested in routing, not in the manner in which this history is built. Therefore, we shortcut the emergent effect of collecting documents by using a HISTORY() remote pro-cedure call, which gathers the history of accrued documents from neighbors in memory . When considering deployment of ZeroRAG, this can (and should) be replaced with a performant storage layer. 

## 3.2 High-Level Description 

ZeroRAG is built around the insight that if a peer’s neighbors hold semantically similar content, then a query is best served by greed-ily walking toward the most similar peer at each step. To this end, whenever a peer 𝑖 connects to another peer 𝑗 , 𝑗 sends a set of adver-tisements to 𝑖 . These advertisements are compact summaries, in the form of document embedding vectors, of the content 𝑗 locally holds and wants to advertise for routing. This allows owners to retain their documents and decide whether and what to disclose, which is not the same as privacy-preserving retrieval: advertisements, query embeddings, and issuer referrals may reveal information even when documents are withheld. Now, some peer 𝑝 aims to obtain the top-𝑘 

documents that are relevant to a query 𝑞 . To facilitate this search, peer 𝑝 maintains a frontier , which is a ranked list of candidate peers to visit, ordered by their estimated relevance to the query. The best-first walk proceeds by repeatedly popping the highest-scoring peer from the frontier, sending it the query, and collecting its response: a set of relevant local document embeddings together with new 

referrals to that peer’s own neighbors. These referrals are scored against the query and put back into the frontier, extending the search into previously unvisited parts of the network. Meanwhile, peer 𝑝 keeps track of the document embeddings that it has seen. After a fixed number of steps, 𝑝 will inspect the these embeddings and compare their relevance against query 𝑞 . Peer 𝑝 then retrieves the top-𝑘 relevant documents from the peers that offer them. ZeroRAG continuously reconnects the overlay itself as queries are issued. After each query completes, the querier adds a directed edge toward whichever peer returned the most relevant result, evicting the oldest existing connection if the neighbor list is full. This implicit feedback loop causes the overlay to self-organize around semantic affinity: peers that frequently serve useful results for a given topic accumulate more incoming connections, and future queries on that topic are naturally steered toward them earlier in the walk. As the overlay network reconnects, peers are increasingly likely to quickly find the documents they need, without any global coordination or shared data structure. In addition, each peer keeps a bounded routed-query memory: a cache of embedding vectors from queries it has recently responded to, which were issued by other peers. When scoring an incoming query, a peer consults this memory alongside its neighbor advertisements, and can refer the querier not only toward peers with relevant documents but also toward peers that have recently asked similar questions, and are thus likely to be connected to, or themselves hold, relevant content.

## 4.1 Experimental Setup 

We next describe our experimental setup, including our workloads, baselines and implementation details. 

4.1.1 Workloads. We evaluate ZeroRAG and baselines using three real-world workloads: AOL-IA [36, 43], Amazon Electronics [3], and PubMedQA [25]. The three workloads cover complementary retrieval settings: single-target and multi-relevant retrieval, real and synthetic user behavior, and general-domain, e-commerce, and biomedical query distributions. We additionally use PubMedQA for an end-to-end RAG evaluation with ZeroRAG, extending our evaluation beyond routing performance alone. 

AOL-IA provides a query-and-click stream with user ids, query text, clicked documents, and timestamps. The full workload con-tains 657 426 users, 428 157 documents and 2 594 705 total queries. We adapt the workload to our setting by selecting the 10 000 users with the most queries, together performing 2 171 460 queries. Prior to running this workload, we populate the local document stores with the documents that the user has clicked. During our evaluation, we then replay these events in a randomized order by having peers search for the document they are interested in ( e.g., 𝑘 = 1), with-out them looking into their local database first. The single-target retrieval objective (find the exact document) enables us to test the effectiveness of query routing in ZeroRAG with a binary objective score (whether the document is found or not). 

Amazon Electronics is an adaptation of the personalized product-search benchmark introduced by Ai et al. [3]. This bench-mark contains 63 001 product descriptions, and each product con-tains, besides some basic metadata, a category path ( e.g., “Camera, Photo → Digital Camera Lenses”). The workload authors syntheti-cally constructed search queries by taking each product’s category path and flattening it into a query string, stripping stopwords and duplicates. For example, “Camera, Photo → Digital Camera Lenses” becomes the query “photo digital camera lenses.” Thus, queries are categorical and fairly repetitive across users. The queries were then mapped to users using the reviews of purchased products: if a reviewer purchased a product associated with a category-derived query, a corresponding reviewer–query pair is made. In our evaluation, each reviewer in the Amazon Electronics benchmark becomes a peer in the network. Each reviewer–query pair becomes one simulation event, i.e., one search query issued by one peer. The relevant products for that reviewer–query pair become the documents that peer is searching for. The retrieval objective for ZeroRAG is to recover as many relevant products as possible from other peers, rather than to recover a single clicked item. Those documents are distributed across other peers in the network, based on which peers reviewed those products. Because queries come from product categories rather than individual user intent, the same query string will naturally recur across many different reviewers. This means the experiment ends up simulating a setting of repeated, overlapping product-search demand, where many peers are asking semantically similar things at different times. 

PubMedQA is a biomedical question-answering dataset built from abstracts in the PubMed database [25]. We adopt the discrete-answer subset of PubMedQA with 1000 items, in which each item contains a biomedical question, an abstract, and a categorical an-swer ( yes , no , or maybe ). To increase query-level semantic variety, we supplement each original question with five rephrased queries generated using the GPT-5-nano model, resulting in six query variants (the original question and five rephrasings). To assign PubMed documents to peers, we use Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms, assigned to PubMed articles, as biomedical topic labels. We sample a MeSH-level preference vector 𝜽 𝑢 from a Dirichlet (𝜶 ) distribution, where 𝜽 𝑢 defines user 𝑢 ’s preference distribution over all 23 904 MeSH topics. This produces user profiles that are biased toward a small number of topics. We fix 𝜶 = 7×10 −5,where a peer gets on average 15.9 documents on the first topic, 6.6 on the second topic, 3.4 on the third topic and so on. Given a tar-get number of documents 𝑡 , we sample the number of documents assigned to each MeSH topic according to 𝜽 𝑢 . We fix 𝑡 = 30 un-less otherwise stated. We then uniformly sample documents from each selected MeSH topic, and for each document uniformly choose one of its six query variants. Under this user-model, most peers are interested in 1-3 main topics with the interest dropping off for other topics. Each document of a peer 𝑖 also carries one randomly selected query variant. This (peer, document, query) triple defines a simulation event: peer 𝑖 issues the query, and the retrieval objec-tive is to locate the corresponding document on another peer.Each peer issues exactly as many queries as it holds documents. Unless otherwise stated, experiments with the PubMedQA workload con-tain 50 000 simulated peers and 30 queries per peer, leading to 1.5 million query-document events, issued in random sequence. 

4.1.2 Implementation. The experiments were simulated by follow-ing a timeline of ‘events’, one event representing a peer sending a query which aims to retrieve one or more target documents. We fix the contact budget 𝐶 = 50, meaning that a peer contacts at most 50 peers during document retrieval. We used the all-mpnet-base-v2 embedding model for embedding generation of documents and queries [49]. For embedding generation of documents in the AOL-IA workload, we concatenated the title , body , text , and url 

values from the data set using the string “\n\n”. For documents in the Amazon Electronics workload, we concatenated their title ,

description , categories , brand , and price in a similar fashion. The overlay topology is implemented as a global directed con-nection graph, where each graph node represents a peer. Message passing between nodes is implemented using remote procedure calls. Document embeddings are kept in a single unified memory and the local storage of peers are references in that memory. Figures 3, 4, 6, and 7(b) and all ablation tables use five runs per configuration: a randomized starting topology for each run of ZeroRAG, and order of the peers in the tree for Semantica. For matching windows, bins, or table summaries, we compute the five-run mean ¯ 𝑥 and sample SD in Figures 4 and 7(b), and the tables. Figures 3 and 6 instead show the mean with 95% Student-𝑡 

confidence intervals; DHT-LSH was not repeated because its hash functions are fixed, retrieval does not depend on randomized peer connections, and unrepeated baselines have no uncertainty bars. 

4.1.3 Hardware. All simulations were performed using 12 comput-ing nodes which are equipped with two AMD EPYC 7282 processors (16 cores per socket, 2.8 GHz), for 32 physical cores in total. 

4.1.4 Baselines. We evaluate ZeroRAG against two related base-lines that together occupy different parts in the design space. Middleware ’26, December 14–18, 2026, Tarragona, Spain Neague, et al. 

Semantica [41] is the closest prior decentralized semantic re-trieval system and organizes peers with a globally maintained se-mantic trie. We use the source code of Semantica as published in the author’s Github repository. For the hierarchical navigable small world (HNSW) implementation we utilize hnswlib [38]. 

DHT-LSH is a baseline that pairs a DHT ( e.g., Kademlia [39]) with locality-sensitive hashing (LSH) schemes to provide ANN search in fully decentralized settings. DHT-LSH relies on 𝑝 -stable hash functions that map embedding vectors into discrete buck-ets such that semantically similar vectors collide into the same bucket with high probability [12]. To improve recall, DHT-LSH maintains 𝐿 independent hash tables, each using a composition of 𝑘 

such functions with bin width 𝑤 . At indexing time, each document embedding is hashed across all 𝐿 tables, and the resulting bucket identifiers are used as keys in the DHT, which distributes storage of bucket contents across peers. At query time, the query embedding is hashed through the same 𝐿 tables, yielding 𝐿 bucket keys, each of which requires a separate DHT lookup to retrieve candidate doc-uments. We adopt the suggested hyperparameter values from the original LSH paper [12], fixing 𝑘 = 10. We adopted 𝑤 = 3 instead the of the 𝑤 = 4 used by the authors because 𝑤 = 4 placed 44.7% of the AOL-IA documents in the single largest bucket. 

4.1.5 Metrics. Our primary success metric is recall : the percentage of relevant documents retrieved from the entire set available in the network, excluding documents already held locally by the querying peer. For AOL-IA, where each query has a single target document, this reduces to a binary success rate. For Amazon Electronics, where a query may have hundreds of relevant documents, we in-stead report the fraction of available relevant documents retrieved 

within the routing budget. For PubMedQA we additionally measure end-to-end RAG accuracy: the fraction of questions answered cor-rectly when the retrieved top-𝑘 documents are supplied as context. This assesses whether routing quality translates into downstream generation quality. For brevity, in the remainder of this section, we will use the notation recall@k for the recall when the retrieved top-𝑘 documents are supplied as context. Unless stated otherwise, we report recall averaged over batches of 1000 queries to smooth per-query variance and surface trends in routing performance as the overlay evolves over time.

## 4.6 Spreading New Concepts 

Our last experiment shows how fast ZeroRAG spreads a new topic in a network, analyzing how recall evolves for topics which appear anew, and are very sparsely available, but suddenly become popular with all peers. To do so, we omit a single topic from the PubMedQA workload and re-introduce it at the 60% mark into the experiment. We chose a random topic (“Peptostreptococcus”) that has 10 relevant documents, and we vary how many peers own it initially. All peers then continuously send queries to find documents on our chosen (initially omitted) topic. A document starts appearing in other peers’ local data stores only once a query successfully reaches it. Figure 8 shows the rolling recall@10 for both the topic’s queries per initial owner count and the average for other topics as a gray line. Seeding the topic at more owners lets recall recover faster once the concept reappears: with 10 or more owners the documents are immediately reachable across the network and the recall be-comes 1.0 almost instantly, causing the lines to overlap. With less initial owners the documents also spread less fast, with the single-owner case being the slowest, climbing back only as the documents propagate through the overlay once queries begin to reach them. 

Takeaway (Figure 8). ZeroRAG adapts to newly introduced, ini-tially sparse topics through local interactions alone, with its speed governed by how widely the new content is initially held. 

## 5 Related Work 

We discuss works on RAG and nearest-neighbor search, federated and distributed RAG that scale retrieval without removing a co-ordinating authority, and decentralized peer-to-peer retrieval and routing, which is closest to our setting. Table 6 gives an overview. 

Retrieval-augmented generation. RAG grounds LLM gener-ation in external documents retrieved at query time, improving factual accuracy without retraining [14, 28, 62]. Retrieval is typi-cally cast as nearest-neighbor search over high-dimensional embed-dings: queries and documents are embedded into a shared space, and the documents closest to the query are returned [42]. Because exact search is expensive at scale, practical systems rely on ANN indexes such as HNSW graphs [38] or LSH [12, 29]. These tech-niques assume documents are collected and indexed under a single Middleware ’26, December 14–18, 2026, Tarragona, Spain Neague, et al. 

administrative domain, so the index can be built globally. ZeroRAG targets the opposite: when documents remain with their owners and no global index can be constructed. 

Federated and distributed RAG. Federated approaches route or merge results across independently managed collections: RA-GRoute selects which remote sources to query for a given re-quest [19], FeB4RAG benchmarks federated search for RAG [58], and pFedRAG personalizes retrieval models across federated clients [20]. Others target the privacy or efficiency of the re-trieval: FedVSE provides privacy-preserving vector search over fed-erated databases [15], PRAG offers end-to-end privacy-preserving RAG [30], and Proximity caches semantically similar queries to avoid repeated vector-database lookups [7]. A parallel line scales the retrieval substrate itself: DRAGON distributes RAG retrieval across local and cloud resources [31], DGRAG organizes graph-based re-trieval over edge-cloud systems [63], and HARMONY and SPIRE build scalable distributed vector databases for high-throughput ANN search [60, 61]. These systems improve scalability, privacy, or cost, but assume a federated, cloud, or managed deployment in which a coordinating component still orchestrates retrieval. Ze-roRAG instead routes each query through peer-local state alone. 

Decentralized retrieval and routing. The closest systems to ZeroRAG use a decentralized overlay and a local or global state. One family imposes a shared logical structure that must be maintained as peers and documents change. Semantica organizes peers into a global LLM-guided semantic tree overlay [41], while DHT designs such as Kademlia and LSH-based distributed indexes place keys in a global address space for approximate lookup [12, 39]. Blockchain-based designs decentralize control through shared ledger state: D-RAG coordinates decentralized RAG through a blockchain [6], and a related system records a reliability score for each document source on a blockchain so that the trustworthiness of retrieved content cannot be tampered with [34]. These approaches remain decentralized but trade a central authority for global coordination. A second family keeps routing state local but depends on ex-plicit symbolic descriptions. REMINDIN’ routes semantic queries using social metaphors and observations of previously success-ful peers [54]; DRAG/TARW performs topic-aware random walks given global knowledge of the available topics [59]; and SHIMI or-ganizes decentralized agent memory through an explicit semantic hierarchy [22]. These systems share ZeroRAG’s local-state philos-ophy, but rely on topic labels, expertise categories, or maintained hierarchies rather than routing directly from unlabeled embeddings. A third, longer line of decentralized recommendation and nearest-neighbor work anticipates our overlay mechanics. WHATSUP dis-seminates news recommendations through an epidemic protocol that clusters users by interest without any central server [8], and a follow-up obfuscation-based protocol makes such profile-based collaborative filtering privacy-preserving [9]. Most directly, Cluster-and-Conquer constructs a 𝑘 -nearest-neighbor graph by starting from a random overlay and greedily improving it through neighbor-of-neighbor exploration [16], the same locality-exploiting princi-ple ZeroRAG uses to rewire its overlay. These build on classical decentralized overlay techniques such as gossip-based peer sam-pling [23], semantic-proximity content search [57], and the broader literature on robust peer-to-peer search [47]. ## 6 Summary 

We presented ZeroRAG, a decentralized routing layer for RAG that locates relevant documents across an overlay of peers without any central authority, labeled data, or global index. Each peer keeps only a bounded set of neighbor advertisements and a small cache of recently routed queries, routing each query through a best-first walk that continuously reconfigures the overlay toward semanti-cally relevant neighbors. Our evaluation on the AOL-IA, Amazon Electronics, and PubMedQA workloads shows that ZeroRAG con-sistently outperforms Semantica and DHT-LSH, approaches the recall of a centralized HNSW index in an end-to-end RAG pipeline (93.0% vs. 97.3% at comparable accuracy). ZeroRAG recovers to 95.5% recall after losing half the network, scales to 100 000 peers, and absorbs new topics through local interactions alone. These results indicate that decentralization is a practical foundation for RAG, even when documents remain with the peers that own them.
