## Abstract 

Embedding models compress variable-length text into fixed-size vector representations, but this compression can entangle semantic content with residual token-level information, such as artifacts aris-ing from attention sink behavior. We propose a simple method that prepends trainable [DUMP] tokens to the input during embedding model training. The method keeps the model architecture, pool-ing strategy, and training loss unchanged, while optimizing the added tokens through the standard contrastive retrieval objective. By introducing trainable prefix positions that interact with regular tokens during encoding, [DUMP] tokens help reduce the influence of residual or uninformative token-level signals on the final retrieval representation. Experiments on standard retrieval and long-context benchmarks show that our method consistently improves over fine-tuned baselines across encoder and decoder embedding models. Further analysis shows that [DUMP] tokens alter token-level ac-tivation and attention patterns associated with low-information tokens. These results suggest that trainable prefix tokens provide a lightweight way to improve retrieval representations.

## 1 Introduction 

Embedding models have become a fundamental component of mod-ern retrieval systems, where queries and passages are mapped into a shared vector space for efficient semantic matching [12, 19]. Their effectiveness heavily depends on the ability to compress variable-length text into fixed-dimensional representations while preserving the critical semantic information relevant to retrieval across diverse domains. This compression is typically performed through pooling or token-selection strategies [14, 21], effectively forcing the model to funnel rich contextual dynamics into a narrow informational bot-tleneck. However, this process can also introduce representational interference: token-level information that is not directly useful for semantic matching, such as recurrent positional patterns, special-token artifacts, or other non-semantic cues, may be absorbed into the embedding representation and distort its true semantic con-tent [6, 15, 25]. When this noise accumulates, it fundamentally limits the discriminative power of the resulting embeddings in downstream retrieval tasks. This issue becomes particularly important in light of the atten-tion sink phenomenon, where certain positions attract dispropor-tionately large attention despite carrying limited semantic con-tent [25, 28]. Since the final retrieval representation is obtained by pooling or selecting from contextualized token representations, such artificial attention bias can cause non-semantic information to become deeply entangled with content-bearing tokens [8, 33], thereby diluting the overall sequence representation. Standard re-trieval fine-tuning addresses this issue only indirectly: contrastive objectives improve query–passage alignment at the macro embed-ding level, but they do not explicitly control how residual token-level information is represented or routed before pooling. Conse-quently, conventional retrieval optimization may still leave residual token-level information entangled with the embedding representa-tion, leaving a gap in how we optimize dense retrievers. In this work, we focus on how such residual information is han-dled during retrieval encoding by proposing a simple yet highly effective architectural intervention. We use trainable [DUMP] to-kens as input prefixes during embedding model training. These prefix tokens participate in contextualization before the sequence is compressed into a single embedding, acting as dedicated seman-tic buffers. signals, they help reduce the influence of residual token-level in-formation that would otherwise be mixed into the embedding rep-resentation. Crucially, the method does not require modifying the core model architecture, introducing auxiliary losses, or changing the retrieval objective, making it a highly scalable plug-and-play so-lution. Experiments on six diverse embedding models show that our method yields average relative improvements of 2.46% on standard retrieval benchmarks and 2.41% under long-context retrieval set-tings, demonstrating robust generalization. Further analyses show that the number of prefix tokens affects performance in a non-monotonic manner, while token-level inspection indicates that the added prefixes successfully change attention patterns associated with residual or uninformative tokens, effectively routing noise away from the final sequence representation. 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

Dense retrieval models encode queries and documents into fixed-dimensional representations and compare them in a shared embed-ding space to enable highly efficient semantic matching. In practice, these models typically obtain sequence-level embeddings through pooling or token-selection strategies, such as [CLS] , mean, or last-token pooling [2, 8, 14]. Although these strategies provide a simple way to aggregate contextualized token representations, they also create a severe compression bottleneck, as heterogeneous token-level information is collapsed into a single retrieval vector, often causing fine-grained semantic details to be lost. Prior work has recognized this bottleneck in dense passage retrieval and proposed specialized pre-training strategies to improve the quality of the compressed representation [16, 26]. However, while these methods enhance the general representation space, they do not inherently re-solve the internal routing of noisy or uninformative signals during the actual encoding phase. Recent studies on Transformer attention dynamics further sug-gest that not all token-level information contributes equally to the final semantic representation. In particular, the attention sink phenomenon shows that certain tokens can attract disproportion-ately large attention scores despite carrying limited semantic con-tent [10, 20, 25, 28]. Driven by the softmax operation that forces attention allocation [18, 34], such behavior can help stabilize au-toregressive generation, but it inadvertently introduces represen-tational interference in embedding models, where the pooled vec-tor is strictly expected to reflect the pure semantic content of the input. Related observations have also been made in vision Trans-formers, where learnable register tokens are introduced to absorb high-norm outlier information and reduce non-semantic artifacts in feature maps [6]. These studies directly motivate our use of trainable [DUMP] prefix tokens to provide a dedicated structural pathway that absorbs residual or non-semantic information dur-ing retrieval encoding, yielding cleaner and more discriminative sequence representations. 

## 3 Methods 

We use [DUMP] tokens as trainable prefixes during embedding model training, aiming to reduce residual token-level information by providing dedicated slots for the model to route non-semantic attention. The model architecture and retrieval objective remain unchanged. We first describe the encoding process and then present the retrieval training objective. 

## 3.1 Dump-Token Conditioned Encoding

## 4 Experiment 4.1 Experimental Setup 

Training. We implement our method across a diverse set of encoder and decoder architectures. For the encoder models, we utilize gte-en-mlm-base [29], NeoBERT-base [4], EuroBERT-210m [3], and bge-m3-retromae [5]. For the decoder models, we employ ettin-decoder-150m [24] and MobileLLM-R1-140M [31]. For encoder models, we use [CLS] pooling, while for decoder models, we use last-token pooling [30]. We set the number of [DUMP] tokens to 𝐾 = 4 in our main experiments, corresponding to [DUMP1] , [DUMP2] , [DUMP3] ,and [DUMP4] . For training, we construct a dataset of 110k instances, utilizing one negative passage per query. Specifically, we use 100k examples from RLHN [23] to leverage its relabeled hard negatives, which provide cleaner contrastive supervision by mitigating the in-fluence of false negatives. To enhance long-context capabilities, we additionally include 10k instances from MLDR [5], a long-document retrieval dataset constructed from lengthy Wikipedia and mC4 ar-ticles. All models are fine-tuned for one epoch using a maximum sequence length of 8192, a learning rate of 1 ×10 −4, and a contrastive temperature scale of 20.0. Training is conducted with bfloat16 pre-cision and gradient caching [9], maintaining a global batch size of 256 to gather sufficient cross-device in-batch negatives. 

Evaluation. We evaluate retrieval performance using the MTEB framework [17] on a subset of the BEIR benchmark [22]. To rigor-ously test the models’ discriminative capabilities, we specifically utilize the hard-negative versions of Natural Questions [13], Hot-potQA [27], Quora [7], DBPedia [11], and MS MARCO [1]. We additionally evaluate on the English subset of Wikipedia from MTEB [17] to measure performance on a large-scale general-domain corpus. Furthermore, we use LongEmbed [32] to assess long-context embedding performance, where models must effectively compress substantially longer inputs into fixed representations. We report nDCG@10 for all retrieval evaluations.

## 5 Analysis 5.1 Effect of the number of [DUMP] tokens. 

Figure 1 compares retrieval performance when varying the number of [DUMP] tokens, with 𝐾 ∈ { 1, 2, 4}. Adding these tokens generally improves performance over the baseline, in most cases across both models, but the gains are not strictly monotonic. For gte-mlm, using two tokens yields the best average improvement, whereas using four tokens slightly hurts NQ despite improving most other datasets. For MobileLLM-R1, using four tokens achieves the largest average gain, showing a clearer benefit from increasing the number of prefix tokens. Although the best token count varies across models and datasets, using [DUMP] tokens is generally more effective than not using them. These results suggest that the number of prefix tokens acts as a tunable hyperparameter. While additional tokens can improve retrieval performance, increasing the token count does not consistently lead to better results across all settings.

## 6 Conclusion 

We investigated whether trainable [DUMP] tokens can reduce rep-resentational interference in embedding models when prepended to the input text. Our approach preserves the original model archi-tecture, pooling function, and retrieval objective, while allowing the added prefix tokens to be learned through the same contrastive training process. Across retrieval and long-context benchmarks, our method consistently improves over finetuned baselines, showing that learnable input prefixes can enhance retrieval representations without additional supervision or architectural changes. Beyond overall retrieval gains, our analysis indicates that [DUMP] tokens can change how low-information token signals are represented and attended to during encoding, providing evidence that the improve-ment is linked to internal representation behavior rather than only external performance differences. Overall, these findings suggest that trainable prefix tokens offer a simple and effective way to im-prove retrieval embeddings while preserving the underlying model architecture.
