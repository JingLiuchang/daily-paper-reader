# Enhancing Local and Global Structure in Compressed AI Agent Memory 

Henry Han 

Abstract —AI agents store text as embedding vectors and compress them at scale. A compression is accepted when each item’s nearest neighbors survive, but agents also consolidate, delete, and compare memories, tasks that depend on which items lie far apart. We evaluate both ends with two label-free fidelities from our prior work: the local fidelity L at 10 neighbors and the global fidelity G at 30 to 50 percent of the memory. At 96 bytes per passage, rescored binary codes reach an L of 0.97 yet move 10.1 percent of passages to another theme; PCA with 8-bit codes, at an L of 0.77, moves 1.7 percent. Rescoring leaves the stored codes, and thus G, unchanged. We prove that across the G band, 1 − νk is at most 2 to 2.18 times the square root of the near–far rank-violation rate, and that this rate follows the distance noise of the compression (correlation 0.979). Our locality-aware quantization (LAQ) turns the bound into a hinge objective on the codebooks of an OPQ index, keeping its bytes, rotation, and search. To our knowledge, LAQ is the first quantizer whose objective comes from a proven bound on the far end. Reconstruction alone raises G in 0 of 52 held-out runs; the far hinge raises it in all 52. LAQ raises G in all 52 ( p = 8 .5 × 10 −4 over 13 memory–encoder groups), including the external FinDER memory, has the highest G of seven equal-size quantizers in all 12 Multi-Doc-2025 runs, scales to a million passages, and keeps evidence recall. 

Index Terms —Vector quantization, embedding compression, agent memory, neighborhood preservation, rank correlation, product quantization, label-free evaluation.

1 INTRODUCTION 

# LANGUAGE -MODEL agents remember by embedding what they read, say, and do. When they need an item, they recall the nearest embeddings [1]. Managed agent memories extract records from conversations, retrieve them by semantic search, and consolidate them over time [2]. At millions of vectors, every byte per vector costs memory, latency, and money. The vectors are therefore compressed: by truncating Matryoshka embeddings [3], by projection, by scalar or binary codes, or by product quantization [4]. Cloud services offer each of these as a switch [5, 6]. How should an agent memory be compressed so that it keeps both what lies near and what lies far? Today, a compression is accepted by its nearest neighbors. Vendors report that binary embeddings retain 97 to 98.5 percent of retrieval accuracy [5] and evaluate quantized indexes by recall@100 [6]. At 96 bytes per passage on FinQA, rescored binary codes keep L at 0.97 and top-10 agreement at 0.87, yet move 10.1 percent of passages to another theme (Section 6). Embedding compression studies report nDCG@10 [7] or benchmark task scores [8]. Yet an agent also acts on what lies far apart: it consolidates a year of filings into themes [9, 10], deletes every item of a client or a session on request, and maps how companies and sectors relate. Because these operations compare each item with the whole memory, they decide what a sector brief says and what a deletion removes. Keeping this far end is difficult for two reasons: a private agent memory has no labels to test it with, and quantizers are trained for reconstruction, nearest neighbors, or query relevance. Our idea is to measure the far end with the same

Local fidelity L is chance-corrected recall@10. Global fidelity G averages, over 30 to 50 percent of the memory, the agreement on which items lie in each item’s nearer part. At its upper edge, the profile measures agree-ment on the near and far halves, which under a Gaussian copula equals Kendall’s τ of the distances. What is new. To our knowledge, LAQ is the first quantizer trained on the far end of a memory, and its objective comes from a bound we prove. The closest learned quantizers train codebooks with pairwise or triplet losses. PQN uses triplets from class labels [19], and spreading vectors pair a nearest-neighbor triplet loss with an entropy term [20]. DPQ trains product codes end to end via straight-through assignments [21], and JPQ and Distill-VQ train with queries and relevance labels [22, 23]. LAQ differs in target, input, and output. Its target is the rank order at 30 to 50 percent of the memory, selected via our theoretical bound; its input is the unlabeled memory alone; and its output modifies only the codebook values of an OPQ index. We arrived at νk from our DLP curve [18]. Rank-based quality criteria [24, 25] also count kept neighbors across scales and summarize them by an area that weights the near end. To our knowledge, the far end had no proven meaning until our earlier work showed that its midpoint is a rank correlation of distances [17] (Propositions 2–3). This paper takes the far end from a measure to a target. We prove that rank violations bound the profile at every scale (Theorem 1), that under noisy order the profile is fixed by one ratio, the distance noise of a compression against the spread of distances (Proposition 4, Corollary 2), and that at an OPQ start the far hinge falls at no first-order cost in reconstruction (Proposition 5). To our knowledge, no earlier quantizer, learned or not, trains its codes on the far end of this curve. 

Contributions. Our contributions, in order of importance, are: 1) A quantizer derived from a bound. A new rank-violation bound ties the profile at every scale to the order of cross pairs, and a hinge surrogate makes it trainable. A noisy-order proposition and its corollary predict the violation rate and the profile in the G band from one ratio, the distance noise of the compression against the spread of distances. A further proposition shows that, at the OPQ start, the far hinge falls at no first-order cost in reconstruction, and the straight-through steps of training meet its condition at every start. Each result comes with a proof. LAQ turns the bound into a label-free objective on the codebooks of an OPQ index and replaces that index at the same bytes and search. The ablation confirms that the gain comes from the far hinge: reconstruction alone raises G in 0 of 52 held-out runs, the near hinge raises it by 0.0058 on average, and the far hinge raises it in all 52, by 0.0127. 2) Held-out evidence that it works. On held-out companies and conversations across three memories, LAQ raises G in all 40 runs and L in 34. The gain is largest with Amazon Titan V2 [5], where G rises by 0.032 to 0.038 on every split, two to five times the gain with the three retrieval-tuned encoders. On FinDER [26], an external financial memory run once after the recipe was fixed, LAQ raises both fidelities in all 12 runs. On Multi-Doc-2025 it lifts the lowest theme agreement from 0.80 to 0.89 and has the highest G among seven quantizers of equal size. Paired tests confirm the gain: G rises in all 13 memory–encoder groups ( p = 8 .5 × 10 −4, sign test), LAQ has a higher 

G than each of six other quantizers in all 12 Multi-Doc-2025 runs, and evidence recall@10 equals that of OPQ (p = 0 .84 ). On a million passages, one training run takes 175 seconds and a second run reproduces the codebooks bit for bit. 3) Why the gain matters. Each end governs its own operations. Across two agent memories and six encoders, retrieval tracks L (partial rank correlation 0.83 to 0.99 given G). With the retrieval-tuned encoders, consolidation and deletion track G (0.55 to 0.93 given L). Rescoring repairs the near end at query time but leaves the far end of the stored codes unchanged. LAQ applies wherever a vector index also serves cluster-ing, deduplication, or mapping. Section 2 reviews related work. Section 3 presents the mea-surement theory and the bound, and Section 4 introduces the LAQ quantizer. Section 5 describes the memories, encoders, and operations. Section 6 shows what each end measures, and Section 7 evaluates LAQ on held-out memories. 

2 RELATED WORK 

We draw on three lines of research and apply them to agent memory: vector quantization, the evaluation of compressed vectors, and local and global structure. 

Vector quantization. Quantizers compress vectors through subspace decomposition, additive codewords, or binary projections. Product quantization (PQ) splits a vector into subvectors and codes each separately [4], while OPQ learns a rotation to balance variance across subvectors [16]. Resid-ual quantization (RQ) [27] and local search quantization (LSQ++) [28] represent a vector as a sum of codewords, whereas ScaNN weights quantization error by its impact on query inner products [29]. For binary representations, iterative quantization (ITQ) rotates the data before taking signs [30], and RaBitQ generates randomized binary codes with a theoretical error bound on distance estimates [31]. Learned quantizers train codebooks end to end. PQN applies an asymmetric triplet loss on labeled images [19]. UNQ trains a nonlinear encoder for nearest-neighbor search [32]. Spreading vectors combine a nearest-neighbor triplet loss with an entropy regularizer [20]. DPQ trains product quanti-zation end to end via soft assignments and straight-through estimators [21]. In dense retrieval, JPQ, RepCONC, and Distill-VQ optimize quantizers using queries and relevance labels or teacher rankings [22, 23, 33]. Unlike these methods, our proposed LAQ trains codebooks for far-end rank order using the unlabeled memory alone. 

Evaluation of compressed vectors. Quantized indexes are typically evaluated by nearest-neighbor recall [6, 34], re-trieval quality [5, 7], or downstream task scores [8]. Kisako et al. [8] conjecture that both clustering and retrieval depend on neighborhood geometry, and Kuffo and Boncz [35] find that one-bit codes support the internal clustering an index uses to partition its vectors. Earlier, Turaga et al. [36] showed that fitting a moment-preserving quantizer per cluster pre-serves a known k-means partition. In contrast, our measure 4

asks whether the compressed memory keeps both the partition and the global arrangement of the uncompressed memory, with no partition given in advance. 

Local and global structure. Rank-based quality criteria evaluate neighborhood preservation across scales [24, 25]. Lueks et al. [37] split the co-ranking curve into local and global regimes, and recent visualization methods such as DREAMS and CMET balance or score both ends [38, 39]. Global embedding structure is also commonly scored by pairwise distance correlation and triplet accuracy [40, 41]. In our prior work, we introduced the degree of locality preservation [18]. In a subsequent study [17], we defined the local fidelity L, global fidelity G, and entity fidelity on its chance-corrected profile and proved that the profile’s midpoint converges to a rank correlation of distances. We also derived the subsample estimator of G and applied these fidelities to two-dimensional maps and compressed agent memory. Building on that measurement framework, the present paper proves which pairwise order controls the profile, turns the resulting bound into a trainable quantizer, and evaluates both ends on held-out memories. 

Agent memory. Benchmarks such as LoCoMo [15] and LongMemEval [42] evaluate whether agents can recall facts from long interactions. Agent memory systems consolidate new records against the most similar stored entries [2], while corpus-level frameworks cluster embeddings into themes and summaries [9, 10, 43]. Whereas these systems com-press memory at the text level through summarization and consolidation, we study the compression of the underlying embedding vectors and the memory-wide operations that depend on them. 

3 TWO -END FIDELITY OF A COMPRESSED MEMORY 

One chance-corrected profile measures both ends of a compressed memory, while the relative order of near and far items controls this profile at every scale. This section defines the profile, proves what its two ends measure, defines the local and global fidelities L and G, and proves the bound that LAQ optimizes in Section 4. 

3.1 Setting 

A memory holds n items with vectors x1, . . . , x n ∈ Rd,where d is the embedding dimension. A compression maps each xi to a stored code. We write zi for the vector with which the memory computes distances: the decoded vector for scalar, PCA, and product codes, or the ±1 vector for binary codes compared by Hamming distance. All other distances are Euclidean. Let N = n − 1. For item i, let 

rxi (j) ∈ { 1, . . . , N } be the rank of item j̸ = i among the other items by increasing distance to xi, with ties broken by index, and let rzi (j) be the corresponding rank among the compressed vectors. For 1 ≤ k ≤ N −1, the k-neighborhoods are N xk (i) = {j : rxi (j) ≤ k} and N zk (i) = {j : rzi (j) ≤ k}.

νk there. The model rests on a normal approximation of each anchor’s distances. In the G band, these distances are sums over many directions and lie in the bulk of their distribution. The independence of the residuals also holds approximately: items that share a codeword have correlated errors, and the noise varies across items. 4 LOCALITY -AWARE QUANTIZATION 

Locality-aware quantization (LAQ) trains the codebooks of an OPQ index to keep the order of cross pairs at the middle and far scales, the target identified by Theorem 1. Whereas product quantizers learn codebooks to reconstruct vectors or keep nearest neighbors, LAQ adds this far-end target, needs no labels, and keeps the rest of the OPQ index intact. What LAQ changes. LAQ modifies the codebook values and nothing else: the PCA basis, rotation, bytes per item, and asymmetric distance computation (ADC) search all remain identical to those of OPQ. An LAQ index therefore replaces an OPQ index directly and adds no operation at query time. A single recipe serves every memory and encoder: tmax =1,000 steps, λmid = λfar = 1 , and η = 1 (Appendix B). 

Complexity. Let n be the number of training items, d

the dimension, K = 256 the codebook size, B = 2 ,048 the number of cross pairs per step (1,024 anchors in each of the two groups), and tmax the number of steps. Ranking the training memory once costs O(n2(d + log n)) time and 

O(n2) memory. Each step encodes all items with the current 8

codebooks in O(nKd ) time and evaluates the hinge on B

cross pairs in O(Bd ) time. Training therefore costs O(n2(d +log n) + tmax (nKd + Bd )) time in total, while the query cost and the encoding of a new item, O(Kd ), remain those of OPQ. The n × n matrix of 7,336 to 7,672 items fits on one 48 GB GPU, and the 1,000 steps take 35 to 112 seconds on Multi-Doc-2025. For a memory of a million items, a scalable variant ranks against m = 8 ,192 reference items at each step and decodes only the items used in that step, with a reconstruction batch of b = 16 ,384 . This reduces the per-step cost to O(Bm (d + log m) + ( B + b)Kd ) and memory to 

O(nd + Bm ) (Section 7.5). 

5 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

We evaluate the two fidelities across five memories and six encoders, and test LAQ on held-out splits of four memories as well as on a million-passage memory. 

Memories. Table 1 lists five memories: three agent memories and two topic corpora. FinQA, LoCoMo, and the topic corpora serve the measurement, and Multi-Doc-2025 and two additional memories test LAQ. FinQA [14] contains pages from the annual reports of S&P 500 companies paired with numerical questions annotated with supporting facts. We retain the 106 companies in the current index and up to three report pages per company. Every sentence and table row is one item, prefixed by the company name and fiscal year. This yields 8,356 items across 11 Global Industry Classification Standard (GICS) sectors and 863 ques-tions. Multi-Doc-2025 [11] is a financial question-answering benchmark spanning 179 10-K filings from 89 S&P 500 companies for fiscal years 2022 to 2024. Each paragraph and table row serves as a candidate item with the same prefix. Every filing retains the passages containing its questions’ evidence plus a random sample, up to 56 passages, yielding 10,024 items and 898 questions with located evidence. Multi-Doc-2025 is used to test LAQ. LoCoMo [15] is a long-term conversational memory benchmark for agents, comprising ten conversations of 19 to 32 sessions, 5,882 turns, and 1,536 answerable questions annotated with their evidence turns. Each turn, prefixed by its speaker, forms one item. 20 Newsgroups [48] and AG News [49] contribute 10,000 random documents each, spanning 20 and 4 topics, respectively. A sixth memory, FinDER [26], is reserved for testing LAQ: it pairs 5,703 queries written by financial professionals with expert-selected evidence from the 10-K filings of 497 S&P 500 companies. Following our Multi-Doc-2025 construction, we keep up to 30 passages per filing. Because FinDER provides the evidence text without specifying its company, we locate each excerpt in the filing that contains the most of its rarer words. When multiple filings match, the company named in the query breaks the tie, and any excerpt located in a company other than the one named is dropped. Taking the one or two passages in that filing whose words appear in the excerpt as the evidence passages yields 14,949 items and 5,129 questions. Finally, a seventh memory comprising 1,000,000 passages from MS MARCO [50] tests LAQ at scale (Section 7.5).

6 WHAT THE TWO ENDS MEASURE 

Each end of the profile predicts its own operations: retrieval follows L, consolidation and deletion follow G,and compressions of equal size can keep very different far ends. Section 7 tests whether LAQ raises the far end at the same bytes. 

6.1 Compressions separate on the (L, G ) plane 

Compressions of equal size keep different ends of a memory. Figure 2a places every compression of FinQA with BGE-base on the (L, G ) plane; in Appendix C, Figure 5 shows both agent memories with the retrieval-tuned encoders, and Figure 6 shows the other three encoders. Within a given memory, the two ends correlate across compressions (Spearman 0.81 to 0.95 over the 18 runs) because fewer bytes lower both. Yet compressions with the same L can differ widely in G. With the retrieval-tuned encoders, half precision and 8-bit scalar codes keep both ends at 0.97 or above. PCA, PCA with 8-bit codes, and product quantization keep G within 0.12 of L, whereas binarization after PCA and a random rotation places G 0.06 to 0.22 below L. Among the linear compressions, rescored binary codes fall furthest below the diagonal, by 0.21 to 0.41. At 96 bytes per passage on FinQA with BGE-base, they keep L at 0.97 but move 10.1 percent of the passages to another theme. 6.3 Rescoring repairs the near end and keeps the stored codes 

Rescoring repairs the near end while leaving the far end tied to the stored codes. Disk-based vector search keeps binary codes in memory and rescores candidates using full-precision vectors read from disk; Figure 2f, g reproduces this pipeline using the 40 nearest codes. On FinQA, rescoring raises L from 0.74 to 0.97 with BGE-base, from 0.61 to 0.87 with E5-base, and from 0.77 to 0.98 with BGE-large. It lifts top-10 agreement from 0.57 to 0.87, 0.30 to 0.53, and 0.62 to 0.90. Yet G remains fixed at the value of the binary codes, as does every operation computed on them. Theme agreement on the financial memory stays at 0.79, 0.63, and 0.82, and LoCoMo session deletion stays at 0.56, 0.35, and 0.57. Judged by a recall check, rescored binary codes appear close to the uncompressed memory; judged by G, they keep only the far end of plain binary codes. The same pattern holds for every code family that preserves Euclidean distances (Table 7). 

Evidence and fiscal year. With the retrieval-tuned encoders, evidence recall follows L, with partial correlations of 0.71 to 0.83 given G on FinQA and 0.61 to 0.87 on LoCoMo (Figure 9c in Appendix C). Because financial text repeats across years, a retrieved passage can come from the right company but the wrong fiscal year. In the uncompressed memories with the retrieval-tuned encoders, 0.18 of the right-company passages come from another fiscal year, and every linear compression keeps this share between 0.15 and 0.24 (Figure 9d).

These gains hold on FinDER, a memory built by others from the queries of financial professionals. 7.5 A memory of a million passages 

LAQ also raises G on a memory of one million passages. The memory holds 1,000,000 passages of MS MARCO [50], encoded with BGE-base and stored at 96 bytes each. OPQ and LAQ were fitted on 100,000 other passages, and the 6,980 development queries search the memory by ADC. At this size, training draws a new subsample of 8,192 reference items at each step and chooses the cross pairs by their ranks within it, the argument behind the estimator of G. It decodes only the items a step uses and averages the reconstruction term over 16,384 items. All other settings are those of the recipe. Over three seeds, LAQ raises G from 0.774 to 0.784 to 0.785. The gain of 0.010 to 0.011 is about three times the standard deviation of G over subsamples (at most 0.0039). 

L, computed on the 10 nearest neighbors of 20,000 random passages, stays within 0.002 of OPQ. Top-10 agreement is 0.818 for OPQ and 0.818 to 0.820 for LAQ. Evidence recall@10, over the 820 queries whose relevant passage is in the memory, is 0.860 for OPQ and within 0.008 of it for LAQ. One training run takes 175 seconds and at most 10.0 GiB of one 48 GB GPU, and a second run reproduces the codebooks bit for bit. 

8 DISCUSSION 

Why the far end can be learned. Ordering cross pairs controls the far end, and this is what makes LAQ work. The bound has a leading factor near 2 across the G band and near pN/ 10 at k = 10 . Reconstruction and nearest-neighbor objectives serve the near end. LAQ combines the two through a hinge loss at middle and far scales, a normalized reconstruction term for the near end, and a checkpoint selected on the far end of a monitor set. The ablation of Section 7 isolates the far hinge: reconstruction alone keeps the OPQ start, and Lfar alone gives the full gain in G in all 52 runs (Figure 4). The gain is largest where the OPQ codes keep the least of the far end, as with Titan V2, and it reaches the operations that follow G.

Cost against other quantizers. LAQ trains in minutes, between PQ and the additive quantizers, and keeps the query cost of OPQ. Each LAQ step costs about one k-means iteration of PQ and OPQ [4, 16], plus a one-time ranking of the training memory. In contrast, additive quantizers spend their cost on encoding. RQ keeps several partial codes per item in a beam search, and LSQ++ encodes vectors by joint local search over all codebooks [27, 28]. ITQ alternates a 

d × d rotation with sign codes [30], and RaBitQ applies a random rotation without training [31]. On the Multi-Doc-2025 training memories, the faiss implementations [59] on CPU took 6 to 12 seconds to fit PQ, 146 to 517 seconds for ITQ, 511 to 1,193 seconds for RQ, and 8.2 to 17.1 hours for LSQ++. By comparison, LAQ took 35 to 112 seconds on one GPU after its OPQ start. LAQ and OPQ encode a new item in O(Kd ) time, while RQ and LSQ++ repeat their search for every item added to the memory. LAQ thus has the highest 

G among these quantizers (Table 3) at a training cost between PQ and the additive quantizers. 

What to report. A compressed memory can be reported with (L, G ) against the memory it replaces, computed on the operator’s own vectors. L says whether queries return the same items. G says whether the memory keeps its far end: which items are consolidated or deleted together, and how sessions, companies, and themes relate. Neither needs labels, and G is estimated from a subsample of 2,000 items. Where a memory serves consolidation or deletion, G belongs in its acceptance test. The compression is then chosen as the one with the highest G among those with the required L at the bytes available. Theorem 1 adds a diagnostic: the violation rate of cross pairs at a scale shows which part of the profile a compression loses. 

Financial memory. The consequences are clearest in financial memories. A consolidation that groups filings differently changes the sector briefs an analyst agent writes. A deletion that selects different passages changes what a client request removes. Peer groups, the information barrier, and the company map compare company centroids. A check of these operations combines L with agreement measured on the firm’s own companies. 

Extensions. Residual and additive codebooks are natural next targets for this objective, and sampling reference items already extends training to one million passages (Section 7.5). 

9 CONCLUSION 

Agent memories are compressed and accepted by their nearest neighbors, even though agents also consolidate, delete, and map what they hold. One label-free profile measures both ends. Its near end predicts retrieval, and with retrieval-tuned encoders its far end predicts consolidation and deletion, each beyond the other. Our main result is that the order of near and far items bounds the profile at every scale, and that this bound can be trained. Locality-aware quantization (LAQ) learns this order and raises the global fidelity of every held-out memory at the same bytes and search as the OPQ index it replaces. To our knowledge, LAQ is the first quantizer whose objective comes from a proven bound on the far end of a memory. It needs no labels and no change at query time, and it applies wherever embeddings are compressed and then clustered, deduplicated, or mapped. For agent memory, (L, G ) gives an acceptance test for both ends, and LAQ gives a way to pass it. Data and code: https://github.com/hank08819/LAQ

APPENDIX BLAQ RECIPE 

Every LAQ run uses one recipe: M = d/ 8 subspaces of 8 dimensions with 256 codewords each, the full PCA basis, 1,000 steps, 1,024 anchors per scale group and step, λmid = λfar = 1 , margin η = 1 , Adam with a learning rate of 0.002 times the standard deviation of C0 and cosine decay, temperature from T0 to 0.05 T0, a monitor set of 2,000 training items checked every 250 steps, and a reconstruction limit of 1.15 for a checkpoint.
