Abstract 

Long-horizon LLM agents often exceed their context windows, forcing them to discard in-formation that may be needed later. Existing context management strategies rely on summa-rization, masking, or structured memory, requir-ing decisions about what to preserve or how to retrieve it before future needs are known. How-ever, these methods can introduce lossy com-pression, potentially discarding details needed for future tasks. To address this limitation, we introduce G REP MEM , a simple, lossless, and index-free memory mechanism built on two components: an append-only plain-text log of the agent’s complete history and a regular-expression search tool for on-demand recall. Unlike structured memory systems, G REP -MEM requires no embeddings, retrieval index, memory extraction, or additional models. We train Qwen3.5-9B with reinforcement learning under five context management strategies and evaluate on multi-question research sessions, long-stream recall, and agent-trajectory ques-tion answering. G REP MEM achieves 95–97% accuracy on ContextBench, versus at most 21% for lossy baselines, and outperforms SUPO’s summarization by 8.8 percentage points on BCP-Multi. G REP MEM scales effectively to increasingly long input streams while remain-ing on the accuracy–token-cost Pareto fron-tier across all five evaluation tasks. Our re-sults show that a plain-text log and grep pro-vide a simple but competitive approach to long-horizon agent memory, without requiring com-plex structured memory infrastructure.

An identifier or fact encountered early may become relevant only after several later searches, making it difficult to decide which de-tails to retain when reducing context. Keeping the entire trajectory in context is expensive and eventu-ally exceeds the window; even within that window, models do not reliably use all available informa-tion (Liu et al., 2024). Therefore, the challenge is not only to manage the working context but also to preserve the ability to recover previously observed information on demand. Existing approaches address this challenge through compression or external memory but can incur additional computational overhead while risk-ing the loss of important information needed for 1arXiv:2611.25123v1 [cs.CL] 18 Nov 2026 future tasks. Yet unlike human memory, digital storage allows agents to preserve complete interac-tion histories beyond the limited context window at relatively low cost. Recent studies further suggest that simple lexical search over raw histories can rival structured memory systems (Li et al., 2026a; Sen et al., 2026), offering a lightweight way to recover past information on demand. Taking this idea to its logical extreme, we revisit the bound-ary between short-term context management and long-term memory in LLM agents: can a complete digital record and a simple search tool provide ef-fective agent memory without a structured store or retrieval index? 

We introduce G REP MEM , which separates the agent’s bounded working context from its persistent history (Figure 2). We build on the summarization loop of SUPO (Lu et al., 2026), a reinforcement learning (RL) algorithm that jointly optimizes tool use and context summarization. When the context reaches a length threshold, the agent summarizes its interaction history and resumes from the initial prompt and the summary. G REP MEM extends this loop by automatically appending every message and tool output to a persistent plain-text log. After the first compaction, the agent can retrieve earlier observations through a single tool, grep_history ,which searches the log using regular expressions and supports both retrieving and counting matching lines. In G REP MEM , summaries provide context for ongoing reasoning, while the lossless log pre-serves all details omitted during compaction. No embeddings, retrieval index, memory extraction, or additional models are required. We train Qwen3.5-9B (Qwen Team, 2026) under five context management strategies: ReAct (Yao et al., 2023), observation masking (Lindenbauer et al., 2025), SUPO (Lu et al., 2026), MemGPT-style memory (Packer et al., 2024), and G REP -MEM (Table 1) using the same RL recipe, train-ing data, and task tools (§4). We evaluate each strategy before and after RL training on multi-question research with BCP-Multi, constructed from BrowseComp-Plus question triples (Chen et al., 2026); long-stream recall on ContextBench (Shao et al., 2026); and agent-trajectory question answering on AMA-Bench (Zhao et al., 2026). To-gether, these settings test both retaining findings for known questions and recovering details for ques-tions revealed later. Overall, G REP MEM improves accuracy while remaining on the accuracy–token-cost Pareto fron-tier for all five evaluation tasks (§5.4, Figures 1 and 5). Its greatest advantage emerges when exact details must remain accessible beyond the active context window. G REP MEM achieves 95–97% ac-curacy on ContextBench, compared with at most 21% for SUPO’s summarization (Lu et al., 2026) and observation masking (Lindenbauer et al., 2025). On BCP-Multi, it improves accuracy by 8.8 per-centage points over SUPO and 7.0 over MemGPT. These gains come at competitive token costs: on ContextBench, G REP MEM uses only 29% as many tokens as MemGPT while achieving substantially higher accuracy on Log Triage. Our contributions are threefold: • Lossless memory without an index. We in-troduce G REP MEM , which pairs lossy context summarization with a lossless trajectory log pre-serving the full interaction history, enabling the agent to recover omitted details through a single search tool, grep_history (Figure 2). • Pareto-optimal accuracy–token-cost trade-offs. We compare five context management strategies before and after RL training across three complementary settings. G REP MEM lies on the accuracy–token-cost Pareto frontier across all five evaluation tasks, achieving higher accu-racy than summarization-based baselines at com-petitive token costs (Figures 1 and 5, Table 3). • Scalable and accurate recall over long histo-ries. GREP MEM maintains high accuracy on ContextBench’s KV Store and Log Triage tasks (Table 2) even as input streams grow far beyond the model’s context window, enabling accurate and scalable long-horizon recall without complex memory infrastructure (Figure 4).

3 GREP MEM 

3.1 Bounded Context, Persistent History 

As discussed in the introduction, memory is es-sential for long-horizon agents, yet existing ap-proaches face a trade-off: context compression risks losing information beyond the context win-dow, while long-term memory systems often in-troduce additional complexity in memory retrieval. These limitations highlight the potential of com-bining context compaction with simple, long-term access to the complete interaction history to address both information loss and retrieval complexity. GREP MEM revisits the relationship between short-term context management and long-term memory by maintaining a bounded working con-text alongside a lossless interaction history. Algo-rithm 1 summarizes the overall procedure in detail. Building on the summarization loop of SUPO (Lu et al., 2026), our context compaction mechanism generates a summary when the working context approaches its limit, allowing the agent to resume from the original prompt and summary. Mean-while, an append-only log preserves all interac-tions, including messages, tool calls, outputs, and summaries, making the interaction history lossless :nothing is discarded, even after the working con-text is compacted. Together with on-demand grep retrieval, this gives the agent a compact working context and a complete, searchable history (Fig-ure 2). During an episode, the agent iteratively gen-erates responses and invokes task-specific tools to interact with its environment. Each tool call returns an observation to the working context, while the harness appends the agent’s messages, tool calls, and outputs to the persistent history log. These two representations serve complementary purposes: the working context supports ongoing reasoning through summarization, while the lossless history enables on-demand recovery of details omitted dur-ing compaction. Appendix B describes how sum-marization input fits within the context window. 

3.2 On-Demand Recall with Grep 

After the first compaction, grep_history becomes available during subsequent task execution. The agent decides when to invoke it, subject to the inter-action budget. The tool searches the persistent his-tory log using case-insensitive regular expressions. By default, it returns matching lines in chrono-logical order, with surrounding context and their original locations in the history, enabling recovery of details omitted during compaction. To limit response size, results are paginated, and the agent can request additional pages to access the remaining matches. The tool also supports a count-only mode that reports the total number of matching lines without returning their contents. Be-cause retrieval relies on regular-expression match-ing rather than semantic similarity, the agent must formulate search patterns that match relevant text in the history log. These patterns may be simple keywords, such as names or identifiers, or more complex regular expressions. Appendices A and B give the tool schema, output limits, and examples. 

3.3 Learning with Reinforcement Learning 

Since G REP MEM preserves complete histories and enables on-demand retrieval, effective memory use requires the agent to decide when and how to re-4trieve relevant information. We therefore use re-inforcement learning to teach agents these capa-bilities by jointly optimizing task actions, context summarization, and memory retrieval through a shared episode-level reward, without separate su-pervision for summaries or grep queries. We evalu-ate G REP MEM both before and after RL training, with details provided in Section 4 and Table 3. 

4 Experimental Setup 

We evaluate GREP MEM with four baselines across five tasks spanning multi-question research, recorded-trajectory analysis, and recall over long input streams. GREP MEM achieves the highest average accuracy both before and after RL train-ing (Table 3) and lies on the accuracy–token-cost Pareto frontier across all five tasks (§5.4). 

4.1 Context Management Strategies 

We compare five context management strategies, summarized in Table 1. ReAct (Yao et al., 2023) retains full history until the context limit is reached. 

Masking (Lindenbauer et al., 2025) replaces older tool observations with placeholders. SUPO (Lu et al., 2026) summarizes and resumes the con-text. MemGPT-style memory (Packer et al., 2024) combines a notepad with searchable message his-tory and archival memory. 4.2 Training and Shared Settings 

To ensure a fair comparison, we separately train Qwen3.5-9B (Qwen Team, 2026) with thinking enabled under each context management strategy using the same GRPO-based RL recipe (Shao et al., 2024). All strategies use a 32,768-token context window and the same interaction budgets. Our training set contains 4,000 episodes: 70% single-question OpenResearcher tasks (Li et al., 2026b), 15% three-question sessions constructed from the OpenResearcher training split, and 15% synthetic ContextBench-style streams (Shao et al., 2026). This mixture targets complementary capa-bilities: research and tool use in single-question tasks, retention of findings across questions in multi-question sessions, and precise lookup and counting over long histories in synthetic streams. 4.3 Evaluation Tasks 

We evaluate G REP MEM on BCP-Multi , AMA-Bench file and stream (Zhao et al., 2026), and 

ContextBench (Shao et al., 2026), as summa-rized in Table 2. These tasks are distinguished by whether questions are revealed before or after the relevant input. When questions are known in advance, agents can use them to guide retention and search. This setting tests whether agents can retain findings while investigating multiple research ques-tions (BCP-Multi) or search recorded trajectories with a shared search tool (AMA-Bench file). When questions are revealed after the input, agents must process the input without knowing future needs, so answering depends on whether relevant details re-main available in the working context or, for strate-gies supporting retrieval, through memory search (AMA-Bench stream and ContextBench). 

Questions provided before the input. BCP-Multi extends single-question evaluation to test retention across research questions. We group three questions into one episode so that findings from earlier searches must remain accessible while the agent investigates other questions and pre-pares a joint final answer. We construct it from BrowseComp-Plus (Chen et al., 2026) by shuffling its 830 BrowseComp questions (Wei et al., 2025) with a fixed seed into 276 disjoint triples, omitting the remaining two questions. All three questions are provided together in the initial prompt and are answered together within 60 turns over the origi-nal 100K-document corpus. The agent retains its context across all three questions in each episode. 

AMA-Bench file uses recorded agent trajectories and associated question–answer pairs provided by the benchmark (Zhao et al., 2026), with the source trajectories kept outside the model’s working con-text and accessible through grep_trajectory . We 5evaluate 208 trajectories, with the agent answer-ing all 12 questions for each trajectory in a sin-gle session with a shared budget of 60 turns. We provide all context management strategies with 

grep_trajectory , a tool that searches recorded actions and observations using regular expressions and returns matching lines grouped by step. Unlike 

grep_history , which searches the agent’s own in-teraction history, grep_trajectory searches the benchmark-provided fixed source trajectory. 

Questions revealed after the input. Con-textBench (Shao et al., 2026) evaluates whether agents can recall information from long sequences of key–value records or service logs. The agent receives these records incrementally, then answers 24 questions about them. KV Store requires retriev-ing exact values, while Log Triage requires finding specific log entries and counting entries that sat-isfy given conditions. We evaluate input sequences ranging from 33K to 968K tokens across 11 length levels, with four random seeds per level. AMA-Bench stream evaluates question answering over recorded agent trajectories presented incrementally. Unlike AMA-Bench file, which stores the source trajectory as a searchable file, AMA-Bench stream feeds each trajectory into the agent’s context in suc-cessive chunks of approximately 12K characters. We evaluate 189 trajectories, each containing at most 128K tokens. After the agent has processed an entire trajectory, we save its final context and memory state. Each of the trajectory’s 12 questions is then answered independently, starting from an independent copy of the final context and memory state. Both settings hide questions during input processing, preventing question-guided retention. 

4.4 Metrics and Reporting 

We evaluate the model before and after RL training. OpenResearcher, BCP-Multi, and AMA-Bench an-swers are graded by gpt-5.6-sol (OpenAI, 2026) against references; ContextBench uses its official programmatic graders. We report 95% confidence intervals for accuracy. These intervals reflect vari-ation across test items, not across training runs. We report total token cost across all model calls, including summarization and recall (Appendix E). 

5 Results and Analysis 

We evaluate the effectiveness of different context management strategies in terms of accuracy, token cost, and how their performance changes with RL training. 5.1 Where Does G REP MEM Improve Accuracy? Questions known before the input. On BCP-Multi, the RL-trained G REP MEM achieves 46.9% accuracy, outperforming SUPO by 8.8 percent-age points and MemGPT by 7.0 points. Al-though all three questions are provided in the initial prompt, the agent must retain findings from earlier searches while investigating subsequent questions. SUPO and G REP MEM average 25–27 context com-pactions per episode, testing whether these findings remain accessible after repeated summarization. GREP MEM can recover findings omitted during compaction by searching its interaction history. On AMA-Bench file, by contrast, all strategies can re-visit the original trajectory through a shared search tool, while all strategies except ReAct perform sim-ilarly, suggesting that searching the agent’s own history provides the greatest benefit when the origi-nal information is no longer directly accessible. 

Questions revealed after the input. The largest gains over lossy baselines occur on ContextBench, where KV Store and Log Triage require exact val-ues or individual log entries that summarization can discard, making them well suited to lossless recall. After RL, G REP MEM achieves 97.4% accuracy on KV Store and 95.3% on Log Triage, compared with just 6.6–20.7% for SUPO and Masking. How-ever, revealing questions after the input does not by itself make lossless recall beneficial. On AMA-Bench stream, all strategies except ReAct differ in accuracy by at most 1.2 percentage points after RL, and G REP MEM ’s lead over SUPO narrows from 4.1 points before RL to 0.9 points after RL. Even on trajectories exceeding the context window, the gain is not statistically significant. Unlike Con-textBench, many AMA-Bench stream questions concern causes or state changes that summaries can preserve, rather than exact values or log entries. 5.2 How Does RL Affect Context Management Strategies? 

As shown in Table 3, RL improves the average accuracy of most context management strategies, with G REP MEM gaining 5.9 percentage points to achieve the highest post-RL average accuracy of 66.4%. The largest improvement occurs on Con-textBench Log Triage , where accuracy increases by 20.3 points since it requires lookup and counting over service logs. RL may improve query formu-lation and evidence aggregation, enabling G REP -MEM to retrieve and utilize historical information more effectively. On ContextBench KV Store ,accuracy increases by 3.5 points. Since this task re-quires exact-value retrieval for a given key, RL may help G REP MEM formulate more effective search queries. These tasks require reasoning about state transitions and causal rela-tionships across agent trajectories, which retrieval optimization alone may not sufficiently address. 5.3 How Does Performance Scale with Stream Length? 

As shown in Figure 4, G REP MEM maintains strong performance on both KV Store and Log Triage as stream length increases, demonstrating its ability to scale to longer input streams. In contrast, SUPO and masking exhibit substantial performance degra-dation as stream length grows, with accuracy ap-proaching zero in some settings. MemGPT main-tains relatively stable performance on Log Triage but remains substantially below G REP MEM , with accuracy around 50%. These widening gaps sug-gest that preserving searchable history becomes increasingly valuable as input length grows. Fur-thermore, RL improves G REP MEM ’s performance on Log Triage at longer stream lengths, while its KV Store accuracy remains high both before and after training. These results suggest that G REP -MEM ’s searchable history enables effective scaling to longer input streams by preserving access to rel-evant information, while RL further improves evi-dence utilization. Such capabilities are valuable for long-running agents, where historical information accumulates beyond the model’s context window. 

5.4 What Does Grep Cost? 

GREP MEM lies on the accuracy–token-cost Pareto frontier across all five benchmark settings: no eval-uated strategy is both cheaper and more accurate (Figures 1 and 5). Compared with SUPO, it uses only 6% more tokens on BCP-Multi and 5% fewer on AMA-Bench file while achieving higher accu-racy. On ContextBench, it substantially outper-forms SUPO with only 19% additional token us-age and consumes only 29% as many tokens as MemGPT. The notable exception is AMA-Bench stream, where G REP MEM uses 2.3× as many to-kens as SUPO for a small improvement in accu-racy and calls grep roughly 19 times per trajectory. These results suggest that recall incurs additional retrieval cost, but it is worthwhile and most cost-effective when the retrieved evidence materially improves the final answer. 

6 Conclusion 

We introduce G REP MEM , a simple, lossless, and index-free memory mechanism that combines con-text summarization with a complete, searchable interaction history. Our results demonstrate that GREP MEM supports accurate long-horizon re-call, scales effectively to increasingly long input streams, and lies on the accuracy–token-cost Pareto frontier across all five evaluation tasks. Compar-isons before and after RL training further show that RL can improve how agents retrieve and utilize historical information, with benefits varying across tasks and context management strategies. Our work reexamines the boundary between short-term context management and long-term memory in LLM agents. What should an agent forget when it can remember everything, and how much complexity does remembering truly require? By preserving complete histories alongside com-pact working contexts, G REP MEM suggests that lossless preservation and lossy compaction can serve complementary purposes. Looking ahead, we envision G REP MEM as a simple but competi-tive baseline for evaluating future methods, inspir-ing principled memory designs for reliable long-running agents. 8Limitations 

Our evaluation focuses on controlled benchmarks and a single model, leaving open how G REP MEM 

generalizes to broader agent settings. However, these benchmarks cover diverse memory require-ments, from multi-question research to exact recall over long histories, providing evidence for the ben-efits of lossless memory. G REP MEM also relies on lexical search, which may be less effective when relevant information cannot be identified through explicit search patterns. Nevertheless, our goal is not to replace more sophisticated memory systems in every setting, but to demonstrate that preserving raw history and retrieving it with a simple tool can already provide strong performance without struc-tured memory infrastructure. Finally, the benefits of lossless recall depend on the task: when sum-maries retain sufficient information, additional re-trieval may introduce unnecessary overhead. This suggests that an important future direction is to determine when simple, lossless retrieval is most valuable and how to use it selectively. 

Ethics Statement 

Our experiments use established benchmarks, fixed offline corpora, and publicly available agent tra-jectories, without involving human subjects or col-lecting new personal data. G REP MEM preserves complete interaction histories, which may contain sensitive information in real-world deployments. This concern is shared by other memory systems that retain historical interactions, rather than being unique to our approach. As with any persistent memory mechanism, appropriate access controls and data retention policies should be applied ac-cording to the deployment setting. Our work fo-cuses on improving long-horizon agent reliability through a simple and transparent memory mech-anism. To support transparency and reproducibil-ity, we will release our code, prompts, and trained model checkpoints.

System Prompt (Deep Research) 

You are a deep-research agent. Answer the user's question by researching a fixed document corpus with the tools provided. Tools: - search(query): dense retrieval over the corpus; returns the top 10 documents (docid, url, first ~512 tokens). - get_document(docid): returns the full text of a document (up to 8192 tokens). {extra_tools} Guidelines: - Questions often require combining several clues; break them down, search iteratively, and verify candidates against the evidence. - Prefer short, specific search queries. Open documents when snippets are insufficient. - When you are confident, stop calling tools and give your final answer as a short phrase inside \boxed{}, e.g. \boxed{Marie Curie}. - You have at most 30 turns (assistant replies). The number of turns left is shown after each tool result; give your final answer before it runs out. 

ReAct omits the last guideline. G REP MEM adds “Replies that only call grep_history do not count as turns (up to 10 such replies).” MemGPT adds an equivalent instruction for its memory tools. 

grep_history Description (After First Com-paction) 

- grep_history(pattern, max_matches, offset, context_lines, count): regex search over your full, uncompressed interaction history (all earlier tool outputs and messages), including parts removed from your context by summarization. Each match is labeled with the turn and tool call it came from. 

The tool’s JSON schema documents the argu-ments ( max_matches : default 10, at most 30; 

offset : matches to skip; context_lines : default 1, at most 3; count : only count matching lines). It recommends patterns made of previously observed exact strings (names, numbers, dates, IDs).

B Implementation Details 

Log format and grep output. Each log record is a header line (e.g. [turn 4 tool get_document(...)] ) followed by its text, and 

grep_history matches the text line by line; records of earlier grep_history outputs are skipped, since they only repeat older matches. The output starts with the number of matching lines by source, then lists the matches grouped under their record’s header, with line numbers within the record ( : marks a match, - a context line, – a gap). 13 Matches are returned oldest first, ten per page by default (at most 30), with one surrounding line by default (at most three). Each response is capped at 6K characters, ends at a whole match and gives the offset for continuing the listing. Lines are clipped to 300 characters around the match, an invalid pat-tern returns the compiler’s error, and a search that takes more than 10 s is stopped with an error. An example on a small log:

Fitting the compaction call. Several large tool outputs in one turn can push the context past τ

before the agent can compact, but the compaction prompt must still fit in W . We estimate tokens from characters, calibrated on the last call’s exact prompt-token count, and drop the oldest turns (al-ways keeping the system prompt, the task and the latest turn), then shorten the latest tool outputs if needed, until the prompt fits. If the model answers the compaction request with a tool call instead of text, its final reasoning is used as the summary. 

C Baselines 

Masking. Lindenbauer et al. (2025) keep the last 

M observations. With a fixed context window, we mask observations according to a token bud-get. When the next model call would exceed τ ,the oldest unmasked observations are replaced by the placeholder until the context is below τ / 2. In stream tasks, these observations are the incoming user messages. Since Qwen3.5 (Qwen Team, 2026) retains the reasoning from every turn in the current episode, masking observations alone may not free enough space. In that case, we remove reasoning from the oldest turns while preserving their ac-tions. We drop entire turns only if the prompt still does not fit within the context window. As with compaction, masking in batches keeps the prompt prefix unchanged between masking events. 

MemGPT. The main context is the system prompt with the working-context block, followed by a message queue headed by the task and a re-cursive summary. At 70% of τ the agent is warned. At τ , the queue is flushed: the oldest messages are evicted until about half the window remains, and the model writes a new recursive summary of the old summary and the evicted messages, in a call without tools. Like MemGPT’s summarizer, tools are disabled during this call. When tools are available, the base model returned a memory-tool call instead of a summary in about one-third of context flushes. conversation_search finds the messages (whole tool outputs or streamed batches) that contain a case-insensitive substring and reports their number, five per page. Archival memory ranks passages with BM25 (Robertson and Zaragoza, 2009) instead of MemGPT’s embeddings and also returns five per page. On ContextBench, each streamed operation allows up to 8 memory-tool replies, and the chain of replies within one opera-tion is trimmed if it would overflow the window. 

D Training Details 

Data. We train on 4,000 episodes compris-ing three task types: 70% single-question OpenResearcher episodes, 15% three-question OpenResearcher sessions, and 15% synthetic 14 ContextBench-style streams. OpenResearcher (Li et al., 2026b) questions are English questions with answers of at most ten words (3,013 ques-tions), searched over the OpenResearcher corpus of 14.9M web documents with Qwen3-Embedding-8B (Zhang et al., 2025); search returns the top 10 documents with their id, URL and first ∼512 to-kens. get_document returns up to 8,192 tokens of a selected document. To construct three-question training sessions, we shuffle the OpenResearcher training split with seed 2, partition it into triples and take the first 600 triples. Each triple is presented as three numbered questions in one initial prompt and answered together as numbered \boxed{} lines. The 60-turn budget is shared across the three ques-tions, not enforced as separate 20-turn sessions. A question may appear both in a single-question train-ing episode and in a three-question session. Con-textBench training streams use seeds from 1000 on (evaluation uses 42–45), with KV Store at 1,000 or 2,000 records and Log Triage at 1,600 or 3,200 lines (1.5–6 × the window), and 12 queries each. 

Turns as training sequences. Each model call is one step. Consecutive turns that extend the same context are merged into one training sequence. A context compaction or a new user turn in a stream starts a new sequence whose prompt is the full con-text available at that point. Compaction and mem-ory calls are sequences of their own, so summaries are trained too. All training sequences within an episode share the same episode-level advantage, allowing RL to assign credit across context com-pactions without separate rewards for summaries or individual grep calls. 

Hyperparameters and compute. Table 4 lists the hyperparameters, which are shared by all strate-gies. We train with rLLM (Tan et al., 2025) on verl (Sheng et al., 2025), with vLLM (Kwon et al., 2023) for rollouts. Each run uses 24 H100 GPUs (8 for the FSDP trainer, 16 for rollouts; ReAct uses 8 GPUs for rollouts because its rollouts are shorter), and a dense-retrieval server on 3 more GPUs is shared by all runs. In total, training used about 6K H100-hours; MemGPT’s runs were the slow-est, since its ContextBench rollouts make several hundred calls.
