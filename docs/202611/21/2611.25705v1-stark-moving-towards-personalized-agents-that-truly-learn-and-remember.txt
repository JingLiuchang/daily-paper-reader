STARK: Moving Towards Personalized Agents That Truly Learn and Remember
Aryan Jain, Abhishek Jha∗, Anish Nediyanchath, Promod Yenigalla
RBS Tech Sciences, Amazon
{arynjn, abhisjht, anishned, promy}@amazon.com
Abstract
Long-term memory is essential for personalized agentic sys-
tems that interact with users over long time periods, yet current
architectures only capture what happened while leaving the
reasoning of why it happened to be derived later by underlying
LLMs.These events can, however, convey not only what a user
experienced but also how that experience shaped their behav-
ioral patterns. We introduce STARK, a dual-graph memory
architecture which treats these events not just as nodes but
also as edges between behavioral insights. STARK maintains
an Episodic Graph which records interactions as temporally,
causally, and semantically linked episodes; and an Insight
Graph which represents LLM-extracted behavioral signals
scored by how many events support them, connected by the
very events that led to them. At inference, behavioral knowl-
edge is retrieved directly from insight nodes grounded by
event-as-edges, eliminating repeated episodic traversal. The
event-as-edge structure also naturally enables projecting the
knowledge graph into a relational embedding space where
cross-user transfer becomes possible under shared contextual
circumstances, allowing the system to infer missing insights
for cold-start users. Evaluations show that STARK improves
by 1.48 judge score on LongMemEval information extraction
and 0.93 on LoCoMo open domain questions over state of the
art. On the public LongLaMP abstract-generation benchmark,
cross-user transfer improves open-ended judge scores by 0.7
points and ROUGE-L by 3 at 5% graph sparsity.

Introduction
As autonomous interactive agents are increasingly deployed
to manage complex, long-running tasks such as workflow
orchestration and support-ticket triage under evolving con-
straints (Wu et al. 2023; Wang et al. 2023; Jain, Ghosh, and
Yenigalla 2026), their effectiveness relies on accumulating
knowledge across long-horizon interactions. For example, for
a deal negotiation agent, a compliance constraint surfaced
during negotiation in one quarter must inform procurement
strategy in the next; a failed negotiation strategy must not
be repeated in the future. Moreover, many of these agents
are domain-specific, wherein users pursue structurally simi-
lar goals and exhibit convergent behavioral patterns, making
cross-user knowledge transfer a natural consequence of shared
goal distributions.
Recent persistent-memory systems partially address this by
constructing knowledge graphs from interactions (Rasmussen

et al. 2025; Xu et al. 2025; Jiang et al. 2026), but maintain
a single flat graph per user where events, preferences, con-
straints, and goals coexist as undifferentiated nodes. This
design has two consequences. First, behavioral signals (the
durable, reusable insights into how a user tends to act and de-
cide, namely their preferences, constraints, goals, and values,
distilled from many individual episodes and distinct from
the episodes themselves) remain distributed across episodic
nodes rather than being represented explicitly, which forces
the LLM to re-derive them at query time, redundantly and
often inconsistently (Richardson et al. 2023; Gutiérrez et al.
2024). Second, each user’s graph is self-contained and pro-
vides no pathway for cross-user transfer. The common root
cause is that these graphs capture what happened while leav-
ing the reasoning behind why it happened to downstream
inferencing.
We introduce STARK, a dual-graph memory architecture
built around this distinction. Our core contributions are as
follows:
1. A dual-graph architecture that separates episodic events
from behavioral knowledge. It is composed of an Episodic
Graph with temporally, causally and semantically linked
episodes and an Insight Graph that contains behavioral
signals, each carrying evidence vectors from the episodes
that support it. The two are joined by an event-as-edge
bridge: episodic identifiers serve as typed edge labels in
the Insight Graph, keeping every insight grounded in the
episodes that produced it.
2. A cross-user transfer mechanism for cold-start users.
Insight nodes are embedded in a relational space where
users with similar preference fingerprints cluster together,
and link prediction over that space infers a new user’s likely
signals without accessing another user’s raw interactions.
3. We deploy STARK for a production vendor-negotiation
assistant and evaluate it in an 8-week A/B pilot (N=40
managers) cutting negotiation rounds per deal by 18% and
cycle time by 15% (p < 0.05).

Consider users U = {u1, . . . , uN} interacting with an LLM
agent over many sessions spanning long periods. For each

The persistent-memory problem is to construct from Ci a

advance the user’s underlying goal, from acquiring informa-

3.1
Episodic Graph
Gi
E = (VE, EE) captures the factual record of a user’s inter-
actions. Canonical node types are {User, Event, Milestone,
Organization}, where Event nodes form the core, each a
discrete interaction observed in conversation, and a Mile-
stone marks a significant workflow or life-state change; the
vocabulary is open, extended on demand by induced types
such as Activity, Person, or Decision. Edges are temporal
(followed_by), causal (led_to, caused), or action edges
(domain-specific entity interactions, e.g. between users and
the entities they act on, such as topics or artifacts), each carry-
ing a timestamp t(e). Together they support temporal, causal,
and entity-centric graph retrieval. We also provide qualitative
extraction examples on the LongLaMP abstract-generation
domain in Appendix A.8.
3.2
Insight Graph
While Gi
E records what happened, Gi
I = (VI, EI) captures
the behavior behind why it happened, with the Useri node
as the one fixed type anchoring each user to their signals.
Edges EI carry two kinds of labels. Structural relations
form a stable, closed set Rs = {prefers, wants, values,
tried, avoids, learned}; a Preference learned a Con-
straint, for instance, is a signal-to-signal edge expressible only
because signals are nodes rather than edge attributes. Event-
linked relations Re = {id(e) : e ∈VE} reuse episodic event
identifiers as labels: an edge (va, eid, vb) ∈EI means the
relationship between va and vb was observed during event eid.
This event-as-edge bridge enables bidirectional navigation
between a signal and its originating events, anchoring each
insight to its episodic provenance without merging the two
graphs (Fig. 2).

on an inferred signal, and the cost is asymmetric: a one-off,
weakly supported guess is risky, whereas a signal reinforced

evidence, query context, and recency rather than re-deriving
it from raw history; when these leave the signals tied, STARK
abstains and presents the trade-off to the user.
4
Cross-User Structural Transfer
Each user’s dual-graph is built independently, so every
(Gi
E, Gi
I) is a self-contained island: a sparse-history user
has almost no signals to retrieve, exactly when guidance is
most needed. Yet STARK targets domain-specific settings
whose users share a goal distribution and converge on recur-
ring patterns. For example, among authors writing in the same
research area, one author establishes that a particular method
suits a class of problems, and the next author working on that
problem class rediscovers the same relevant concepts from
scratch. This is the same shared-goal structure that arises
among account managers negotiating with a common vendor
base in our deployment (Section 7).
We therefore project every user’s signal relations into one
relational embedding space, learned from triples (h, r, t) over
the combined dual-graph, the union of all users’ (Gi
E, Gi
I),
and read transfer off the geometry by user-level similarity
matching and link prediction over missing signals.
Why a relation-aware embedding? Collaborative filtering
(Koren, Bell, and Volinsky 2009) collapses every interaction
into one interacts-with edge, whereas our signals are heteroge-
neous: the same constraint node plays different geometric roles
under prefers and under the collapsed surfaced relation. We
therefore use TransR (Lin et al. 2015), with entity embeddings
e ∈Rd, relation embeddings r ∈Rk, and per-relation pro-
jections Mr ∈Rk×d that map entities into a relation-specific
subspace before scoring f(h, r, t) = ∥Mreh + r −Mret∥2.
This projection, which a single-space model like TransE (Bor-
des et al. 2013) lacks, separates prefers from surfaced and
drives clustering, drawing two users who connect to the same
signal under the same relation together. It holds only among
users sharing a domain and goal distribution, so we claim no
cross-domain transfer. We further compare TransR against
RotatE and CompGCN in Appendix A.5. We use standard
TransR notation: in a triple (h, r, t), t is the tail entity and e
an entity embedding, distinct from the episodic timestamp
t(e) and event e.
Selective prefixing: The geometry produces transfer only
if users share target nodes, so during triple extraction we pre-
fix user-specific entities but leave behavioral-signal entities
unprefixed, letting two users point at one shared signal node,
the seam along which one user’s geometry becomes informa-
tive for another (evidence in Appendix A.7). Event-linked
labels in Re appear mostly once, giving TransR no repeated
examples to estimate Mr, so we collapse them to a single
surfaced relation while keeping Rs intact.
Preference fingerprints: The first mechanism finds be-
haviorally similar users so a user, especially a cold-start one,
can borrow their signals. Raw user-node embeddings fail, as
a user node connects overwhelmingly to unshared episodic
edges, so we represent each user only by their shared-signal

Experimental Setup
We evaluate on five public benchmarks. LoCoMo (Maharana
et al. 2024) and LongMemEval (Wu et al. 2025) target long-

horizon memory (exercising the Episodic Graph via temporal,
causal, and multi-hop retrieval); RealPref (Guo et al. 2026)
and PAL-Bench (Huang et al. 2025) are preference-oriented
(exercising the Insight Graph). Evidence scoring is active
only where reliable outcome labels exist (RealPref, LoCoMo,

four are single-user, so we evaluate cross-user transfer on the
public LongLaMP abstract-generation benchmark (Kumar
et al. 2024; Salemi et al. 2024), whose authors act as users
with disjoint train/test authors for a genuine cold-start setting.
We report on the full public test split of each benchmark and
simulate cold start by retaining {5 −100}% of each user’s
graph operations (protocol in Appendix A.3).
We compare against memory systems Mem0 (Chhikara
et al. 2025), A-MEM (Xu et al. 2025), Zep (Rasmussen et al.
2025), and MAGMA (Jiang et al. 2026), and full-context
LLM baselines (Claude Haiku 4.5, Sonnet 4.5/4.6, and Opus
4.5). For fairness, every memory system and all three A/B
arms use Claude Sonnet 4.6 as the backbone and the same
B=4000-token retrieval budget as STARK (Table 5; full-
context truncation policy in Appendix A.3). MCQ, true/false,
and ranking are scored by exact match and are therefore
judge-independent; open-ended answers are scored 1–5 (≥4
correct) by a GPT-5.1 judge.

Results
Graph structure matches full-context on short histories
and dominates on long ones. On LoCoMo (Table 1, scores
on a 1–5 scale), STARK is best across all four categories:
it leads on single-hop (3.98 vs. MAGMA: 3.88, +0.10; vs.
best LLM-only Opus 4.5: 3.84) and substantially outperforms
all baselines on multi-hop (3.56 vs. MAGMA: 2.64, +0.92),
temporal reasoning (3.77 vs. MAGMA: 3.25, +0.52), and
open-domain queries (4.08 vs. Zep: 3.15, +0.93). Notably,
on LoCoMo every other memory system does worse than full-
context Opus 4.5 on multi-hop (2.50–2.70 vs. 3.34): lossy
retrieval hurts when the history already fits, and STARK
is the only system that avoids this cost. LongMemEval is
the opposite case: histories no longer fit and full context
collapses (information extraction around 1.6–1.8), so struc-
tured retrieval becomes essential. Here STARK achieves 4.38
on information extraction (+1.48 over MAGMA), 2.62 on
temporal reasoning (+0.52), and 3.32 on knowledge update

Removing the Episodic Graph badly hurts the factual and

most for preferences (RealPref −0.49) and only marginally

LongLaMP and PAL-Bench, whose data carry no reception

affects only the shared-domain LongLaMP (−0.29, +90 ms
at O(1)). On long histories STARK is also faster than full
context (e.g. LongMemEval 1839 vs. 3823 ms) as it sends
a small subgraph, while insights saturate as episodes grow
(Fig. 3), keeping the read path flat.
7
Deployment and Impact
STARK is deployed as a drop-in memory layer for a vendor-
negotiation assistant used by Account Managers (AMs) who
negotiate terms with thousands of vendors. Negotiations span
many sessions across quarters, constraints surface mid-deal
and must inform later ones, and closing strategies sit buried
in each AM’s history; new hires start cold, and the team
rediscovers the same constraints independently.
We ran an 8-week live A/B test over the 40 AMs (18 new
hires under 6 months, 22 with 3+ years). It is paired at the
vendor level: each of a manager’s five vendors gets three
otherwise identical deals, run separately to avoid carryover:
memory-on, memory-on with cross-user transfer disabled
(transfer-off), and memory-off control. This yields 200 vendor
triples (600 deals), with the order of the three arms randomized
within each triple, and the transfer-off arm isolates cross-user
transfer with the rest of the memory layer fixed. Each AM’s
dual-graph was initialized from up to a year of their prior
history (under six months for new hires), so every arm retrieves
over the AM’s full available history from the first deal; the
8-week window scopes only the deals conducted in the pilot,
all 600 of which closed, so cycle time carries no closure-
based selection. Vendors respond live, each deal carries an
AM-provided target, and all arms use Claude Sonnet 4.6.
Memory-on cut negotiation rounds per deal by 18% and
cycle time by 15% (paired t-test, n=200, both p < 0.05),
raised deals closed at or above target by 9 pp, and lifted blind
guidance ratings by 0.8/5. The gains concentrate in new hires
(∼24%/∼19% vs. experienced ∼13%/∼11%). The transfer-
off arm pins this on cross-user transfer: without it, memory-on
retained only ∼11%/∼9%, so transfer supplies roughly 40%
of the gain overall and most of it for new hires (new ∼9% vs.
∼24%; experienced ∼12% vs. ∼13%). Through the pilot we
learned that the AMs preferred inferred signals surfaced as
accept-or-dismiss options rather than acted on, so we leave
the decision to them. We also observed that new–experienced
fingerprint similarity (Eq. 2) rose 0.31→0.58, a descriptive
within-arm trend (not isolated by the transfer-off arm).
Why it works?
Surfacing known constraints and concession
patterns up front removes rounds spent rediscovering them;
outcome-weighting (Eq. 1) front-loads previously successful
tactics and down-ranks rejected ones, shortening the cycle;
and for new hires this knowledge arrives via cross-user transfer.
Cost, governance, and rollout are in Appendix A.6.
Conclusion
We presented STARK, a dual-graph memory that decouples
episodic events from behavioral knowledge via an event-as-
edge bridge, enabling evidence-ranked retrieval and cross-
user transfer for cold-start inference. However, STARK has
several important limitations. TransR updates in O(1) but its
per-relation projections have fixed capacity, so embedding
quality degrades as the graph densifies. Privacy is enforced
agentically, not formally: we do not bound residual leakage of
aggregate fingerprints under membership inference. Transfer
is domain-specific, holding only among users who share a
goal distribution. In future work, we plan to use the graph
to look forward rather than back. Using behavioral paths as
a search tree and running an MCTS-style rollout over likely
trajectories can help the agent act on the path most likely to
reach the user’s goal.

A.4
TransR training details
We train a single TransR model over the combined multi-user
graph, with entity embeddings e ∈Rd, relation embeddings
r ∈Rk, and per-relation projection matrices Mr ∈Rk×d,
scored by f(h, r, t) = ∥Mreh + r −Mret∥2. Training mini-
mizes a margin-based ranking loss over the positive triples in
the combined set against tail-corrupted negatives (a positive
(h, r, t) paired with (h, r, t′) for a sampled t′̸ = t), pushing
the geometry to score observed structure below corrupted


structure; we use otherwise standard TransR hyperparameters
and do not tune the embedding dimensions d and k per dataset.
A full offline train takes 58 s on the LongLaMP training set.
Between full retrains the model is updated incrementally in
O(1) per new triple as conversations arrive, and a periodic
full retrain admits new users and entities; in the interval before
a user appears in a retrain, cold-start link prediction serves
them off the existing embedding space. We adopt TransR
because a live memory needs incremental, no-full-graph-
recompute updates, which rules out CompGCN (it requires
full message-passing at inference); we prefer it to RotatE,
which is competitive on our metrics, for its interpretable
per-relation subspaces (see Appendix A.5).
A.5
TransR comparison with other embedding
models
The Insight Graph contains semantically heterogeneous re-
lations (prefers, surfaced, wants) that require distinct
geometric subspaces: the space in which a user prefers a
behavioral pattern differs from the space in which that pat-
tern surfaces a constraint. TransR addresses this directly
via per-relation projection matrices Mr that map entities
into relation-specific subspaces before translational scoring
(Mrh + r ≈Mrt). RotatE (Sun et al. 2019) models rela-
tions as rotations in a single complex space; empirically it
clusters comparably to TransR (Table 9), so we claim no
quality advantage over it. We nonetheless adopt TransR be-
cause its explicit per-relation projection matrices expose an
inspectable subspace per relation, which suits the provenance
and audit requirements of the deployment, and because its real
entity space matches the fingerprint’s real-vector aggregation
fp(u) = P wr · emb(pi). CompGCN (Vashishth et al. 2020)
entangles entity representations across relation types during
neighborhood aggregation and requires full message-passing
at inference, making it incompatible with the O(1) per-session
incremental updates a live memory needs; TransR and RotatE
both support such updates, CompGCN does not.
A.6

Engineering readiness.
STARK concentrates cost on the
one-time write path and keeps the read path flat as a user’s
graph grows. Ingestion is the cost center: each conversation
is processed by two sequential LLM passes (episodic then
insight), and a repeated behavioral signal is folded into an

on the LongLaMP training set used in our experiments) and
served with O(1) incremental updates per new triple, with
periodic full retraining (∼11 min at 10K users) to admit new

Preference Extraction Prompt
You are an expert preference-memory
extraction agent for personal
conversation analysis.
YOU MUST RESPOND WITH **ONLY** VALID JSON
- ABSOLUTELY NO OTHER TEXT.
TASK: Extract a PREFERENCE MEMORY
GRAPH from a conversation using memory
operations.
You will receive: 1. A user_id
identifying the person 2. A conversation
(and optionally persona context) 3. A
list of EPISODIC OPERATIONS already
extracted (episodes/events from the same
conversation)
== CORE DESIGN PRINCIPLE ==
Behavioral signals (preferences, values,
goals, constraints) are the only nodes;
the preference graph has NO Event nodes.
Edges between signals carry one of two
label kinds: (1) a structural relation
from the closed set {PREFERS, WANTS,
VALUES, TRIED, AVOIDS, LEARNED} - e.g.
a Preference LEARNED a Constraint; or (2)
an episodic event entity_id, recording
that the relationship was observed during
that event (the event-as-edge bridge).
Chain structure: User -[PREFERS]-> P1
-[LEARNED]-> C1 (structural relation)
User -[PREFERS]-> P1 -[event_entity_id]->
P2 (event-as-edge bridge)
== OPERATIONS ==
ADD_NODE - Create a new
preference/value/goal/constraint node
Properties MUST include: user_id,
graph_type ("preference"), name,
paths, category For grounding, emit
ONLY evidence_list: the distinct
episodes supporting this signal, each
{"episode_id", "outcome"}. The evidence
vector ev(v) = (strength s, evidence
count n, outcome weight w, confidence
c) is computed deterministically from
evidence_list at aggregation time
(UPDATE_NODE); do NOT score s, w, or c
by hand.
LINK - Create an edge between two nodes
For User-to-signal: edge_label is one
of the closed structural set {PREFERS,
WANTS, VALUES, TRIED, AVOIDS, LEARNED}.
For signal-to-signal: edge_label is
either a structural relation from the
same set (e.g. LEARNED) or an episodic
event entity_id.
UPDATE_NODE - Delta-update an existing
signal (re-triggers evidence aggregation)
DELETE_NODE - Remove a signal UNLINK
- Remove an edge NOOP - Nothing
preference-worthy in this chunk
== EVIDENCE RULES ==
The agent emits only the raw grounding;
all scores are computed downstream,
not by the LLM: - evidence_list:
[{"episode_id": "str", "outcome":
"success|failure|partial|pending"}] - one
entry per distinct supporting episode.
This is the ONLY evidence field the agent
produces. - The aggregator then derives
the evidence vector deterministically:
evidence count n = |evidence_list|,
outcome weight w = (successful episodes)
/ (resolved episodes), where resolved
means marked success or failure and
partial/pending episodes count toward
n but are excluded from w (w defaults
to 0.5 when no episode is resolved),
and strength s and confidence c via
the deterministic scoring functions
(both continuous in (0,1)). These are
recomputed on every UPDATE_NODE.
NO Event/Activity nodes in the preference
graph. Those belong in episodic only.
