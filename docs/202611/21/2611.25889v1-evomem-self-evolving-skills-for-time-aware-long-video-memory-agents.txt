Abstract 

Although memory-augmented architectures have expanded the ability of large vision-language models (LVLMs) to pro-cess long videos, existing retrieval processes still largely rely on global semantic matching and lack state-driven tempo-ral control, often returning fragmented or redundant evidence and failing to maintain a coherent evidence chain. To ad-dress this challenge, we propose EvoMem , a time-aware long-video memory agent guided by self-evolving retrieval skills. EvoMem constructs a hierarchical time-indexed mem-ory, where different types of evidence are aligned onto a uni-fied video timeline; during retrieval, temporal cues from ac-quired evidence are further converted into explicit signals that constrain subsequent memory access. To address recurring procedural errors in long-video question answering, such as unclear retrieval targets, improper memory access paths, or premature stopping, EvoMem introduces retrieval skills to provide process-level guidance, enabling the agent to dynam-ically adjust retrieval content, memory operations, and target temporal ranges according to the current evidence state, while deciding when to stop. Furthermore, inspired by reinforce-ment learning, we formulate skill evolution as a reward-guided policy improvement process. Each candidate skill is treated as an external policy patch, and rewards are assigned accord-ing to answer-correctness state transitions on the validation set. In this way, effective skills are accepted and low-quality skills are rejected without updating model parameters. Exper-iments on four long-video question answering benchmarks show that EvoMem significantly outperforms existing base-lines, achieving an average accuracy of 60.1% and an average improvement of 8.6 percentage points over the previous best methods, demonstrating its effectiveness for long-video rea-soning. 

# Introduction 

Recent advances in large vision-language models (LVLMs) have substantially improved video understanding, particu-larly on short clips with limited temporal scope (Bai et al. 2025; Comanici et al. 2025; Wang et al. 2025a; Lin et al. 2024; Li et al. 2024). However, extending these capabili-ties to hour-scale or longer videos (Yang et al. 2025b; Tian et al. 2025; Wang et al. 2025b; Fu et al. 2025; Wu et al. 2024) remains fundamentally challenging: question-relevant evidence is often sparse, temporally scattered, and buried in redundant visual and textual observations, while correct answering requires modeling temporal dependencies across distant events. Simply scaling up the context window with more frames, captions, or intermediate summaries (Liu et al. 2025; Li et al. 2025; Wang et al. 2026b) introduces high com-putational cost and severe distraction from irrelevant context, as illustrated in Fig. 1(a). As a result, even strong LVLMs often struggle to maintain stable temporal grounding and precise evidence localization in long videos. To overcome the limitations of direct long-context model-ing, recent studies have increasingly shifted toward memory-augmented architectures and retrieval-based video agents. Video-RAG organizes long-video content into queryable ev-idence units through retrieval augmentation (Luo et al. 2025; Wang et al. 2024). HippoMM builds hippocampal-inspired multimodal memories by consolidating audiovisual streams into semantic representations, while WorldMM constructs complementary episodic, semantic, and visual memories for multimodal retrieval across temporal scales (Lin et al. 2026b; Yeo et al. 2026). In parallel, agentic systems such as M3Agent and Ego-R1 introduce tool use and multi-step reasoning, en-abling LVLMs to iteratively acquire question-relevant infor-mation (Long et al. 2025; Tian et al. 2025). Together, these efforts move long-video understanding toward selective ac-cess to external memories, laying an important foundation for scalable long-video reasoning systems. However, as illustrated in Fig. 1(b) and Fig. 1(c), the avail-ability of external memory does not necessarily lead to re-liable evidence retrieval. Agent systems that rely on textual summaries or structured nodes can improve evidence ac-cessibility, but they also compress low-level visual informa-tion, making it difficult to verify fine-grained motions, object states, or local visual differences. Visual memory access can complement these visual cues to some extent; however, as shown in Fig. 1(c), when visual retrieval still relies on a coarse temporal range obtained from global textual match-ing, visual verification may be performed over an imprecise candidate window and miss the key evidence. These issues suggest that when upstream textual or semantic retrieval still operates over the global memory space, multi-turn retrieval can be attracted to semantically similar segments and repeat-edly return temporally fragmented or redundant evidence, thereby failing to continuously complete the evidence chain within the target temporal range. Therefore, a key bottleneck of existing methods lies not only in memory construction or arXiv:2611.25889v1 [cs.CV] 19 Nov 2026 Figure 1: Motivating example of EvoMem. To address the above limitations, we propose EvoMem ,a time-aware long-video memory agent that combines state-driven temporal retrieval with a self-evolving retrieval skill bank, as shown in Fig. 1(d) and Fig. 1(e). EvoMem first orga-nizes long videos into hierarchical time-indexed memories, enabling the agent to convert temporal cues obtained during retrieval into explicit constraints and dynamically narrow, expand, or shift subsequent retrieval ranges, thereby contin-uously acquiring evidence along the same temporal evidence chain. However, long-video questions often involve complex and diverse evidence requirements, and different question types correspond to different retrieval targets, modality pref-erences, retrieval orders, and stopping conditions. To prevent multi-turn retrieval from degenerating into locally guided trial-and-error without global planning, and to equip the agent with cross-turn retrieval planning ability, EvoMem se-lects a relevant retrieval skill before inference begins to guide target localization, memory operations, temporal range ad-justment, semantic queries, and stopping decisions during multi-step retrieval. Furthermore, EvoMem formulates skill self-evolution as reward-guided policy improvement: candi-date skills are induced from successful and failed reasoning trajectories and evaluated on independent validation ques-tions against the current active skill bank; skills that repair previous errors receive positive rewards, while those that cause performance regressions are penalized. Only candidate skills with sufficiently high cumulative validation rewards are accepted. In this way, EvoMem continuously optimizes the external retrieval policy without updating foundation model parameters, turning accepted skills into reusable procedural experience for subsequent long-video reasoning. The main contributions of this work are as follows: 

• We propose EvoMem , a time-aware long-video memory agent guided by self-evolving retrieval skills. EvoMem or-ganizes long videos into hierarchical time-indexed video memories and uses controllable memory operations to turn temporal information into explicit constraints for sub-sequent memory access, enabling state-driven temporal evidence acquisition. 

• We introduce a reinforcement-learning-inspired skill self-evolution mechanism, where candidate retrieval skills are treated as candidate updates to the current retrieval policy and are rewarded according to answer-correctness transi-tions during validation execution. This enables EvoMem to selectively accept effective skills, reject regressive skills, and continuously optimize the skill bank without updating the parameters of the foundation model. 

• We systematically evaluate EvoMem on four long-video question answering benchmarks, and validate the effec-tiveness of time-aware retrieval, retrieval skill guidance, and reward-guided skill self-evolution through compo-nent ablations and cross-model skill generalization ex-periments. 

# Related Work 

Long Video Understanding 

Recent large vision-language models (LVLMs) have achieved substantial progress in video understanding and have grad-ually been extended from short clips to hour-scale or even longer videos (Bai et al. 2025; Comanici et al. 2025; Wang et al. 2025a). To mitigate the context pressure introduced by ultra-long videos, existing studies mainly improve long-context visual modeling (Bai et al. 2025; Comanici et al. 2025; Zhang et al. 2024) or reduce redundant video content through spatiotemporal sampling, hierarchical summariza-tion, and token compression (Liu et al. 2025; Yang et al. 2025a; Wang et al. 2026b). However, they still largely rely on preserving or compressing video information within a limited context, making it difficult to avoid sparse key evi-dence being overwhelmed by redundant content. This moti-vates a growing shift toward selectively acquiring and using question-relevant evidence in long-video reasoning. 

Memory-Augmented Video LLMs 

To support long-video reasoning, a growing body of work augments video language models with external retrieval and memory mechanisms. Early retrieval-augmented methods typically convert long videos into queryable frames, clips, textual descriptions, or graph nodes, and retrieve question-relevant evidence during inference rather than relying solely on uniformly sampled video inputs. Building on this direc-tion, recent methods have evolved from retrieval-augmented evidence acquisition (Luo et al. 2025; Ma et al. 2025; zhucun et al. 2025) to tool-using agentic reasoning (Tian et al. 2025) and explicit multimodal video memory construction (Lin et al. 2026b; Long et al. 2025; Yeo et al. 2026), collec-tively improving the accessibility of long-video evidence. However, these methods mainly focus on constructing and accessing external evidence, while lacking mechanisms to actively adjust temporal ranges, memory sources, and re-trieval processes according to the evolving evidence state. 

Skill-Based LLM Agents 

Recent studies on LLM agents have begun to treat skills as reusable, manageable, and continuously updatable procedu-ral knowledge (Yang et al. 2026; Wang et al. 2026a; Xia et al. 2026; Ma et al. 2026; Zhou et al. 2026). MUSE-Autoskill or-ganizes skill creation, memory, management, evaluation, and refinement into a unified lifecycle (Lin et al. 2026a); Skil-lOps studies defect diagnosis, compatibility, and long-term usability of skill libraries from a library-maintenance per-spective (Pu, Song, and Zhao 2026). Relatedly, MemSkill represents agent memory operations as learnable and evolv-able memory skills (Zhang et al. 2026). Unlike these works, which mainly focus on general task execution, skill-library maintenance, or general memory management, EvoMem studies self-evolving skills for long-video temporal memory retrieval and continuously improves the skill bank through reinforcement-learning-inspired validation feedback. 

# Method 

This section presents the overall framework of EvoMem, as shown in Fig. 2. Given a long video and a question, EvoMem first constructs hierarchical time-indexed memories, enabling structured relations, interval textual context, and visual evi-dence to be accessed on a unified video timeline. It then per-forms skill-guided state-adaptive evidence retrieval, where the agent dynamically adjusts retrieval content, memory op-erations, and target temporal ranges according to the current evidence state. Finally, EvoMem uses reward-guided skill self-evolution to transform procedural feedback from suc-cessful and failed trajectories into reusable retrieval skills, thereby continuously optimizing the external retrieval pol-icy. 

Time-Indexed Video Memory Construction 

In long videos, similar events, human actions, and object states may recur at different temporal positions, making re-trieval based on semantic matching alone prone to conflat-ing temporally distant segments. To address this, EvoMem associates event relations, interval summaries, and visual evidence with explicit temporal boundaries, making time a key basis for distinguishing recurring events and organiz-ing evidence chains. Based on this, EvoMem converts the input video V into a hierarchical time-indexed video mem-ory bank M, providing a unified basis for subsequent time-aware memory access and cross-modal evidence alignment. To address this, EvoMem generates video summaries at multiple temporal scales L = {ℓ1, . . . , ℓ K } and extracts time-bounded event triplets from the summaries at each scale. Skill-Guided State-Adaptive Evidence Retrieval 

After constructing the time-indexed video memory bank M,EvoMem defines a set of atomic memory access operations with controllable temporal ranges over the memory bank. Specifically, the system supports three types of memory operations: textual retrieval over the multi-scale temporal knowledge graph Mkg for discovering candidate events, en-tity relations, and state changes; interval reading over the interval textual memory Mint for reading continuous local context within a target temporal range; and visual evidence acquisition over the visual memory Mvis for verifying fine-grained visual cues that may be compressed or omitted by textual memories. These operations constitute the executable memory access space of EvoMem, enabling the agent to ac-quire evidence from different memory sources and temporal ranges. Based on these atomic operations, the executor performs long-video question answering as a multi-turn evidence ac-quisition process. At retrieval turn t, the current state is rep-resented as 

st = ( q, O, E t−1, H t−1), (5) where q denotes the input question, O denotes the candidate options, Et−1 denotes the accumulated evidence state up to the previous turn, and Ht−1 denotes the retrieval history. The executor generates a retrieval decision based on the current state: 

dt = Fexec (st). (6) For a non-stop decision, dt is represented as (at, u t, w t),corresponding to an atomic memory operation, a semantic query, and a target temporal window, respectively. If the evidence is sufficient, dt corresponds to a stop action, and the accumulated evidence is passed to the final answering module. After executing a non-stop decision, the system retrieves the turn-level evidence from the corresponding memory: 

∆Et = at(ut, w t; M). (7) The retrieved evidence ∆Et is then incorporated into the evidence state Et, while the current action, query, temporal window, and retrieved evidence are appended to the retrieval history Ht. The updated state is then carried to the next retrieval turn. On top of this base execution process, EvoMem introduces question-type-aware retrieval skills to provide process-level guidance for multi-turn evidence acquisition. For each ques-tion, the system selects a skill S that matches the question description and candidate options, and keeps it fixed through-out the retrieval process. Each skill contains applicability conditions, exclusion conditions, staged process steps, and stopping conditions, and generates a process control signal from the current state. Formally, the decision of the skill-guided executor is written as: 

dt = Fexec (st, S (st)) . (8) Here, S(st) summarizes the current retrieval stage, the com-pleteness of the evidence chain, evidence gaps to be filled, the next retrieval direction, and the stop decision. By incor-porating this control signal into decision making, EvoMem can more effectively schedule textual retrieval, interval read-ing, and visual evidence acquisition along the same temporal evidence chain. 

Reward-Guided Skill Self-Evolution 

In complex long-video reasoning, repeated agent failures are often not incidental execution errors, but symptoms of sys-tematic deficiencies in the underlying retrieval policy. For example, the agent may stop before the evidence chain is complete, fall into local or repetitive retrieval, fail to dynam-ically adjust the temporal range according to accumulated evidence, or overlook necessary visual verification when textual cues are ambiguous. To systematically address such procedural errors, EvoMem draws on the core reinforcement-learning idea of improving a policy through reward feedback, and formulates skill self-evolution as a reward-guided policy improvement process in the discrete space of retrieval skills. The key idea is to lift retrieval skills from static prompts to an external policy state that can be continuously op-timized. As described above, a selected retrieval skill S

generates a process control signal from the current state,

B(r). The retrieval policy induced by this bank is denoted as the incumbent policy πB(r) , which serves as a dynamic base-line for evaluating candidate skills. At the initial round, B(0) 

is initialized as an empty accepted-skill bank; as evolution proceeds, only candidate skills that pass validation are added to this bank. At evolution round r, EvoMem first deploys the incum-bent policy πB(r) on a set of training tasks, collecting com-plete multi-turn retrieval trajectories, state transition records, and final answer correctness. These trajectories constitute the agent’s experience from interacting with the long-video memory environment, and expose scheduling blind spots of the current policy. Based on this trajectory-level feedback, EvoMem induces a candidate skill c to encode a reusable pro-cedural correction. The candidate skill is not a memorized fix for a single failed example; instead, it generalizes the ap-plicability boundary, staged progression logic, and stopping condition of a class of retrieval procedures. # Experiments 

Experimental Setup 

Datasets and Metrics We evaluate EvoMem on four long-video question answering benchmarks: EgoLifeQA (Yang et al. 2025b), Ego-R1 Bench (Tian et al. 2025), LVBench (Wang et al. 2025b), and the long-video subset of Video-MME (Fu et al. 2025). These benchmarks cover com-plementary settings, including ultra-long egocentric QA and reasoning, hour-scale temporal understanding, and compre-hensive video analysis with multimodal evidence. Following prior work, we report answer accuracy (%) on each bench-mark, and also report the average accuracy over benchmarks with available comparable results. 

Baselines We compare EvoMem with three groups of rep-resentative baselines. The first group consists of general multimodal large language models, including InternVL3.5-38B (Wang et al. 2025a), Qwen3-VL-32B-Instruct (Bai et al. 2025), and Gemini 2.5 Pro (Comanici et al. 2025). The sec-ond group includes retrieval-augmented methods, includ-ing HippoRAG (Gutiérrez et al. 2025), LightRAG (Guo et al. 2025), and Video-RAG (Luo et al. 2025), which re-trieve external evidence to support long-video reasoning. The third group includes memory-agent methods for long-video reasoning, including Ego-R1 (Tian et al. 2025), EgoB-utler (Yang et al. 2025b), HippoMM (Lin et al. 2026b), and WorldMM (Yeo et al. 2026). Missing entries indicate that the corresponding results are unavailable or not reported under the same evaluation setting. 

Implementation Details We use Qwen3-VL-32B-Instruct as the base model of EvoMem for all test-time components, including video memory construction, skill selection, re-trieval decision making, evidence interpretation, and final answer generation. For fair comparisonunder matched con-text budgets across all methods, we reproduce each external-memory baseline following its official retrieval procedure and implementation settings, with the only modification be-ing the use of Qwen3-VL-32B-Instruct for all LVLM-based components. GPT-5.5 is used only in the skill self-evolution stage to induce candidate skills from successful and failed retrieval trajectories, and is not used as the test-time answer-ing model. For skill self-evolution, we construct an evolution set from public training questions and reserved benchmark questions. For each benchmark, skill evolution and final eval-uation use disjoint question sets, and all reported results are obtained on the final-evaluation set. 

Main Results 

Table 1 presents the main results of EvoMem and representa-tive baselines on four long-video question answering bench-marks. EvoMem achieves the strongest overall performance on all evaluated datasets, reaching accuracies of 55.0%, 61.0%, 59.1%, and 65.2% on EgoLifeQA, Ego-R1 Bench, LVBench, and Video-MME (L), respectively. Compared with the strongest non-EvoMem baseline on each benchmark, EvoMem brings improvements of 5.4, 4.7, 8.5, and 7.4 per-centage points, with an average accuracy of 60.1%. These results show that the advantage of EvoMem is not limited to a single dataset or video type, but remains consistent across long-video question answering settings with differ-ent lengths, viewpoints, and evidence requirements. The results on LVBench further highlight the advantages of EvoMem in time-aware memory access and skill-guided retrieval planning. As an extreme long-video understanding benchmark, LVBench often involves explicit or implicit tem-poral cues, requiring a model to convert these cues into effec-tive retrieval constraints and use them to locate and verify rel-evant evidence in long videos. Therefore, LVBench provides a representative setting for evaluating time-aware retrieval. The results show that even with only basic memory opera-tions, EvoMem reaches an accuracy of 56.0% through time-indexed memories and controllable textual retrieval, interval reading, and visual evidence acquisition, outperforming the Figure 3: Filtering ratios of candidate skills across LVBench evolution rounds as accepted, rejected, and not generated.

# Conclusion 

We presented EvoMem , a time-aware long-video memory agent that combines hierarchical time-indexed memories with self-evolving retrieval skills.
