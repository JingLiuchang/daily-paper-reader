Abstract 

Retrieving government schemes requires matching citizens’ information needs to for-mal administrative descriptions, often across languages and terminology. We present a bilin-gual search system for Maharashtra govern-ment schemes and public services that com-bines BM25 retrieval with sentence-embedding similarity through equal-weight score fusion. The system indexes aligned English–Marathi scheme descriptions and supports multilingual, Marathi-focused, and translation-mediated se-mantic configurations. Evaluation on manu-ally curated bilingual queries compares BM25, dense retrieval, and hybrid retrieval using In-dicSBERT and MahaSBERT. BM25 provides a strong baseline with a mean reciprocal rank (MRR) of 0.864. Hybrid retrieval increases MRR to 0.872 with IndicSBERT and 0.879 with MahaSBERT, whereas the corresponding dense-only systems achieve 0.663 and 0.655. MahaSBERT-based fusion also increases preci-sion from 0.781 to 0.808 while matching BM25 recall at 0.946. These results support the com-plementary use of lexical and semantic signals in this collection, while showing that improve-ments over a strong lexical baseline are modest and metric-dependent. The study provides an application-focused analysis of hybrid retrieval for bilingual public-service information access. 

1 Introduction 

Government schemes and public services are com-monly described using formal names, departmental terminology, and procedural language. Citizens may instead express their needs through short de-scriptions of a problem or desired benefit. A query such as “financial help for farmers” need not share the wording of a scheme described as providing agricultural credit. This vocabulary mismatch mo-tivates retrieval methods that combine exact term matching with semantic representations. The problem also has a multilingual dimension. A citizen may search in Marathi while relevant de-scriptions are available in English or in translated administrative language. A useful retrieval system must accommodate this variation without losing the discriminative value of scheme names, categories, and domain-specific terms. Bilingual records pro-vide a common document inventory, but translation alone does not resolve mismatches between a user’s phrasing and an official description. BM25 provides an established lexical rank-ing function that rewards informative query-term matches (Robertson and Zaragoza, 2009). Sen-tence embedding models offer a complementary mechanism by representing queries and docu-ments in a shared vector space (Reimers and Gurevych, 2019). In Marathi, MahaSBERT pro-vides language-focused representations, while In-dicSBERT supports multiple Indian languages (Joshi et al., 2023; Deode et al., 2023). However, semantic similarity need not imply that a scheme satisfies a user’s specific requirement. Combin-ing lexical and semantic signals therefore requires empirical evaluation against a lexical baseline. We develop a hybrid search system for a collec-tion of 64 Maharashtra government schemes and public services in English and Marathi. The sys-tem combines field-weighted BM25 scores with sentence-embedding similarity and presents ranked records with their source links. We investigate whether fusion improves retrieval over either com-ponent and whether the choice between a Marathi-focused and a multilingual encoder affects the re-sulting ranking. Our contributions are: • A bilingual retrieval pipeline integrating cor-pus collection, English–Marathi alignment, lexical indexing, and semantic search for gov-ernment schemes and public services. • A comparison of BM25, IndicSBERT-based retrieval, MahaSBERT-based retrieval, and 1arXiv:2611.26514v1 [cs.LG] 19 Nov 2026 their hybrid combinations on 150 manually curated queries. • An analysis of the magnitude and metric de-pendence of hybrid gains, distinguishing im-provements over dense-only retrieval from improvements over an already strong lexical baseline. 

2 Related Work 

Lexical and semantic retrieval. BM25 esti-mates relevance from term occurrences, inverse document frequency, and document-length nor-malization (Robertson and Zaragoza, 2009). It is well suited to exact terminology but does not ex-plicitly represent semantic relationships between different expressions. Contextual encoders such as BERT provide the basis for learned text repre-sentations (Devlin et al., 2019); Sentence-BERT adapts contextual encoding to produce indepen-dently computable sentence vectors (Reimers and Gurevych, 2019). These representations support similarity-based retrieval, although their effective-ness depends on the relationship between the em-bedding training objective and the target retrieval task. MiniLM uses self-attention distillation to ob-tain compact Transformer encoders (Wang et al., 2020), providing a basis for efficient sentence-embedding configurations. Dense Passage Re-trieval (DPR) explicitly trains a dual encoder on question–passage relevance pairs (Karpukhin et al., 2020), illustrating the distinction between general sentence similarity and retrieval-specific supervi-sion. 

Representations for Indian languages. Indic-NLPSuite develops corpora, evaluation bench-marks, and pretrained multilingual models for In-dian languages (Kakwani et al., 2020). MuRIL introduces Indian-language pretraining that incor-porates translated and transliterated sentence pairs (Khanuja et al., 2021). More generally, multilin-gual sentence embedding spaces can be learned through knowledge distillation with parallel sen-tences (Reimers and Gurevych, 2020). LaBSE combines multilingual pretraining with translation-based alignment to learn language-agnostic sen-tence embeddings (Feng et al., 2022). MahaSBERT and HindSBERT apply sentence-level training with translated natural language inference and seman-tic similarity data to Marathi and Hindi (Joshi et al., 2023). IndicSBERT extends sentence rep-resentation learning across multiple Indian lan-guages using multilingual BERT (Deode et al., 2023). These models motivate a comparison be-tween language-focused and multilingual represen-tations for Marathi administrative text. Their per-formance on general similarity tasks does not, by itself, establish their relative effectiveness for re-trieving specific public benefits. 

Hybrid score fusion. Hybrid retrieval combines lexical and semantic relevance estimates. Recip-rocal rank fusion aggregates rank positions rather than raw relevance scores (Cormack et al., 2009), providing an alternative to score-level interpolation. Bruch et al. (2024) analyze fusion functions, includ-ing convex combinations of lexical and semantic scores, and demonstrate the importance of treat-ing score combination as a retrieval-design choice. Our system uses a fixed, equal-weight combina-tion rather than introducing a new fusion algorithm. The contribution lies in its bilingual public-service application and the comparison of encoder choices against a shared lexical baseline. 

Multilingual and cross-domain evaluation. 

Mr. TyDi evaluates dense retrieval in typologi-cally diverse languages and finds that dense sig-nals can improve sparse–dense hybrids even when the dense retriever is weaker than BM25 in iso-lation (Zhang et al., 2021). BEIR examines re-trieval across heterogeneous tasks and domains and identifies BM25 as a robust zero-shot baseline (Thakur et al., 2021). Complementary multilin-gual resources address how retrieval data are con-structed: mMARCO translates the MS MARCO passage-ranking dataset (Bonifacio et al., 2021), whereas MIRACL provides native-speaker rele-vance annotations for monolingual retrieval across diverse languages (Zhang et al., 2023). The distinc-tion is relevant to bilingual public-service search, where translated document coverage and native-language relevance judgments serve different pur-poses. These findings motivate comparison against a strong lexical system and caution against trans-ferring encoder rankings directly from general sim-ilarity benchmarks to a specialized collection. Our evaluation addresses a bilingual administrative do-main rather than the broader benchmark settings studied in these resources. 23 Bilingual Scheme Collection 

3.1 Collection and Preprocessing 

The collection contains 64 distinct government schemes and public services gathered from Maha-rashtra government portals, including Aaple Sarkar, and scheme information repositories. Each record comprises a title, description, and source URL, to-gether with category information used for retrieval and filtering. The collection covers 14 domain categories, including agriculture, health, housing, education, revenue, transport, welfare, and indus-try. We remove HTML markup and navigation con-tent using BeautifulSoup and regular-expression-based cleaning. Text normalization standardizes whitespace and punctuation and corrects encoding artifacts. Potential duplicates are identified through fuzzy matching of names and descriptions; pairs with similarity scores above 85% are manually re-viewed and merged when they refer to the same record. Entries lacking a title or description are cor-rected from their source or excluded. The resulting scheme identifier is shared across the two language versions. 

3.2 English–Marathi Alignment 

The English records are translated into Marathi using the Google Translation API with domain-specific glossaries. Translation is performed in batches, followed by terminology consistency checks and native-speaker review of a 20% sam-ple. The aligned collection contains 64 English records and 64 corresponding Marathi records, rep-resenting 64 underlying schemes rather than 128 independent schemes. Table 1 summarizes the collection. Description lengths vary substantially, reflecting differences in the amount of procedural and service information associated with individual records. This variation motivates document-length normalization in lexi-cal retrieval and consistent record construction for semantic indexing.

4.2 Lexical Retrieval 

The lexical component uses BM25 in Elasticsearch (Robertson and Zaragoza, 2009), with k1 = 1 .5

and b = 0 .75 . Field boosts prioritize scheme titles and category terms relative to descriptions: title, category, and description receive weights of 3, 2, and 1, respectively. This configuration preserves ex-act scheme-name and domain-term matches while allowing description content to contribute to rank-ing.

4.4 Hybrid Ranking 

Let eSlex and eSsem denote normalized lexical and semantic scores. Hybrid ranking uses their convex combination: 

Shyb (q, d ) = α eSlex (q, d )+ (1 − α) eSsem (q, d ), (2) with α = 0 .5. Normalization places the two score streams on comparable scales before fusion. The equal weighting provides a fixed comparison across encoder configurations and does not establish that the weight is optimal for this domain. Candidates from the lexical and semantic branches are combined and reranked using the hy-brid score. The retrieval implementation caps each branch at 2,000 candidates. For this 64-record col-lection, that cap exceeds the corpus size and there-fore imposes no additional depth truncation. The evaluation consequently concerns ranking within a small collection rather than large-scale candidate generation. 

5 Experimental Setup 

5.1 Queries and Relevance Judgments 

The test set comprises 150 manually curated queries: 75 in English and 75 in Marathi. Each query is associated with one or more relevant schemes in the collection. All configurations are evaluated against the same query-to-scheme rele-vance mappings. We compare the shared BM25 baseline, the two dense-only retrievers, and the corresponding hybrid configurations. The query set evaluates retrieval from the fixed collection. It does not measure whether a user meets a scheme’s eligibility requirements or whether retrieval leads to a successful application. These outcomes require evidence beyond query– document relevance.

6 Results and Discussion 

6.1 Comparison of Retrieval Strategies 

Table 2 reports the retrieval results. BM25 achieves an MRR of 0.864, exceeding IndicSBERT-only re-trieval at 0.663 and MahaSBERT-only retrieval at 0.655. Lexical matching is therefore a strong base-line in this collection, and replacing it with dense similarity alone reduces the measured ranking ef-fectiveness. Both hybrid configurations obtain higher preci-sion and MRR than BM25. IndicSBERT-based fusion increases precision by 0.014 and MRR by 0.008, while recall increases from 0.946 to 0.950. MahaSBERT-based fusion increases precision by 0.027 and MRR by 0.015, while recall remains 0.946. Its MRR gain over BM25 is approximately 1.7% relative to the baseline. These results support a benefit from combining relevance signals, but do not show strict improvement on every metric. The gains over dense-only retrieval are substan-tially larger. MahaSBERT-based fusion increases MRR from 0.655 to 0.879, an absolute difference of 0.224, or approximately 34.2% relative improve-ment. IndicSBERT-based fusion increases MRR from 0.663 to 0.872, an absolute difference of 0.209. Distinguishing these comparisons is im-portant: the large improvements over dense-only retrieval do not characterize the smaller improve-ment over BM25. 

6.2 Effect of the Semantic Encoder 

IndicSBERT obtains higher precision, recall, and MRR than MahaSBERT in the dense-only setting. Under hybrid fusion, MahaSBERT instead yields the highest precision and MRR, while IndicSBERT yields the highest recall. The encoder ranking there-fore depends on the retrieval configuration and eval-uation metric. A possible explanation is that an encoder’s use-fulness in fusion depends on which candidates it promotes relative to BM25, rather than only on its standalone ranking. The results are compatible with complementary relevance signals, but do not isolate the mechanism responsible for the change in ordering. They also do not establish language-specific superiority from the aggregate bilingual scores. 

6.3 Implications for Public-Service Search 

Administrative descriptions contain names, cate-gories, and terms that can make lexical matching effective. Semantic representations may contribute when users paraphrase those descriptions, but can also assign high similarity to schemes with related topics and different purposes. The observed results favor retaining lexical evidence while supplement-ing it with an embedding-based signal. This qual-itative pattern is consistent with the complemen-tary sparse and dense signals observed in Mr. TyDi (Zhang et al., 2021), although the collections and evaluation settings differ. Qualitative error analysis identifies ambiguous queries and semantically similar schemes across de-partments as challenging cases. A retrieved record can be topically related without satisfying a user’s intended benefit or eligibility condition. Source links and explicit scheme details therefore remain important for interpreting results. The system sup-ports discovery of potentially relevant information rather than automated eligibility determination. 

7 Conclusion 

We presented a bilingual hybrid retrieval system for 64 Maharashtra government schemes and pub-lic services. The system combines field-weighted BM25 with sentence-embedding similarity through equal-weight normalized score fusion. On 150 En-glish and Marathi queries, MahaSBERT-based hy-brid retrieval achieves the highest precision (0.808) and MRR (0.879), while IndicSBERT-based hybrid retrieval achieves the highest recall (0.950). BM25 remains a strong baseline, with MRR 0.864, and substantially exceeds both dense-only systems. The main finding is that hybrid retrieval provides modest, metric-dependent improvements over lexi-cal retrieval in this collection, rather than a uni-form advantage on all measures. Future work should evaluate larger and more diverse scheme collections, domain-specific representation learn-ing, and query-dependent fusion weights, along-side direct assessment of user information needs and eligibility-sensitive retrieval.

Translation and domain terminology. The Marathi descriptions are translated from English. Native-speaker review of a sample mitigates some translation errors but does not ensure complete se-mantic equivalence. Administrative terminology and eligibility conditions may not be represented adequately by general sentence embeddings. 

Retrieval scope and generalization. The fixed fusion weight and small corpus do not establish op-timal weighting or scalability. The system indexes textual records rather than scanned forms or other multimodal documents. Periodic corpus updates can also delay coverage of newly introduced or modified schemes. Evaluation concerns relevance ranking and does not demonstrate improvements in public awareness, benefit uptake, or user eligibility decisions. 

Ethical Considerations 

Government scheme descriptions may change, and incorrect or outdated results can mislead users seek-ing public support. Retrieved records should re-tain links to authoritative sources, and users should consult current official eligibility and application information. Translation and ranking errors may disproportionately affect users relying on Marathi descriptions. Any future use of personal informa-tion for eligibility-aware retrieval would require appropriate consent, data minimization, and pri-vacy safeguards.
