Abstract
Long-term personalized agents are increasingly expected to answer queries over
user histories in which preferences, goals, and constraints evolve across interleaved
conversations. Existing retrieval-based and structured-memory systems can surface
relevant facts, but often return isolated memory units that do not explain which
earlier preference was refined, contradicted, or superseded by later evidence. We
introduce TRANSMEM3, a transition-aware memory retrieval framework that stores
preference updates as explicit retrieval units alongside ordinary factual memories.
During memory construction, TRANSMEM routes user interaction history into
topic-structured memory graphs, detects shift events, and grounds them as tran-
sition records. At inference time, TRANSMEM first retrieves factual anchors and
then adaptively expands over transition records, producing a compact context of
factual anchors and accepted transition records. Experiments on PersonaMem and
PersonaBench across two Qwen backbones show that TRANSMEM outperforms
representative retrieval-based and structured-memory baselines. Controlled analy-
ses further show that transition records and adaptive expansion contribute beyond
factual anchoring, chronological expansion, flat transition retrieval, and fixed-hop
traversal. These results suggest that preference-update records are useful retrieval
units for long-term personalization.

Introduction
Long-term memory is becoming central to personalized language agents. As LLM-based assistants
interact with users over extended periods, they must track not only isolated facts but also evolving
preferences, goals, routines, and constraints [19, 37, 17]. This is difficult because users are non-
stationary: a preference for quick solo workouts may shift toward longer, social outdoor activities
after joining a hiking group. These changes are rarely cleanly segmented; outdated preferences,
transient states, and current intentions often coexist across interleaved conversational threads. Multi-
turn fragility further compounds the problem, as even strong LLMs can over-rely on premature
assumptions and fail to recover from early errors [10]. Recent benchmarks similarly show persistent
weaknesses in multi-session reasoning, temporal updating, and dynamic user-profile tracking under
long, topic-interleaved contexts [11, 16, 30, 25, 9].
Existing memory-augmented approaches have made substantial progress in storing, organizing,
and retrieving personal information. Traditional retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) retrieves
semantically similar chunks from past interactions [12], but such similarity-driven retrieval can
surface stale or noisy evidence when user histories span long time periods, especially under knowledge

consolidation mechanisms, including sentence graph memory [31], temporal knowledge graphs [22],
scalable memory stores [4], agentic note organization [32], staged memory architectures [5], and
recollection-style retrieval [36]. These systems improve how memories are stored or selected, yet
evolving personal preferences introduce a distinct retrieval challenge: the decisive evidence is often
not a single memory item, but the update that explains how one user state superseded another.
A subtle difficulty is that the evidence needed to resolve a preference update is not always the evidence
most similar to the query. A user may ask for a current recommendation without mentioning the
historical event that changed the preference. Even when old and new facts are retrieved together,
presenting them as independent snippets leaves the preference-update relation for the model to infer.
The useful evidence is therefore often the update itself: what the user previously preferred, what
replaced it, why it changed, and when the new state became valid.
Figure 1 contrasts recommendations based on stale snippets, independent temporal facts, and explicit
preference-update evidence. This motivates preference-transition retrieval: retrieving the update
relation itself rather than leaving the model to infer it from disconnected factual snippets. We
introduce TRANSMEM, a transition-aware memory retrieval framework that stores preference
updates as transition records alongside ordinary factual memories. During construction, TRANSMEM
routes streaming interactions into topic-structured memory graphs and detects shift events as transition
records. During retrieval, it first grounds the query in factual anchors and then adaptively expands
over adjacent transition records when update evidence is needed. This design recovers compact
preference-update trajectories while avoiding unrelated chronological neighbors and keeping online
retrieval lightweight.
We evaluate TRANSMEM on complementary personalized-memory benchmarks, with Person-
aMem [9] stressing long-context, topic-interleaved preference evolution and PersonaBench [25]
testing broader personalized QA. Experimental results show that TRANSMEM consistently achieves
the best overall score among representative baselines. Ablation and control experiments with factual-
only retrieval, chronological expansion, flat transition retrieval, and fixed-hop expansion show that
the gains are not explained by factual anchoring, temporal-neighbor context, direct transition-text
retrieval, or fixed traversal depth alone.
Our contributions are as follows. First, we identify preference updates as a missing retrieval unit
in long-term personalized memory and represent them as structured transition records. Second, we
introduce TRANSMEM, which combines topic-structured memory construction, shift-event detection,
and adaptive transition-aware retrieval. Third, we evaluate TRANSMEM on PersonaMem and
PersonaBench across two Qwen backbones, with controlled analyses isolating the roles of transition
records and adaptive expansion.

Methodology
Preliminary.
We consider personalized question answering for a user u with chronological message
history (m1, . . . , mt). A memory-augmented system builds a memory Mu from this history and
retrieves an evidence context C(q) for a query q. An LLM with parameters θ then generates an answer
y conditioned on the query and retrieved evidence:
C(q) = Retrieve(q, Mu),
y ∼pθ(· | q, C(q)) .
Questions may concern historical facts, current preferences, or changes across time.
Overview.
We propose TRANSMEM, a two-stage framework for retrieving evidence from long-term
personal memory under evolving user preferences. The construction stage organizes each user’s
interaction history into topic-structured memory graphs and stores both ordinary factual memories
and explicit preference-update records. The retrieval stage first selects query-relevant factual anchors
and then adaptively expands over stored transition records when preference-update evidence is needed.
Figure 2 illustrates the overall pipeline.
Notation.
In TRANSMEM, the memory Mu is represented by the user memory graph Gu. We use
topic to denote a fine-grained, user-specific topic graph (e.g., fitness routine), rather than a coarse
domain label such as hobbies or work. We denote the i-th topic graph by Ti with summary embedding
si. A normal fact is an atomic, non-transition memory extracted from the user history. A transition
record is a structured old-to-new preference update detected from historical evidence. A transition
record stores the content of the update, while its grounded transition edge provides the graph structure
used for traversal.

2.2
Adaptive Preference Retrieval
At inference time, TRANSMEM retrieves evidence in two stages: initial factual anchoring, followed
by adaptive transition expansion. The final context contains factual anchors and accepted transition
records, whose fields include the old and new states.
Initial Factual Anchor Retrieval.
Given a query q, TRANSMEM first retrieves query-relevant
normal facts:
A(q) = arg topK
f∈Fnormal
s(q, f),
(10)
where Fnormal contains all normal factual memories and s(q, f) is the factual relevance score. The
selected facts A(q) are factual anchors: they are included directly in the final context and also provide
entry points into the transition graph. Each normal fact is stored as an atomic fact edge in Gu with
head and tail nodes; the endpoints of selected factual anchors provide the entry nodes for transition
expansion.
Adaptive Transition Expansion.
Let p denote a partial transition path. For the empty path,
candidate transition edges are adjacent to the endpoints of the factual anchors; after a transition is
accepted, candidates are adjacent to the target endpoint of the current path. For retrieval scoring, each
transition edge is represented by the text of its associated transition record. Let Cp be the trajectory
state of path p, with C∅= E(q). A candidate next transition e is scored by
σ(e | p) = cos(Cp, E(e)) ,
(11)
where E(e) embeds the text associated with transition edge e. A candidate is retained only if it
remains coherent with the current trajectory:
e ∈Φ(p)
⇐⇒
σ(e | p) ≥τc ∧(p = ∅∨σ(e | p) −σlast(p) ≥−δc) ,
(12)
where τc is the continuation threshold, δc is the drop tolerance, and σlast(p) is the score of the most
recently accepted transition in p. The first condition requires the next transition to remain relevant to
the evolving trajectory. The second condition prevents abrupt coherence collapse within a single hop.
When a transition edge e is accepted, its target endpoint determines the next structural neighborhood,
while its associated transition record updates the trajectory state:
Cp⊕e = γCp + (1 −γ)E(e),
(13)
where p ⊕e denotes the path obtained by appending e. This interpolation keeps the trajectory
state anchored to the original query while allowing accepted transition records to steer later hops,
preventing expansion from drifting to locally adjacent but semantically unrelated transitions. The
final retrieved context consists of factual anchors and accepted transition records, allowing the QA
model to resolve stale and current preferences without retrieving unrelated chronological neighbors.

Experiments
3.1
Experimental Setup
Datasets.
We evaluate on two complementary personalized-memory benchmarks. PersonaMem [9]
directly stresses long-context preference evolution: each user is associated with a chronological
message stream and questions spanning seven preference- and memory-related categories, and we
use both its 32k-token and 128k-token context regimes. PersonaBench [25] provides a broader
personalized-QA setting over synthetic private user data and evaluates answer quality with lexical
F1. Together, the two benchmarks test whether transition-aware retrieval helps both in long, topic-
interleaved preference histories and in a separate personalized-QA benchmark. Dataset and evaluation
details are provided in Appendix A.
Baselines.
We compare TRANSMEM against three baseline groups. Zeroshot-only: ZEROMEM
provides no user history. Retrieval-based: NAIVERAG [12] and RF-MEM [36] select passages
by query relevance. Structured memory: ZEP [22], A-MEM [32], MEM0 [4], and LIGHTMEM [5]
maintain external user memory representations.

3.2
Main Results
We first test whether retrieving preference-update records improves end-to-end personalized QA.
Across the evaluated PersonaMem and PersonaBench settings, TRANSMEM achieves the highest
overall score among representative retrieval-based and structured-memory baselines, with the largest
improvement reaching 4.84 overall points. Controlled analyses with factual-only retrieval, chronolog-
ical expansion, flat transition retrieval, and fixed-hop expansion further show that the improvements
are not explained by factual anchoring, temporal-neighbor context, direct transition-text retrieval, or
fixed traversal depth alone.
Table 1 shows that TRANSMEM does not dominate every category, but it consistently improves
the overall score under both backbones. Baselines remain competitive on locally answerable or
stable-fact categories, while TRANSMEM achieves the highest New Scenario accuracy under both
backbones and the highest Track Evolution accuracy with Qwen3-8B. This pattern is consistent with
our view that the useful retrieval unit is often not an isolated fact but the transition that explains how
one user state supersedes another.

3.4
Accuracy–Efficiency Trade-off
We then ask whether the improvements come from retrieving more evidence or more informative
evidence. We measure end-to-end inference time from query submission to final answer, including
retrieval, context assembly, retrieval-time model calls, and QA generation. In our reproduced
pipelines, MEM0 and A-MEM invoke an additional LLM call during retrieval, whereas TRANSMEM
performs embedding-space expansion after memory construction. As shown in Figure 4 (left),
TRANSMEM achieves the highest accuracy with 1.82k inference tokens and 0.98 s latency, compared
with 3.4k tokens and 2.35 s for the next-best system by accuracy, A-MEM. This suggests that the
improvements come from retrieving more targeted evidence rather than simply increasing the amount
of context.
3.5
Analysis of Retrieval Expansion Strategies
Finally, we isolate the expansion mechanism using three controls from the same factual anchors.
CHRONOEXPAND tests whether ordinary temporal neighbors are sufficient by adding chronological
normal-fact neighbors from the same topic timeline. FLATTRANS tests whether transition records
alone are sufficient by retrieving them as flat documents with query–transition similarity, without
following graph endpoints. Fixed-hop variants test whether a manually chosen traversal depth can
replace adaptive stopping. TRANSMEM differs from all three by following graph-grounded transition
endpoints while updating a trajectory state during expansion. These controls correspond to the main
retrieval choices in Figure 2: whether to use transition records, whether to follow grounded endpoints,
and whether to stop adaptively. Further details are in Appendix C.3.
Figure 4 (right) compares these strategies for the Qwen3-8B backbone under both context regimes.
CHRONOEXPAND (53.47% / 46.51%) improves over the worst fixed depths but trails TRANSMEM,
showing that nearby chronological normal facts alone are not enough. FLATTRANS (54.32% /
46.92%) improves further, confirming that transition records carry useful signal, but still falls short of
TRANSMEM (56.84% / 48.03%). This indicates that the gain comes not only from storing transition
text, but also from using the memory graph to choose which transition records to consider next.
Fixed-hop retrieval further shows that no single depth is uniformly appropriate: hop 2 is best in 32k
while hop 3 is best in 128k, whereas adaptive expansion exceeds the best fixed alternative in both
regimes (by 1.68 pp and 0.83 pp respectively). Together, these controls show that transition records

are a useful retrieval unit, but their strongest effect comes from using them in an adaptive graph
expansion rather than retrieving them as isolated text.

Related Work
Agentic memory and memory management.
Long-term agent memory has been studied as a
way to extend LLM-based agents beyond stateless interaction. Previous systems such as Generative
Agents, MemoryBank, and MemGPT introduced persistent experience stores, reflection, hierarchical
memory, and virtual context management for long-horizon interaction [19, 37, 17]. Recent systems
improve memory writing and consolidation through temporal graphs, scalable memory stores, self-
organizing notes, staged consolidation, and recollection-style retrieval [22, 4, 32, 5, 36]. Long-term
dialogue and personalization systems further use event memory, persona modeling, segment-level
memory construction, and persistent user profiles [13, 18, 29]. Other work studies modular, cognitive,
or hierarchical memory organization for long-term agents and multi-agent systems [28, 34, 27, 24, 8,
14, 35]. A-Mem [32] organizes memories as linked notes and updates their contents and connections
as new experiences arrive. TRANSMEM instead stores explicit old-to-new state transitions and
conditions subsequent graph expansion on the transitions already accepted along the retrieval path.
Memory retrieval.
Retrieval-augmented generation retrieves chunks or passages that are seman-
tically relevant to the query [12]. Temporal retrieval and time-aware memory organize evidence
with timestamps, temporal knowledge graphs, or memory-augmented temporal reasoning mod-
ules [20, 22, 31, 26]. Graph-based retrieval further supports multi-hop evidence access through
graph-augmented passage ranking, path retrieval, and graph walks [6, 2, 1]. These approaches
improve access to relevant passages, facts, graph neighborhoods, or reasoning paths. In evolving
personal memory, however, the missing retrieval unit is often the preference update itself: which
earlier state was superseded, what new state replaced it, why the change occurred, and when the new
state became valid. Recent memory systems also explore inference-time controllers for adaptive
retrieval or reader feedback [23, 7], representing a different online-computation trade-off from the
lightweight retrieval setting studied here. TRANSMEM instead moves preference-update interpreta-
tion to offline memory construction and retrieves stored transition records with embedding-based
transition expansion at inference time.
9


5
Conclusion
We studied long-term personal memory under non-stationary user preferences. TRANSMEM stores
preference updates as transition records and retrieves them through factual anchoring followed by
adaptive transition expansion. Across PersonaMem and PersonaBench, TRANSMEM achieves the
strongest overall scores under both Qwen backbones, and controlled analyses show that transition
records and adaptive expansion add value beyond factual anchoring, chronological expansion, flat
transition retrieval, and fixed-hop traversal. These results suggest that preference-update records are
useful retrieval units for personal agents that must distinguish current user states from superseded
ones as users change over time.
6
Limitations
TRANSMEM relies on backbone language models for offline shift detection and final answer gen-
eration, so detector errors, hallucinations, or calibration failures may propagate into retrieval and
responses. Our evaluation focuses on text-based personalized-memory benchmarks and two open-
source Qwen backbones; future work should test broader model families, multimodal histories, and
longer deployment horizons. As long-term personal memory may contain sensitive information
and persistent memories can become stale or be manipulated, practical systems should support user
inspection, correction, deletion, and access control [15]. A promising deployment direction is to store
sensitive memory graphs locally or in user-controlled storage, exposing only the minimal retrieved
evidence needed for each interaction.

Dataset Details
This work focuses on evolving personal preferences rather than general conversational memory.

A.1
PersonaMem
PersonaMem [9] is a long-term personalized-memory benchmark constructed from multi-session

User-case unit.
In PersonaMem, the same persona can appear with different historical chat contexts.

unit. This ensures that each evaluation case is paired with its own personalized memory rather than
merging distinct histories from the same persona name.

B.2
Memory Baselines
RF-Mem [36].
RF-Mem models retrieval through a familiarity–recollection mechanism that first
performs lightweight top-k retrieval and then selectively expands evidence through a slower recollec-
tion path when uncertainty remains. It is a strong retrieval-oriented baseline because it adapts evidence
collection at inference time without explicitly constructing or traversing preference-transition edges.
Zep [22].
Zep builds agent memory on top of a temporal knowledge graph, converting conversations
into entities, relationships, and temporally scoped facts via its Graphiti engine. Outdated facts are
invalidated rather than deleted, so provenance is preserved. We reproduce Zep from its open-source
release rather than its commercial API.
A-Mem [32].
A-Mem introduces an agentic memory system inspired by the Zettelkasten method,
converting each interaction into a structured note with contextual descriptions and keywords, then
dynamically linking it to related historical memories. Unlike flat vector stores, A-Mem emphasizes
note-level self-organization and memory updating, making it a meaningful comparison for preference-
tracking tasks.
Mem0 [4].
Mem0 is a scalable long-term memory architecture that dynamically extracts, consol-
idates, updates, and retrieves salient information from ongoing conversations. We use the graph-
enhanced variant, which additionally represents relational structures among memory items, and
reproduce it from the open-source release rather than the commercial API.
LightMem [5].
LightMem is an efficiency-oriented memory framework using a three-stage pipeline
inspired by the Atkinson–Shiffrin model: sensory memory filters raw interactions, short-term memory
groups and summarizes by topic, and long-term memory performs offline consolidation. This design
reduces online overhead and is a strong baseline for understanding whether structured compression
alone suffices in long-context settings.
B.3
Intra- and inter-topic transitions
Preference updates are not always expressed within a single stable topic boundary. Such updates
may occur within a fine-grained topic graph, such as revising a fitness routine, or across related topic
graphs, such as a change in social life altering what outdoor activities the user now prefers. We
4https://github.com/getzep/graphiti
15


therefore label grounded transition edges by the topic assignment of their endpoints, which helps
separate local refinements from updates whose consequences cross fine-grained topic graphs.
Intra-topic transitions.
An intra-topic transition captures a preference update whose old and new
states are grounded within the same topic graph. For example, a user may initially prefer short
morning workouts but later state that they now prefer longer evening strength sessions after changing
their work schedule. Both states belong to a fitness-routine topic, but the transition record is
still necessary because the later state revises how future fitness-related recommendations should be
interpreted. Thus, intra-topic transitions primarily model local refinements, replacements, or reversals
within a stable topical context.
Inter-topic transitions.
Some preference updates are expressed in one topical context but change
how memories in another topic should be interpreted. For example, a user may initially prefer quick
solo workouts because of a busy schedule, but later prefer longer outdoor activities after joining a
weekend hiking group. The old state may be grounded under a fitness-routine topic, whereas
the new state may be grounded under a social-outdoor-activities topic. Both endpoints are
produced by the same shift detector; the edge is labeled inter-topic only because the grounded
endpoints belong to different topic graphs. Thus, inter-topic transitions model preference updates
whose consequences cross fine-grained topic boundaries.
Figure 3 evaluates the contribution of these two edge types separately, showing that intra-topic and
inter-topic transitions provide complementary retrieval signals.

C.3
Various Retrieval Strategies
We include two inference-only controls to separate the effect of transition-aware retrieval from
simpler context-expansion alternatives. Both controls reuse the same constructed memory and final
QA prompt as TRANSMEM, but replace adaptive transition expansion with a simpler retrieval strategy.
The three expansion-control variants compare as follows:
• CHRONOEXPAND: factual anchors plus chronological normal-fact neighbors.
• FLATTRANS: factual anchors plus flat retrieved transition records.
• TRANSMEM: factual anchors plus graph-grounded adaptive transition expansion.
ChronoExpand.
CHRONOEXPAND starts from the same factual anchors as TRANSMEM. For each
anchor fact, it locates the anchor in the same topic’s fact timeline (sorted by valid_at/created_at),
retrieves the h nearest normal facts on each side, and merges the result into the final context under
the same token budget. It does not use transition records and does not follow transition endpoints.
This tests whether nearby temporal context alone can explain the gains of TRANSMEM.
21


For example, if an anchor fact states that the user recently joined a hiking group, CHRONOEXPAND
may also retrieve the immediately preceding and following normal facts in the same outdoor-activity
timeline. It does not know whether those neighboring facts describe a preference update or merely
adjacent context.
We select h on a 20-user diagnostic subset (257 queries) using the same procedure as inference
hyperparameter search (Appendix C.6). Table 12 reports overall accuracy for h ∈{1, 2, 3}.

FlatTrans.
FLATTRANS starts from the same factual anchors as TRANSMEM. It then collects all
transition records associated with the user’s active topic groups—both intra-topic transition edges
and inter-topic transition edges—and ranks them as flat documents by cosine similarity between the
query embedding and each transition record embedding. The top-KT transition records are merged
with the factual anchors under the same final context budget and QA prompt as TRANSMEM. Unlike
TRANSMEM, FLATTRANS does not follow transition endpoints, does not maintain a trajectory state,
and does not adaptively expand across hops. This tests whether transition text is sufficient once
factual anchors are fixed, independently of graph-grounded endpoint traversal.
For example, if the query asks for a current cultural recommendation, FLATTRANS may retrieve
transition records mentioning cultural participation, but it does not require those records to be
reachable from the factual anchors or to form a coherent endpoint-grounded update path.
On Qwen3-8B, FLATTRANS reaches 54.32% (32k) and 46.92% (128k), which are the values reported
alongside CHRONOEXPAND and TRANSMEM in Section 3.5.

We study the main inference-stage hyperparameters of TRANSMEM on a randomly selected sample
of 20 users from PersonaMem 32k, comprising 257 evaluation queries. To keep the search tractable,
the retrieval configuration is tuned sequentially: we first vary the factual-anchor budget (Equation 10),
then the trajectory-retention factor γ (Equation 13), and finally the continuation/stopping rule (τc, δc)
(Equation 12). The resulting configuration is used for all main full-evaluation experiments. For the
128k setting, the trajectory-related configuration chosen from this study is carried over, and only the
factual-anchor budget is retuned to account for longer histories.

C.7
Case Analysis
The categories where TRANSMEM is not consistently strongest tend to involve either locally answer-
able latest-state lookup or open-ended ideation. In the former case, a strong note- or profile-based
memory baseline can succeed by directly retrieving the most recent surface fact, while transition
expansion may add little if the question does not require resolving a supersession relation. In the
latter case, performance depends not only on memory recovery but also on the backbone’s ability to
creatively extrapolate from retrieved evidence. These cases suggest that transition records are most
beneficial when the answer depends on determining which prior state has been replaced, rather than
when the task is direct lookup or unconstrained generation.
We illustrate when adaptive transition expansion helps and when it can introduce noise through four
PersonaMem cases.
Case 1: Aligned Recommendation (856b)
Query. Recommend a local weekend event that is both cultural and interactive.
Options. (a) interactive boutique art festival; (b) refusal; (c) literary festival; (d) food/drink tasting.
Outcome. Gold (a); factual-anchor-only (c); TRANSMEM (a).
Representative factual anchors.
• Conducted an insightful interview with a local author.
• Is involved in organizing community events.
• Personalized recommendations aim to create engaging interaction.
Accepted transition records.
• Passive literary engagement →active literary-community participation (intra-topic; rationale: national
literature festival).
• Reading for personal enjoyment →engaging with literature through interviews and creative-process explo-
ration (intra-topic; rationale: interview with a local author).
• Passive literature consumption →active participation in literary communities and discussions (inter-topic;
rationale: book-signing events at indie bookstores).
Interpretation. The factual anchors make the literary option plausible but remain isolated: they do not
specify whether the user’s current preference is passive literary attendance or participatory cultural
interaction. The accepted transition records shift the evidence toward active community-facing
cultural participation, which is better aligned with the interaction-heavy art festival. These records
provide complementary evidence for the preference update.
Case 2: Preference-Arc Recovery (7d41)
Query. Inspired by a travel memoir, the user restarted emotionally rich travel journaling. Which option best describes the
attitude toward travel?
Options. (a) disliking→liking→liking; (b) loving→disliking→liking; (c) no prior interest→liking→liking; (d) lik-
ing→disliking→liking. Note: the original answer choices are long; we show the preference-trajectory keywords.
Outcome. Gold (d); factual-anchor-only (b); TRANSMEM (d).
Representative factual anchors.
• Started a travel journal to capture emotions and stories from adventures.
• Experiences anxiety when thinking about organizing a trip.
• Travel no longer brings the same excitement.
Accepted transition record.
• Enjoying travel planning and exploring new places →experiencing anxiety and difficulty with travel planning
(intra-topic; rationale: rising pressure from cooking and surprise-trip planning).
Interpretation. The anchors expose current journal interest and earlier anxiety, while the transition
explicitly links earlier enjoyment of travel planning to later anxiety. Together, they support the
liking→disliking→liking arc in (d). The factual-anchor-only model instead selects (b), which
overstates the initial preference as loving rather than liking.

Case 3: Expansion Noise on Fact Recall (da4a)
Query. What are some fun ways to bring family together during the weekends?
Options. (a) family movie night; (b) garden project; (c) cooking challenge; (d) board-game tournament.
Outcome. Gold (d); factual-anchor-only (d); adaptive TRANSMEM (8B) (a).
Representative factual anchor.
• Games are chosen as one of the activities to bring the family together.
Accepted transition record (most salient).
• Awkward campfire gatherings →family movie nights (intra-topic; rationale: movie nights felt more comfortable
and enriching).
Interpretation. The factual anchors support the gold board-game option. The accepted transition adds
a competing “family movie nights” signal, and the 8B QA model selects that option after expansion.
Qwen3.5-27B answers correctly from the same context, suggesting that the backbone affects how the
retrieved evidence is used.
Case 4: Over-Grounding on Open-Ended Idea Generation (0787)
Query. What new creative writing pursuits could I explore to further engage with other readers and reflect on literary
works?
Options. (a) blog posts / literature podcast; (b) literary salons at home; (c) online writers’ workshops; (d) poetry reading
circle.
Outcome. Gold (a); adaptive TRANSMEM (8B) (b).
Representative factual anchors.
• Enjoys reading literary fiction.
• Values reflecting on books with others.
Accepted transition records.
• Reading for personal enjoyment →engaging with literature through interviews and creative-process explo-
ration (intra-topic; rationale: insightful interview with a local author).
• Passive consumption of literature →active participation in literary communities and discussions (inter-topic;
rationale: book-signing events at indie bookstores).
Interpretation. The transition records are coherent and locally faithful to the user’s literary trajectory.
For an open-ended idea-generation query, however, they over-ground the model in familiar in-person
literary-community formats, biasing the 8B backbone toward a conservative preference-consistent
continuation (literary salons) rather than the more creative outward-facing formats in the gold answer.
This case supports the main-text observation that New Ideas does not consistently benefit from deeper
retrieval. Partly off-topic retrieved context may also contribute to this error.
