InfoOrgBench: Can Agents Turn Scattered Information into Coherent
Hierarchies?
Justice Ou1,2*, Lanming Ma3, Zijian Zhang4, Yilun Zhao5
1Microsoft, 2University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign
3New York University, 4University of Michigan, 5Yale University
Abstract
When agents organize records into folders, they
may correctly group the excerpts of a design
proposal yet place the proposal under the wrong
owning group. Existing agent benchmarks do
not directly measure such higher-level errors.
We introduce INFOORGBENCH, a benchmark
of 150 tasks in which agents organize scattered
records from meeting recaps, issue threads and
design proposals into hierarchies.
Integrity
checks verify that every record is preserved,
and a Structure Score measures agreement with
the source hierarchy at every level, indepen-
dent of folder names and order. We evaluate 45
systems that pair nine models with five agent
harnesses. The best system scores 83.2 out
of 100, but no system exceeds 78 on meeting
recaps or issue threads. Model choice affects
scores far more than harness choice: model
means span about 27 points, whereas the aver-
age effect of each harness is below one point.
More importantly, parent-level relations have
higher error rates than leaf-level relations in all
three domains. In design proposals, nearly half
of the outputs analyzed by level make exactly
this kind of error. These results show that eval-
uating information organization requires mea-
suring higher-level relations separately from
record preservation and local grouping.

Introduction
Agents increasingly collect and produce records
for people, such as meeting notes, issue discus-
sions and design documents. Keeping these records
useful requires more than putting related items to-
gether: each group must also sit under the right
parent, because people rely on folder hierarchies
both to find information later and to plan how a
project divides into parts (Jones et al., 2005). The
two requirements can come apart. An agent may
*Corresponding author: zo6@illinois.edu.
gather every excerpt of a design proposal into one
folder yet file the proposal under the wrong owning
group. Such an output keeps every record, so an in-
ventory check would count it as complete, whereas
a binary exact-match criterion would score it as
the same failure as an output whose entire hierar-
chy is disrupted. Evaluating organization therefore
requires separate, graded measures of record preser-
vation, local grouping and the higher-level relations
that connect groups.
Existing agent benchmarks mainly evaluate task
completion, such as resolving repository issues, op-
erating websites, desktops and enterprise software,
or working with large file collections (Jimenez
et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2024; Xie et al., 2024;
Drouin et al., 2024; Tang et al., 2026). Work on
information organization uses language models
to cluster texts, build taxonomies and route doc-
uments into an existing folder hierarchy (Zhang
et al., 2023; Wan et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2026a).
These settings do not directly isolate whether an
agent, given a fixed collection of records, recovers
both the local groups and the parent relations that
connect them. We therefore ask how reliably agents
recover the relations between groups of records, be-
yond preserving the records or clustering them.
We introduce INFOORGBENCH, a benchmark
of 150 tasks in which agents organize scattered
records into folders. The tasks span three families
built from public sources (Figure 1): meeting re-
caps (Recap) group AMI summary sentences (Car-
letta et al., 2006) by meeting and section, issue
threads (Thread) group Jira records by parent issue
and subtask, and design proposals (Design) group
Kubernetes proposal excerpts (contributors, 2026)
by owning group and proposal. Agents see the
record contents and an organizing criterion, while
the reference hierarchy recorded in the sources
remains with the evaluator. After each episode,
final-state checks confirm that every record is kept
unchanged and filed, and a Structure Score com-

Each agent works through a harness, which sets
its tools, the history it keeps and when the episode
ends, so we evaluate all 45 systems that pair nine
models with five agent harnesses. GPT-6.1 Sol
achieves the highest score under every harness,
reaching 83.2 out of 100; by family, no system
exceeds 78 on Recap or Thread, whereas the best
reaches 95.8 on Design. The choice of model af-
fects scores far more than the choice of harness:
model means span about 27 points, whereas the
average effect of each harness is below one point.
Analyzing errors by hierarchy level, we find that
parent-level relations have higher error rates than
leaf-level relations in every family. In Recap, more
than half of the output folders mix notes from dif-
ferent meetings; in Thread, half of the outputs omit
the parent-issue layer; in Design, nearly half of
the outputs analyzed by level keep every proposal
group intact but place some under the wrong own-
ing group. These results indicate that the evaluated
agents recover local groups more reliably than the
hierarchy that connects them.
We make the following contributions:
• We introduce INFOORGBENCH, a benchmark
of 150 tasks in which agents organize scat-
tered records into hierarchies, with references
constructed deterministically from hierarchies
recorded in public sources.
• We design an evaluation that checks record
preservation separately from structure and scores
structural agreement at each hierarchy level with
a Structure Score that ignores folder names and
penalizes excess single-child folders.
• We evaluate 45 systems that pair nine mod-
els with five harnesses and find that, in every
task family, agents recover parent-level relations
less reliably than leaf-level groups, and that
model choice affects scores far more than har-
ness choice.

INFOORGBENCH Benchmark
This section defines the organization task, describes
how tasks and their reference hierarchies are built
from public sources, and characterizes the resulting
data and the dependence among tasks.
3.1
Task
The task maps a flat or scattered collection into
a hierarchy. Inputs provide stable record identi-
fiers, an organizing criterion and task-specific ev-
idence. Leaves retain every input record exactly
once; internal nodes express groups and subgroups.
An agent inspects and moves records through har-
ness tools, and independent capture makes the final
workspace the scored artifact. Structural compari-
son ignores consistent label renaming and sibling
order. Appendix A gives the evidence boundaries
and worked task examples.
Design groups substantive proposal excerpts by
owning group and proposal. Recap groups sum-
mary sentences by source meeting and section;
Thread groups issue and comment records by par-
ent issue and subtask. Source metadata fixes the
hidden target, while supplied text and the criterion
define the intended task evidence. Generated prose
lies outside the output contract.
3.2
Construction
Construction selects source records while retaining
source-native relations. Kubernetes Enhancement
Proposals provide documents, ownership metadata
and heading paths. AMI links summary sentences

3.3
Data
Recap uses AMI meeting summaries. Table 2 de-
scribes retained declared inputs with 2,436 Recap,
2,421 Thread and 1,800 Design record appearances.
Every Design packet has 36 records, four owners
and 12 proposals: 600 proposal appearances across

The public sources retain their licenses. Source
manifests record provenance and exclusions fol-
lowing the documentation principles of Datasheets
for Datasets (Gebru et al., 2021); the task appendix
also states the artifact-specific license, attribution
and redistribution boundaries.
3.4
Reference Construction
The evaluator reference is reconstructed determin-
istically from the pinned source hierarchy: meeting
and summary-section membership for Recap, par-
ent issue and subtask membership for Thread, and
owning group and proposal paths for Design. Ma-
terialization assigns opaque record identifiers only
after these relations are fixed and stores source-
native identifiers, paths and content hashes outside
the agent workspace. Replay checks verify the
packet and reference bytes before scoring. The
resulting gold target measures reconstruction of
the source organization; alternative useful organi-
zations remain a separate question of downstream
utility (Zhao et al., 2024).
3.5
Dependence
The scored packets are overlapping development
instances, without a held-out source-disjoint split.
A packet can contain several recurring source units,
so record appearances and task counts are not in-
dependent sample sizes. Source identifiers support
dependence-aware descriptions.
4
Evaluation
This section defines how outputs are scored and de-
scribes the harnesses, the systems and the protocol
under which they are run.
4.1
Metrics
We compare each output hierarchy O with the refer-
ence hierarchy R over the same records. An output
is valid if every record appears exactly once with
unchanged content. Invalid outputs score 0.
Agreement is measured with rooted triples,
which compare hierarchies through record identi-
ties and ignore folder names (Sand et al., 2013;
Critchlow et al., 1996).
A hierarchy resolves
records a, b and c as ab|c when a and b share a
deeper common ancestor than either shares with c.
Below a shared wrapper folder, every reference has
two levels: parent groups (meetings, parent issues
or SIGs) and leaf groups within a parent. Each
triple belongs to the level at which its records sep-
arate in R. At each level, recall is the fraction of
triples resolved by R that O resolves the same way,
and precision is the fraction of triples resolved by
O that agree with R. Level-balanced F1, F bal

4.2
Harness
Agent performance depends on both the model and
the harness, the layer around the model that man-
ages tools, context and episode termination (Yao
et al., 2026). We therefore evaluate each model
with five harnesses and score the final workspace
state (Xie et al., 2024).
We implement ReAct-FC, a ReAct agent (Yao
et al., 2023) that acts through function calls. It
provides five tools for listing, reading, searching
and moving records and for checking the inventory.
The model keeps the full episode history, including
its reasoning items.
We also evaluate four public harnesses: Ope-
nAI’s Codex CLI (OpenAI, 2026b), DeepSeek Har-
ness (DSH) (DeepSeek-AI, 2026), Pi (Earendil
Works, 2026) and Deep Agents (LangChain, 2026).
Each uses its own tools, built-in instructions and
history management. In every harness, the final file
paths and contents are captured outside the agent
and define the outcome. Appendices B and C give
the task instructions and run settings.
4.3
Systems
We pair nine models with the five harnesses of Sec-
tion 4.2, which gives 45 systems (model–harness
configurations).
The models come from four
providers: Claude Haiku 4.5 (Anthropic, 2025),
Claude Haiku 5.5 (Anthropic, 2026a) and Claude
Sonnet 5.5 (Anthropic, 2026b) from Anthropic,
GPT-5.6 Luna and GPT-5.6 Terra (OpenAI, 2026c),
GPT-6 Luna (OpenAI, 2026d) and GPT-6.1 Sol
(OpenAI, 2026a) from OpenAI, Grok 4.7 (xAI,
2026) from xAI, and MAI-Code-1.1-Flash (Mi-
crosoft, 2026) from Microsoft. All models use
medium reasoning effort, except Claude Haiku 4.5,
which uses the provider default. Appendix C lists
the model identifiers and run settings.
4.4
Protocol
Each system runs one episode per task on all 150
tasks, and each family score averages its 50 tasks
with equal weight. Tasks within a family can reuse
source documents. Rescoring the captured outputs
of 17 systems with the released scorer reproduces
every published task score.
5
Results
This section reports the scores of all 45 systems,
then compares the effects of models and harnesses
and the differences across task families.
5.1
Main Results
Table 3 reports Structure Score, pooled Triplet F1
and the economy penalty for the 45 systems. The
highest-scoring system, GPT-6.1 Sol with DSH,
reaches an average Structure Score of 83.2, and the
lowest-scoring system reaches 52.5. All systems
score lower on Recap and Thread than on Design.
Because folder economy is linear in λ, setting λ to
0 or doubling it to 0.4 changes a family mean by at
most its economy penalty in Table 3. This penalty
is at most 5 points in every Recap cell and in all
but one Design cell, but exceeds 5 points in 29 of
the 45 Thread cells, where λ matters most.
GPT-6.1 Sol obtains the highest average score
with every harness (80.9–83.2). Its mean over har-
nesses (82.3) is 10.8 points above that of Claude
Sonnet 5.5 (71.5), the second-best model. Among
the other models, the ranking differs across task
families.
Sonnet ranks second because of its
Thread score (72.9, compared with 76.9 for Sol),
while its Recap score (48.8) is below that of
Grok 4.7 (53.2). Within each model line, the newer

Haiku 4.5 by 10.7 points, and GPT-6 Luna exceeds
GPT-5.6 Luna by 2.5 points. Appendix E gives
model means and per-task score distributions, and
Appendix G lists the source runs.

5.2
Harness Effects
The harness has a smaller effect on scores than the
model. Averaged over models, the effect of each
harness on S ranges from −0.7 to +0.6 points,
while model means span 27.2 points. For seven of
the nine models, scores across harnesses differ by
at most 2.3 points. The largest differences are 3.4
points for MAI-Code-1.1-Flash and 5.3 points for
Claude Haiku 4.5.
Harness effects of this size are close to the varia-
tion between repeated runs. We ran GPT-5.6 Terra
with Codex three times with identical settings (Ta-
ble 4). The three mean scores differ by at most
0.4 points, while the score of an individual task
changes by 5.9 points on average between two runs.
Differences of about one point between systems,
which include most harness effects, are therefore
comparable to run-to-run variation.
5.3
Task Families
The highest scores are 77.8 on Recap, 77.9 on
Thread and 95.8 on Design. Families separate mod-
els differently. On Design, Claude Haiku 4.5 and
MAI-Code-1.1-Flash average 77.9 and 78.1, while
all other models exceed 88. On Recap, all models
but GPT-6.1 Sol average 53.2 or less.
Low scores are distributed differently across the
tasks of each family. On Design, the ten lowest-
scoring tasks account for 44% of the total shortfall
from the maximum score, compared with 25% on
Recap (Figure 12). Design errors thus concentrate
in a few tasks, while Recap errors are spread across
most tasks. Section 6 analyzes the errors behind
these differences.

6
Analysis
This section examines where outputs disagree with
the reference, how errors relate to task properties,
whether more test-time compute helps, and how
outputs recover complete groups.
6.1
Error Analysis

SIGs) and leaf-level relations (between summary
sections, subtasks or KEPs). For each triple of
records that the reference distinguishes, the output
is agreed, conflicting if it groups a different pair
together, or unresolved if it does not separate the
three records. We analyze the outputs of the sys-
tems with per-level data, 25 for Recap and Design
and 21 for Thread (Figure 2). A single output can
contain several error types.

GPT-6.1 Sol from other models. In the two Sol
systems with per-level data, parent-level agreement
on Recap is about twice the other systems’ median,
and the share of unresolved parent-level relations
on Thread is about two thirds lower, while leaf-

Parent-Level Conflicts in Recap.
Conflicts are
the most frequent parent-level error in Recap (35%
of parent-level triples, compared with 7% in Thread
and 2% in Design). They arise when agents group
notes along a dimension other than the source meet-
ing, for example one folder per project phase that
mixes notes from several meetings. Agents open
96–100% of the notes, yet 54% of output folders
contain notes from multiple meetings. This indi-
cates that agents group notes by topic rather than
by source meeting.
Missing Parent Layers in Thread.
Unresolved
parent-level relations are the most frequent error
in Thread (54% of parent-level triples, compared
with 27% in Recap and 12% in Design). They
arise when agents place each subtask in its own
top-level folder without parent issues, for example
13 top-level folders for 13 subtasks that belong to
two parent issues. The parent-issue layer is ab-
sent in half of the Thread outputs, and its presence
varies across runs and harnesses. Between identical
runs, 76% of large per-task score changes coincide
with the layer being added or removed, and in one
task the same model builds both parent issues with


Codex and neither with ReAct-FC.
Misattached Leaf Groups in Design.
In Design,
agents recover 96% of the reference KEP groups,
and 596 of 1,250 outputs have SIG-level errors
without KEP-level errors, for example an intact
storage KEP filed under apps.
6.2
Task Properties
The errors relate to how well record content identi-
fies the reference groups. Scores increase with the
lexical separability of the reference groups, namely
meetings on Recap (ρ = 0.48) and subtasks on
Thread (ρ = 0.67), and both correlations remain
significant after Holm correction (Appendix K).
The Recap correlation is consistent with the topic-
based grouping described in Section 6.1. In Thread,
a deterministic record-quality screen flags at least
one low-information record in 42 tasks, including
22 tasks in which identical text is assigned to dif-
ferent subtasks. Content alone cannot place such
records in their reference group, which is consis-
tent with lower Thread scores on tasks with more
low-information records (Appendix K). To estimate
how much of the reference such records leave un-
determined, we exchange the reference paths of
records with identical text and count the triples
whose relation changes. On average, these triples
make up 7.7% of leaf-level relations in Thread and
1.6% in Recap, and at most 0.3% of parent-level
relations (Appendix K). Because similar but non-
identical records are not exchanged, these shares
are a lower bound on ambiguity from record con-
tent. The task instruction asks agents to choose the
best-supported folder even when the evidence is in-
conclusive (Figure 6), so these records are always
placed in some group, even when their content
does not identify one. In Design, the records do not
name the owning SIG, so the SIG level has to be
inferred from the content of each proposal. Thread
records, in contrast, contain 561 issue keys and
618 pull-request references, which can indicate the
reference grouping (Appendix K).
6.3
Test-Time Compute
We ask whether more test-time compute helps,
across models and, for one system, through higher
reasoning effort or run aggregation.
Token Use Across Models.
The relation between
token use and score differs by task family (Ta-
ble 16). On Thread, Claude Sonnet 5.5 scores 3.2
points below GPT-6.1 Sol with about one fifth of its
tokens per task. On Recap, Sol scores 27.1 points
above Sonnet with about four times as many tokens.
Token counts come from each harness and are not
fully comparable across harnesses (Appendix L).
Reasoning Effort and Run Aggregation.
For
GPT-5.6 Terra with Codex, neither higher reason-
ing effort nor aggregation of repeated runs raises
the mean score (Table 4). High reasoning effort
uses 12% more tokens and changes the mean by
−1.0 points (95% CI [−3.0, +0.7]), and its per-
task changes are no larger than those between iden-
tical runs. Combining three runs without the ref-
erence, by consensus or by majority vote, changes
the mean by −0.2 and +0.5 points at three times
the token cost. Selecting each task’s best run with
the reference adds 4.5 points, an upper bound for
selection among three runs.
6.4
Additional Diagnostics
Structure Score rewards the relations that the refer-
ence resolves, so an output that splits a reference
group into further subfolders can still score 100
(Appendix D.3). We therefore computed two mea-
sures of complete-group recovery, clade F1 and ex-
act recovery, for a subset of runs: the ReAct-FC out-
puts of GPT-6.1 Sol and Claude Haiku 4.5 on Re-
cap and Thread. Although these runs reach Struc-
ture Scores between 40.8 and 77.9, GPT-6.1 Sol
reproduces the reference hierarchy in one and two
of the 50 tasks per family, and Claude Haiku 4.5 in
none (Appendix D.4).
7
Conclusion
We introduced INFOORGBENCH, a benchmark
of 150 tasks in which agents organize scattered
records into hierarchies, scored against the hierar-
chies recorded in their sources. Across 45 systems,
errors concentrate above the leaf level, and their
form follows the available evidence: agents group
Recap notes by topic rather than by meeting, omit
the parent-issue layer in half of the Thread outputs,
and file intact Design proposals under the wrong
owning group, which the records never name. The
model matters far more than the harness, and for the
system we tested, neither higher reasoning effort
nor run aggregation helps, so gains likely depend
on inferring how groups relate rather than on more
compute. Even GPT-6.1 Sol with ReAct-FC re-
produces the complete reference hierarchy in at
most two of 50 Recap or Thread tasks. Evaluations
of organization should therefore score higher-level


relations separately and compare systems over re-
peated runs. We release all code.
Limitations
The benchmark contains 150 English tasks built
from three public sources, and tasks within each
family reuse source material; all Thread tasks, for
example, draw on the same three parent issues. The
tasks are therefore not independent samples, and
the benchmark has no held-out split with disjoint
sources, so scores may not transfer to other sources,
domains or languages. Because the sources are pub-
lic, some records may also appear in pretraining
data, and we do not measure the effect of such ex-
posure. Each reference is the hierarchy recorded in
the source data, such as the meeting to which a sum-
mary sentence belongs or the group that owns a pro-
posal, but other organizations of the same records
can also be useful to readers. Scores therefore mea-
sure agreement with the source hierarchy rather
than the usefulness of an organization to people,
which would require a separate user study. Finally,
we run each system once per task. In three repeated
runs of one system (Section 6.3), the mean score
varies by 0.4 points, whereas individual task scores
change by 5.9 points on average. Differences of
about one point between systems, including most
harness effects, are therefore comparable to run-to-
run variation and may not reflect a model or harness
effect.

A
Task Contract
This appendix specifies what the agent and the eval-
uator each see, how tasks and references are built
from the pinned sources, the source licenses and
worked task examples.
A.1
Information Boundary
An episode begins with opaque record identifiers
and unchanged text files in one inbox. The agent
receives an organizing criterion and can inspect the
supplied records. Source parent assignments and
the reference tree are held by the evaluator. Every
record must occur once in the final hierarchy with
its original bytes. Generated folder names express
the grouping independently of source labels.
Final paths and record bodies, captured inde-
pendently of the agent’s last message, define the
output. The final JSON declaration mirrors that
captured state. Inventory checking reports preser-
vation and filing; structural agreement and parent
quality remain evaluator-side quantities.
A.2
Task Categories

family.
A.3
Deterministic Reference Construction
The source-derived reference tree is constructed
rather than annotated. Agreement measures for
coder-labelled targets concern a different construc-
tion regime (Artstein and Poesio, 2008). Recap
paths come from AMI meeting and summary-
section membership, Thread paths come from Jira
parent-issue and subtask membership, and Design
paths come from owning-group and proposal di-
rectories. These relations are fixed before records
receive opaque task identifiers.
Retained references bind evaluator paths and
content hashes, with source-native identifiers where
available.
The scored packets retain exact du-
plicates under different parents; no completed
unique-reference admission filter is established for
this suite. Byte checks verify identified retained
packet/reference payloads, but some exports lack
a complete upstream native-record join. These are
separate questions from deterministic scoring, re-
coverability and downstream usefulness.
A.4
Materialization Recipe
Recap uses the AMI manual annotations, version
1.6.2 (Carletta et al., 2006). Each summary file
supplies a meeting, its summary sections and their
sentences. A record is the text of one sentence with
outer whitespace removed, and its reference path
is the meeting and section. Thread records are Jira
subtasks, whose text joins the summary, description
and status, and Jira comments, whose text is the
comment body. Parent and subtask membership
comes from the recorded Jira links rather than from
topic inference.
Recap and Thread tasks are drawn from com-
plete source bundles with 40 to 60 records and
at least two top-level groups.
Empty records
and duplicate source identifiers are rejected, and
the record sets of any two selected tasks overlap
by at most half of the smaller set, measured as
|A ∩B|/ min(|A|, |B|). Each family uses the first
50 eligible tasks in a fixed candidate order. Before
opaque identifiers are assigned, the records of each
task are shuffled with a fixed seed.
Design uses a pinned revision of the Kubernetes
Enhancements repository (contributors, 2026). A
proposal is eligible if it has README and meta-
data files with the same owning group, a title and
a numeric identifier. Excerpts come from second-
to fourth-level README sections in a fixed or-
der of preference: Summary, Motivation, Goals,
Non-goals, Proposal, Design details, User stories,
Risks and mitigations, Drawbacks, and Alterna-
tives. Comments, code blocks, links, URLs and
table rows are removed, excerpts contain 400 to
1,600 characters, and excerpts that name the own-
ing group or proposal directly are rejected. Each
task has four owning groups, three proposals per
group and three excerpts per proposal. A seeded
selection limits each proposal to three tasks and the
overlap between tasks to one half, and then prefers
proposal sets whose owning groups are lexically
similar. Model scores play no part in selection.
Public records contain only an identifier, a for-
mat label and the extracted text; the evaluator keeps
the source path and a hash of each record. Rebuild-
ing one Recap task with 51 records and one De-
sign task with 36 records from the pinned source
archives reproduces their packet and reference files
exactly. Thread tasks come from a recorded Jira
snapshot and were not rebuilt from the upstream
source.

D.5
Folder Economy
The folder-economy equation uses u for the count
of one-child folders and n for the count of output
folders. Only one-child structure in excess of the
reference count activates the correction. A wrapper
can preserve all closest-pair relations while adding
a navigation step; relation agreement and this folder
correction therefore measure different properties.
Structure Score multiplies level-balanced agree-
ment by folder economy.
An entirely flat tree asserts zero resolved distinc-
tions. Filing and structure are therefore distinct:
with Deep Agents, three MAI-Code-1.1-Flash Re-
cap outputs file every record into one common
folder and receive a Structure Score of 0. A near-
correct hierarchy can retain many record relation-
ships while differing at one group. Extra refine-
ment, merged groups, missing records and unread-


able inputs receive separate treatment. The illus-
trative structures describe scoring behavior; human
navigation preference and downstream utility are
separate constructs.
D.6
Aggregation
Each task-family mean assigns equal weight to its
scored task instances. For each metric, Avg is the
arithmetic mean of the three family means, giving
Recap, Thread and Design equal weight. Structure
Score is computed per task before averaging; mul-
tiplying averaged components would change the
estimand. An average requires all three family val-
ues. It summarizes the contributing configurations
and preserves their access, stopping and sampling
qualifications.
Repeated episodes are averaged within a collec-
tion before aggregation across collections, with
task-family rows retained. The exploratory de-
sign packets contain repeated proposals:
600
proposal slots draw from 294 unique proposals.
Independent-workspace uncertainty therefore uses
source collections instead of triples or record ap-
pearances.

Error Definitions and a Worked
Example
Triples and levels.
The scorer compares the ref-
erence and output trees on triples of records. A
triple is reference-distinguishable when two of its
records share a deeper folder in the reference than
either shares with the third. Its level is set by the
reference: parent if the three records are not all
under the same parent (e.g. two in one SIG, one in
another), leaf if they share a parent but not a leaf
(e.g. two in one KEP, one in a sibling KEP).
Outcomes.
For each triple of records that the ref-
erence distinguishes, the output is agreed if it pairs
the same two records more closely, conflicting if it
pairs a different two, and unresolved if it places all
three at the same depth. Conflicts express a wrong
relation and lower precision and recall; unresolved
triples express a missing relation and lower recall.
Score.
S = F bal
1
× E (Equation 2), where F bal
1
averages triple precision and recall across levels
before combining them, so that the many parent-
level relations do not outweigh the fewer leaf-level
ones, and the economy factor
E = 1 −0.2 × excess single-child folders
output folders
penalizes empty shells. Folder names, folder order
and a wrapper folder shared by all records do not
affect S. Economy accounts for 1–10% of the
shortfall 1 −S depending on the family; the rest is
relation recovery.
Output categories.
Each output receives one ex-
clusive category: none (no structural errors; same
tree up to names and order), parent (all leaf-level
triples agreed, some parent-level triple not), leaf
(the reverse), or both. Table 13 reports the triple
outcomes and categories by family.
