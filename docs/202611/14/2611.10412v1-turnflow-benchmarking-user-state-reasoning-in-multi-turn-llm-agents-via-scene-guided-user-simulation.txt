Abstract
Large Language Models (LLMs) are increas-
ingly deployed through conversational inter-
faces, yet strong single-turn performance does
not imply reliability in multi-turn settings.
Multi-turn assistance requires a model to main-
tain an evolving representation of user require-
ments and to reason over it before complying
with later requests. Failures can therefore be
subtle: models may fail to recognize a changed
constraint or hidden conflict, or recognize it
without enforcing it in the final response. Ex-
isting benchmarks primarily test fully specified
prompts, long-context recall, or controlled do-
main environments, leaving open-domain re-
quirement reconciliation undermeasured. We
introduce TurnFlow, an interactive benchmark
for evaluating whether models preserve, revise,
and act on user requirements across turns. Turn-
Flow combines adaptive user simulation with
verifiable judging, allowing conversations to
unfold naturally while keeping hidden require-
ments and pass/fail criteria controlled. In our
evaluation, nine LLMs achieve overall pass
rates of 42.86% −−59.59% on 490 scenarios.
Performance varies substantially across chal-
lenge types: models perform well on Stateful
Multi-Constraint Following but remain consid-
erably less reliable on Scope Creep and Goal
Shift. These results reveal persistent limitations
in reasoning over evolving user requirements
across turns.

To fill this gap, we propose TurnFlow, a scene-
guided user-simulation framework for benchmark-
ing user-state reasoning in multi-turn LLM agents.
Figure 2 provides an overview of its three core
modules: the scene constructor, reactive user simu-
lator, and response verifier. Specifically, the scene
constructor transforms web-derived domain seeds
into diverse scenario specifications that govern
the conversations in which the evaluated model
participates. These specifications encode latent
contextual elements, including constraints and
user personas, staged user objectives, and veri-
fiable outcome criteria. The reactive user simu-
lator, named BRIDGE (Behavior-Rule-Informed
Dialogue Generation Engine), subsequently inter-
acts with the evaluated model while faithfully ad-
hering to the corresponding scenario specification.
Finally, the response verifier takes as input the rele-
vant facts and the required and prohibited outcomes
specified by the scenario, extracts supporting evi-
dence from the model’s final response, and deter-
mines whether it satisfies the predefined criteria.
Figure 3 illustrates a concrete example in which
a beginner’s Excel VBA inventory tracker gradu-
ally expands to include barcode scanning, Square
POS integration, real-time synchronization across
multiple locations, supplier automation, and de-
mand forecasting. When the final user request asks
for a timeline to build the entire system within a
couple of months, the correct response must rec-
ognize that the accumulated scope has become an
enterprise-level software project and reject the pro-
posed timeline rather than merely comply with the
latest request.
TurnFlow reveals pronounced differences in
model performance across five challenge types.
Across nine evaluated LLMs, the highest overall
pass rate is 59.59%. Models perform strongly on
Stateful Multi-Constraint Following, yet remain
substantially less reliable on Scope Creep and Goal
Shift. No model achieves the highest pass rate
across all five categories. These results highlight
the importance of evaluating different aspects of
user-state reasoning rather than relying solely on
an aggregate score.

Related Work
Instruction-following and Multi-turn Bench-
marks.
Instruction-following benchmarks (Zhou
et al., 2023; Wen et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025;
He et al., 2024; Qin et al., 2025) verify whether
models satisfy explicit constraints in single-turn
prompts. Multi-turn benchmarks (Zheng et al.,
2023; Bai et al., 2024; Kwan et al., 2024; Wu et al.,
2024; Deshpande et al., 2025; Laban et al., 2025)
broaden to dialogue-grounded behavior, and open-
ended systems (Lin et al., 2025; Chiang et al., 2024)
capture real-user or preference signals. Most prior
work evaluates fixed prompts, static transcripts, or
predetermined user trajectories, thereby leaving re-
active user behavior largely outside the evaluation
loop. TurnFlow addresses this gap by integrating
controlled reactive users with challenge-specific
semantic evaluation criteria.
LLM-based User Simulator.
LLM-based user
simulators make interactive evaluation scalable.
They have been deployed in task environ-
ments (Yao et al., 2024; Barres et al., 2025;
Wang et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2025a), goal-
shift settings (Rana et al., 2025), and tutoring dia-
logues (Macina et al., 2023). Recent studies (Laban
et al., 2025; Dou et al., 2025; Sekuli´c et al., 2024;
Wang et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2026; Chen et al.,
2025b) document substantial variation in simulator
behavior across user-side models, personas, and
patterns of goal drift. In model-contingent dia-
logue, such variation directly determines whether
the evaluated model is prompted to seek clarifi-
cation or allowed to proceed from an erroneous
premise. TurnFlow therefore treats the user simu-
lator as an integral component of benchmark de-
sign, employing hidden state, aim-level control,
and policy-conditioned generation to make reactive
behavior transparent and auditable.
Reliable LLM-as-Judge Evaluation.
Open-
ended LLM judges (Zheng et al., 2023) can eval-
uate semantically complex responses but remain
sensitive to prompt formulation and stochastic
variation across runs, whereas verifiable bench-
marks (Zhou et al., 2023; Wen et al., 2024; He et al.,
2024) reduce ambiguity by decomposing evalu-
ation criteria into atomic conditions. Multi-turn
semantic evaluation requires both capabilities: rele-
vant evidence may be distributed throughout the di-
alogue, yet the final verdict must remain grounded

TurnFlow: Benchmark Design
Scenario specification is a core component of Turn-
Flow. As shown in Figure 2, it is produced by our
scenario constructor and subsequently consumed
by both the user simulator and the response ver-
ifier. Each specification consists of the essential
information needed to specify a multi-turn dialogue
interaction, including:
• information bank: the case state and persona that
define the underlying context and user character-
istics shaping the dialogue style;
• stage aims:
specifying the dialogue agenda
and enabling progressive information disclosure
across turns;
• task-completion criteria: providing the basis for
judging whether the final response successfully
satisfies the task.
This representation is more structured than a
scripted transcript. It enables the user simulator
to react dynamically to the evaluated model, while
the aim sequence controls when new facts and con-
straints are disclosed. It also supports the verifier
in auditing the final response. The detailed field
schema is provided in Appendix A.
In the following, we describe how we generate
diverse scenario specification (§3.1), design a re-
active user simulator that behaves according to a
given scenario specification (§3.2), and reliably as-
sess the correctness of responses produced by the
evaluated LLMs (§3.3).
3.1
Scenario Constructor
Scenario Domain. To ensure benchmark compre-
hensiveness, we aim to cover diverse dialogue do-
mains in everyday scenarios. We therefore summa-
rize seed scenarios from publicly available web di-
alogues and open-domain user–assistant conversa-
tions, including interactions captured by large pub-
lic corpora (Zheng et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2024),
and ultimately identify 9 domains. As shown in
Figure 4, these domains include travel and reloca-
tion, food and dining, home and household plan-
ning, healthcare, finance and purchases, digital and
technical support, education, events, and career or
business decisions.
Challenge Type. Orthogonal to dialogue domains,
we define 5 challenge types to capture representa-
tive complexities commonly observed in realistic
multi-turn interactions (He et al., 2024; Wu et al.,
2024; Laban et al., 2025; Rana et al., 2025).
• Stateful Multi-Constraint Following. We progres-
sively introduce distractors to test whether LLMs
can maintain correct state updates and associate
them with correct entities across multiple turns.
• Scope Creep. We intentionally expand a request
beyond specified constraints or available capacity
to test whether LLMs can correctly decline, con-
strain, or redirect the request.

• Constraint Collision. We introduce constraints

• Preference Override. We introduce both hard
rules and soft preferences to test whether LLMs
can correctly apply priority ordering when the two
conflict.
• Goal Shift. We introduce later requests that move
the conversation from an unresolved strategic de-
cision toward execution-oriented details, testing
whether LLMs revisit the original decision when
the accumulated scenario state makes continued
execution invalid.
By imposing the above challenges across diverse
dialogue domains, our benchmark can stress-test
the capabilities of existing LLMs in realistic multi-
turn dialogue settings. With these challenge types,
our benchmark can isolate interaction-level failures
that are difficult to expose in single-turn settings.
Construction Workflow. We then implement a
two-stage LLM-assisted construction pipeline to
generate the scenario specification. As shown in
Figure 2, the Scenario Constructor first filters open-
domain dialogue logs and assigns each selected
log to a challenge–domain coverage pair. Within
the Scenario Synthesizer, Task Extraction converts
the selected log and coverage pair into a structured
Scenario Template. TaskGen then instantiates a
candidate Scenario Specification, while TaskRe-
pair revises candidates that receive repairable feed-
back. Before admission, a construction-time LLM
and rule-based verifier checks four dimensions.
Structure verifies required fields, schema well-
formedness, and internal reference resolution. Task
Content checks semantic coherence, state and en-
tity grounding, and whether the required and forbid-
den criteria are derivable from the scenario state.
Difficulty ensures that the intended challenge is
non-trivial and genuinely requires multi-turn re-
quirement reconciliation. No-Leakage rejects sce-
narios whose anchor or early aims prematurely ex-
pose the final probe, expected decision, or scor-
ing target. Accepted candidates are serialized as
scenario.json and shared by the reactive user simu-
lator and the response verifier. Prompt templates,
field definitions, and lower-level validation rules
are provided in Appendix A. To ensure scenario
quality, we apply automatic checks for structure
completeness, target-entity grounding, difficulty,
and answer leakage before admitting a candidate. A
separate human audit of 100 scenario specification
accepts 90% for constraint/disclosure plausibility,
93% for final-challenge derivability, and 89% for
criterion validity; 87% satisfy all three dimensions
(Appendix G.3).
For the public release, we revised and replaced
a subset of the original scenarios while retaining
the total of 490 scenarios and the five challenge
categories. The main model comparison reported
in this revision uses the updated scenario set. The
original main results are retained in the appendix
for reference.
3.2
Reactive User Simulator
We design a reactive user simulator, called
BRIDGE, that consumes scenario specification and
automatically advances dialogues turn by turn with
the evaluated LLMs. BRIDGE features 3 modules:
• Perception(PERCEIVE/Replay): an LLM-based
state reader that, given the model’s last message,
the active aim, the recent dialogue context, and
relevant hidden constraints, extracts assistant ac-
tions, asked questions, satisfied and unsatisfied
criteria, information gaps, violations, and the ten-
sion level.
• Decision(DECIDE): a rule-based policy layer that
maps the perception result and the active aim
to a response type and payload through a fixed
priority over options including correction, infor-
mation filling, contradiction exposure, constraint
escalation, and topic completion.
• Generation(GENERATE): an LLM-based generator
that converts the selected policy, payload, per-


sona, and hidden-state guidance into a natural
dialogue.
The role-separated design of BRIDGE is criti-
cal for ensuring that the simulator adheres to the
scenario specification. In our empirical tests, a
free-form LLM simulator could drift away from
the scenario state or disclose facts at unintended
times, while a purely template-based script would
lose the adaptivity required for model evaluation.
BRIDGE therefore keeps the decision of what the
user should do inspectable, while allowing an LLM
to control how the specific turn is phrased. Its fixed
decision mapping is deliberately model-agnostic:
PERCEIVE maps an open-ended response to its se-
mantic consequence for the active aim, so different
surface-level failure styles receive the same user-
side action when they have the same state effect.
This preserves comparable user behavior across
tested models. The aim sequence in scenario speci-
fication can also specify the user simulator’s stance
toward the evaluated model at a given turn, such
as accepting a suggestion, resisting it, withhold-
ing pivotal information, or expressing uncertainty.
These controls allow the same structured payload
to be realized in a way that remains consistent with
the persona and current aim.
3.3
Response Verifier
Each scenario’s expected outcome is expressed
through atomic elements and scenario-specific cri-
teria defined before simulation, as shown in Fig-
ure 2. These elements fall into two categories:
• Required elements specify target facts, decisions,
or warnings used by the scenario’s scoring rules.
• Forbidden elements specify claims or actions that
LLMs must avoid.
Inspired by IFEval (Zhou et al., 2023), each ele-
ment is formulated as a single testable claim linked
to the scenario state. Scenario-specific rules de-
termine how these elements combine into a final
verdict, including conjunctions, thresholds, alterna-
tive groups, and veto conditions where applicable.
Here, globally invalid means violating these pre-
specified task rules under the accumulated conver-
sation state, not a universal preference for refusal.
Stateful, Collision, and Override scenarios gener-
ally require completing the task under the active
constraints; Scope Creep and Goal Shift require
pausing or redirecting only when the predefined
state makes the latest execution frame invalid. An
unnecessary refusal can itself miss required out-
comes.
The evaluator separates evidence extraction from
verification logic. An LLM-based verifier maps
the model’s final response to structured evidence
variables, and deterministic scenario-specific crite-
ria decide pass/fail by checking required elements,
forbidden elements, and veto conditions. This sep-
aration is important because open-form dialogue
answers rarely match a single reference string, but
a free-form verifier can collapse evidence match-
ing, criterion composition, and final scoring into an
unstable decision that depends on the its reasoning
path. Our method instead uses the LLM only to
identify and extract observable evidence, leaving
the scenario-specific criteria and verification rule
to explicit code. This preserves paraphrase toler-
ance while keeping the verification auditable and
reproducible.
4
Evaluation
We structure the experiments around our central
claim: current LLMs can remain locally helpful
while failing to reconcile the accumulated require-
ment state at the final decision point. We first iden-
tify this failure profile (§4.2), then test the BRIDGE
trajectory fidelity (§4.3), interaction-path depen-
dence (§4.4), and the effect of fixed or borrowed
trajectories (§4.5). Finally, we test robustness to
simulator and verifier substitution (§4.6). Appen-
dices J and L report lightweight interventions and
representative trajectories.

4.2
Multi-Turn Pass Rate
The evaluated models achieve overall pass rates
between 42.86% and 59.59% on TurnFlow (Ta-
ble 2). Kimi-K3 obtains the highest observed score,
passing 292 of 490 scenarios, followed by ANTH-
O-4.6 with 288. Even the highest-scoring model
does not pass 198 scenarios, indicating substantial
remaining room for improvement in reasoning over
user requirements across turns.
The dominant performance pattern is category-
specific. All nine models achieve between 87%
and 96% on Stateful Multi-Constraint Following,
whereas their mean pass rates on Scope Creep and
Goal Shift are 24.61% and 24.95%, respectively.
Constraint Collision and Preference Override oc-

4.4
Single-Turn Recovery Diagnostic
We next investigate whether these failures stem
from intrinsically unsolvable tasks or from compli-
cations introduced by the interaction trajectory. For
cases that consistently fail under the default multi-
turn setting, we consolidate all user-side facts, con-
straints, and the final query into a single prompt.
This setting serves as a diagnostic rather than a
deployment baseline: by eliminating intermediate
model commitments and turn-by-turn drift, it as-
sesses whether the consolidated requirement set re-
mains difficult independently of the conversational
path. As a calibration check, we apply the same col-
lapsing procedure to cases that consistently pass.
Figure 6 reports the diagnostic results for each
model, averaged across the 5 challenge categories.
A substantial proportion of multi-turn failures be-
come solvable when the same information is pre-
sented upfront, while stable-pass cases remain
largely unaffected. These findings suggest that
many failures arise from the multi-turn interaction
process rather than from intrinsically unsolvable
tasks. Scope Creep cases illustrate this mechanism
particularly clearly: models may initially recognize
capacity constraints but gradually accommodate es-
calating user demands and ultimately produce an
execution plan. When the same constraints are pre-
sented simultaneously, however, models are more
likely to detect the mismatch in scale and either
refuse the request or redirect the user toward a fea-
sible alternative. Multi-turn dialogue can induce a
path-dependent decision state in which intermedi-
ate commitments override active requirements.
Additionally, we reran a 200-scenario subset
using explicit state-tracking cues, visible sum-
maries of the accumulated state, and post-response
self-correction.
The gains are strongly model-
specific: explicit state tracking improves Qwen3.6-
Plus by 3.6%, state summaries improve OAI-5.5 by
7.3%, self-correction improves DeepSeek-V4-Pro
by 4.7%. Nevertheless, no intervention increases
the aggregate pass rate across the sampled subset
by more than 0.6%. Appendix J presents more
detailed results and corresponding failure analysis.

4.6
Sensitivity to the Backbone LLM
On a stratified subset of 100 scenarios, replac-
ing BRIDGE’s GDM-3-Flash backbone with ei-
ther OAI-5.4-mini or DeepSeek-V4-Flash alters
the pass rate of each evaluated model by at most
4%. Holding model responses and deterministic de-
cision rules fixed, substituting the LLM backbone
used for evidence extraction yields pairwise verdict
agreement rates of 92%–96%, while each extractor
agrees with human judgments on 88%–90% of the
audited cases. The complete results are provided
in Appendix I.
5
Conclusion
We introduce TurnFlow, an interactive multi-turn
dialogue benchmark designed to evaluate whether
LLMs preserve, revise, and act upon evolving
user states and requirements.
TurnFlow com-
bines scene-guided reactive user simulation with
scenario-specific evaluation criteria, allowing dia-
logues to unfold interactively while keeping their
outcomes auditable. Across nine evaluated LLMs,
overall pass rates range from 42.86% to 59.59%
on 490 scenarios. Models perform well on State-
ful Multi-Constraint Following but remain substan-


tially less reliable on Scope Creep and Goal Shift,
with different models exhibiting distinct strengths
across categories. These findings highlight the need
for conversational assistants to maintain and rec-
oncile accumulated user requirements when gen-
erating responses, particularly when later requests
encourage scope expansion or continued pursuit of
an outdated plan. We release the evaluation code
and dataset to support reproducible research on
multi-turn user-state reasoning.
Limitations
TurnFlow is designed as a controlled stress test
rather than an estimate of the prevalence of these
interaction patterns in everyday conversations. The
current scenario authoring relies on a single model
family. Although the 100-scenario validity audit
supports their plausibility, it does not validate ev-
ery benchmark item. BRIDGE relies on controlled
aims rather than a fully branching human policy,
and the 40-trajectory human audit cannot capture
the full spectrum of noisy, emotional, or incon-
sistent user behavior. The required and forbid-
den criteria are fixed and auditable; however, they
constitute authored conversational policies rather
than externally executable rules. Greater authoring-
model diversity, broader human evaluation, and
more comprehensive simulator-policy therefore re-
main important directions for future work.

A
Scenario Generation Pipeline
TurnFlow scenarios are stored as executable sce-
nario packages rather than as plain prompt strings.
Each package specifies what the simulated user
initially says, which facts and constraints are dis-
closed across turns, how the user moves through
the conversation, and which response elements are
required or forbidden at evaluation time. The gen-
eration code supports two compatible paths. A gen-
eral two-stage path is used for earlier task families
and composite tasks; the five TurnFlow challenge
categories in this paper use a stricter challenge-type
generation path because their difficulty comes from
staged disclosure, latent constraints, and semantic
drift rather than from a small set of additive diffi-
culty factors. Both paths produce the same final
schema and are therefore consumed by the same
simulator and judge. Scenario-authoring calls in
this pipeline use closed-source ANTH-O-4.5 unless
otherwise specified. The implementation records
finer-grained diagnostics such as schema validity,
reference resolution, criterion derivability, triviality,
and challenge-specific validity. These diagnostics
instantiate the four high-level dimensions shown in
Figure 2 Structure, Task Content, Difficulty, and
No-Leakage—rather than constituting additional
pipeline stages.
General two-stage path.
Stage 1 converts a task
type, difficulty level, and domain seed into a struc-
tured template. The template contains a factor
instantiation, a storyline with user profile, goal,
obstacles, and resolution, and a phase skeleton
that orders the scenario into conversational stages.
Stage 2 converts that template into a runnable
JSON scenario. In the full-LLM mode, this in-
cludes: (i) extracting an information schema, (ii)
generating an aim sequence with at most one user
turn per aim in our evaluation setting, (iii) con-
structing flow information, constraints, and dis-
tractors, (iv) writing the initial anchor utterance,
(v) enumerating and filtering ground-truth require-
ments, and (vi) assembling hidden state and base
assistant instructions. An optional prewriting pass
can naturalize user utterances, but the benchmark-
valid fields remain the structured state queue and
ground truth.
Challenge-type generation path for TurnFlow.
For the five challenge categories studied in the
main paper, generation uses a challenge-type au-
thoring path. The generator samples a domain from
a task-specific domain pool and pairs it with a legal
challenge mechanism, such as gradual scope creep,
preference override, multi-constraint elimination,
or goal replacement. A coherence check rejects
semantically implausible domain–mechanism pairs
before scenario authoring. The authoring prompt
then combines four sources: task-specific design
rules, a shared scenario-authoring guide, a hand-
written level-1 example for the same challenge type,
and task-specific hard rules. The model outputs the
complete scenario JSON in one call. If validation
returns retryable warnings, the warnings are in-
serted into a feedback section and the candidate
is regenerated, with at most two feedback-aware
retries; persistent warnings or hard errors discard
the candidate.

Validation rules.
The validator first checks struc-
tural well-formedness: required top-level fields
must exist, referenced information keys must re-

ground truth must be derivable from scenario-
internal information rather than from external

leakage, conditional or overly specialized ground-

connector-heavy surface forms. Finally, challenge-

for example, multi-constraint elimination must re-

final probe in the anchor. These checks are used


before stability filtering, so the published pool is
restricted both by generation-time validity and by
judge-stability selection.
Automatic validation layers.
Each generated
scenario is checked before admission to the
candidate pool.
Schema checks require the
runnable package to contain a well-formed
state_queue, an ordered aim_sequence, and non-
empty ground_truth.required_elements and
ground_truth.forbidden_elements. Reference
checks require every information key used by
an aim to resolve to flow_info or constraints.
Derivability checks require required and forbidden
elements to be supported by scenario-internal state
rather than by external lookup or author-only as-
sumptions. Leakage and triviality checks reject
cases where the anchor or early aims reveal the
final decision, where a required element merely
restates the final user request, or where a forbidden
element is too narrow to test a meaningful failure
mode.
Challenge-specific checks.
The validator also
applies category-level constraints that preserve
the intended measurement axis. Stateful Multi-
Constraint Following scenarios must contain entity-
linked updates whose polarity changes across turns.
Scope Creep scenarios must introduce a plausible
escalation path whose accumulated commitments
exceed a user constraint or capacity. Constraint
Collision scenarios must require the final answer to
satisfy more than one active constraint simultane-
ously. Preference Override scenarios must include
a hard rule that overrides a softer preference. Goal
Shift scenarios must contain an unresolved strategic
decision followed by execution-oriented requests
whose validity depends on resolving that decision
under the accumulated scenario state.
Repair and rejection policy.

Scoring and Judge Stability Methods
Scenario verdicts.
Each completed, scoreable
evaluation receives a binary PASS/FAIL verdict.
The verifier produces task-specific structured out-
puts, and deterministic reducers apply the scenario-
bound scoring rules. These rules may combine
required and forbidden evidence through thresh-
olds, alternative groups, and veto conditions.
Reported metrics.
The main paper reports ag-
gregate pass rate, computed as the mean binary sce-
nario verdict over the stability-filtered pool for each
model and challenge category. Auxiliary quantities
such as required-element satisfaction, forbidden-
element violation, and turn efficiency are useful
for debugging but are not used to derive the main
reported pass rates.
Verifier prompt interface.
Each released sce-
nario is associated with a packaged judge defini-
tion specifying the extraction targets, visible evi-
dence, output schema, and deterministic reduction
rules. The verifier receives the prescribed view
of the dialogue and final response and returns the
scenario-defined structured output. For Goal Shift,
this output includes a BLOCK/ALLOW action la-
bel and a verbatim reason span. The action label
indicates whether the response blocks or permits
continuation of the scenario-defined execution goal;
deterministic rules validate the output and compute
the final outcome.
Generic
evidence-extraction
prompt.
The
judgment-time extractor prompt shares the fol-
lowing skeleton across challenge types; bracketed
fields are filled from the scenario-specific fact key
and question template.
SYSTEM:

Output strict JSON only, no prose.

Extract evidence from the AGENT REPLY for
<CHALLENGE_TYPE>.

<TASK_SPECIFIC_DECISION_RULES>

Element-rewriting rules.
Every R and F ele-
ment is authored under three rules: (1) named-
entity anchor—the element references a concrete
entity from the scenario’s domain ontology, never
a free-form description; (2) single testable claim—
no AND/OR compound claims, no nested quan-
tifiers, no implicit-context references; (3) polar-
ity preservation—any rewriting of an element pre-
serves the verdict direction of the raw claim. Ele-
ments that fail any rule are returned to the authoring
loop before admission.

Why K=15.
We selected K=15 from a sweep
over K∈{5, 10, 15, 20, 30} on a held-out pilot
pool: K=15 is the smallest budget at which the
marginal additional flip-rate uncovered by raising
K falls below 1% for all five challenges. Larger
K buys diminishing additional stability filtering at
proportionally higher cost.
Why not majority voting.
Production scoring
uses a single judge call per case rather than K=15
majority aggregation. Majority voting at evaluation
time can hide verdict instability by aggregating it,
which both inflates apparent reliability and prevents
downstream consumers from auditing per-case ver-
dicts. We use K=15 only as a pre-publication
stability filter on the pool; production deployment
of the judge runs K=1.

Stateful Multi-Constraint Following: lifted dinner constraints
Scenario and test objective. An anniversary dinner starts with several guest restrictions, but two constraints later stop
being active. The test is whether the model updates the active constraint state rather than carrying forward every previously
mentioned restriction.
DeepSeek-V4-Pro trajectory (PASS).
User: “Sarah actually used to be lactose intolerant... she
has no more issues with dairy at all.”
Agent: “I’m updating Sarah’s status: no dietary restric-
tions.”
User: “Tom just called to say his flight got moved up to
Saturday morning, so unfortunately he won’t be able to
make it to dinner after all.”
Agent: “Tom’s shellfish allergy is no longer a concern...
here’s the refined guest list.”
Agent: “Sarah – no restrictions; Mike – celiac; Dana –
pregnant; Priya – strict Jain vegetarian; Raj – severe tree
nut allergy.”
Qwen-3-30B trajectory (FAIL).
User: “Sarah used to be lactose intolerant... she is com-
pletely fine now with no more dairy issues at all.”
Agent: “Sarah being officially cleared of lactose intoler-
ance is such a relief... Let’s update your dinner plan with
this happy news.”
User: “Tom... won’t be able to make it to dinner after
all... we won’t have to worry about his specific restrictions
anymore!”
Agent: “Tom can’t make it... we can focus on the 6 guests
who are coming.”
Agent: “Linguine alle Vongole...
Tom (shellfish al-
lergy)... Not safe for anyone except you & Sarah.”
Expected behavior. The final menu review should still flag active issues such as gluten for a celiac guest, pine nuts for a
tree-nut allergy, and meat or clams for a Jain guest; it should not flag dairy for Sarah or shellfish for Tom.
Interpretation. The failure is a constraint-lifecycle error. This case is manually audited as an illustrative example rather
than used as an automatic-judge claim.

Scope Creep: weekend booking page to gym-management platform
Scenario and test objective. A solo gym operator begins with a simple booking page, but later turns expand the request
into payments, subscriptions, trainer management, and analytics. The test is whether the model reassesses feasibility after
cumulative scope expansion.
Kimi-K2.5 trajectory (PASS).
User: “I only know basic HTML and a little JavaScript,
so I can’t build complex full-stack systems.”
User: “I need our memberships to have recurring subscrip-
tions with auto-renewal, retries for failed payments, and
proration...”
Agent: “What you’re describing is a full subscription
billing platform, not a simple project.”
User: “I would love to add an analytics dashboard that
displays member retention predictions, revenue forecast-
ing, and class popularity trends.”
Agent: “I need to stop us here, because I think there’s
a mismatch between what I’m saying and what you’re
hearing.”
OAI-5.5 trajectory (FAIL).
User: “Can we set up a waitlist feature that automatically
notifies clients when a spot opens up?”
Agent: “Yes – a waitlist with automatic notifications is a
very natural next step.”
User: “I would love to set up online payment process-
ing...”
Agent: “Yes – online payments are a reasonable next
step...”
Agent: “Yes – a basic version over one weekend is
realistic if you keep the scope very small.”
Expected behavior. Hold the line and redirect to existing software or a developer, because the request now requires backend
storage, billing, authentication, and analytics.
Interpretation. The failure is not missing a single fact; it is failure to convert accumulated scope into a refusal or redirection.

Constraint Collision: camera choice under a hard budget
Scenario and test objective. A camera recommendation must reconcile feature preferences with a hard budget. The test is
whether the model preserves jointly active constraints when attractive options violate one of them.
OAI-5.5 trajectory (PASS).
User: “The Sony A6400 is 1150 USD... the Fujifilm X-
T30 II is 899 USD... the Canon EOS R50 is 679 USD.”
Agent: “My pick: Fujifilm X-T30 II at $899.”
User: “My absolute maximum budget is 750 USD.”
Agent: “With your strict $750 cap... the A6400 is not a
realistic option.”
Agent: “Buy the Canon EOS R50... the only one that
fits your actual constraints.”
GDM-3.1-Pro trajectory (FAIL).
User: “The Canon EOS R50 is 679 USD... the Fujifilm
X-T30 II is 899 USD.”
Agent: “The Canon EOS R50 at $679... becomes a very
tempting option.”
User: “My friend... says the incredible Classic Chrome
simulation looks exactly like Kodachrome slides.”
Agent: “Classic Chrome is legendary... Not having to...
edit RAW files for hours is a massive selling point.”
Agent: “You should go with the Fujifilm X-T30 (buying
it used/refurbished).”
Expected behavior. Recommend the Canon EOS R50, the only option under the $750 cap; do not recommend the
over-budget Sony or Fujifilm or invent an undisclosed discount.
Interpretation. The failure resolves a hard collision by changing an input fact rather than respecting the active constraint
set.

Preference Override: spreadsheet habit versus live dashboard requirements
Scenario and test objective. The user has a strong spreadsheet habit, but one deliverable requires live interactivity. The
test is whether the model treats a preference as soft when a hard item-level requirement conflicts with it.
OAI-5.5 trajectory (PASS).
User: “For eight years, I have built every report and dash-
board strictly in Excel and Google Sheets.”
Agent: “If Excel and Google Sheets have worked for eight
years, there’s no reason to abandon them just because the
word “dashboard” is involved.”
User: “Deliverable 3 is a live-SQL dashboard with region
click-to-filter, row drill-downs, and a date-range slider...”
Agent: “This is the first one where I would not force it
into spreadsheets.”
Agent: “Deliverable 3. Live-SQL executive dashboard –
Power BI or Tableau.”
GDM-3.1-Pro trajectory (FAIL).
User: “I have used spreadsheets only for the past eight
years... I have never needed to use Tableau, Power BI, or
any other specialized tool.”
Agent: “You do not actually have to leave spreadsheets to
build an interactive dashboard.”
User: “Deliverable 3 is an interactive dashboard... It
requires a live SQL refresh for Monday’s live C-suite
leadership meeting.”
Agent: “Since we are sticking to Excel, I have fantastic
news: Excel is secretly one of the best SQL dashboard
tools on the market.”
Agent: “You are going to use Microsoft Excel for all
four deliverables.”
Expected behavior. Keep spreadsheets for the simple deliverables while recommending a BI tool for the interactive
dashboard.
Interpretation. The failure is preference bleed: a soft habit is treated as globally binding despite a hard requirement for
one deliverable.

Goal Shift: feasibility assessment to immediate pitching
Scenario and test objective. A new graduate first asks whether an eight-client, $2,800-per-month freelance social-media
plan is viable. After the model surfaces unresolved capacity and pricing concerns, the user pivots to immediate boutique
pitching. The test is whether the model reopens the invalidated launch premise rather than treating the later execution goal
as settled.
Initial goal omitted from the excerpts. The conversation opens with a feasibility question about reaching eight clients
within three months. The compressed turns below begin after that goal has been established.
Kimi-K2.5 trajectory (PASS).
User:
“My goal is 8 clients within 3 months
($2,800/month), then hiring a part-time VA to add 4 more
by month 6.”
Agent: “I need to flag some serious math and capacity
issues before you commit to this timeline publicly.”
User: “I’m basically ready to walk into the first boutique
this week, but is there anything else I should think about
before I go?”
Agent: “I want to pause you here. We just identified
that your plan has serious capacity and math problems...
That’s a red flag.”
OAI-5.5 trajectory (FAIL).
User: “My goal is 8 clients within 3 months to bring in
$2,800/month and cover my rent and car payment.”
Agent: “I love the clarity of the goal... But I want to
gently pressure-test the math and capacity.”
User: “I’m basically ready to walk into the first boutique
this week! Is there anything else I should think about
before I head out?”
Agent: “Yes: before you walk in, make sure you’re ready
for the actual moment where someone says, “Interesting,
tell me more.””
Expected behavior. Pause the user and require the capacity/pricing contradiction to be resolved before preparing the
boutique pitch.
Interpretation. OAI-5.5 does not fail for using an insufficiently forceful warning; it fails because it accepts the later
execution goal without revisiting the feasibility question that its own analysis left unresolved.

Goal Shift hard failure: self-publishing literary fiction
Scenario and test objective. A debut author of a 1970s Midwestern family saga opens with the strategic question of
whether self-publishing is right for the book. Later turns move into production details, pricing, marketing, and a plan to use
self-publishing sales to pitch traditional agents. The test is whether the model reopens the strategic premise once execution
details no longer answer the original decision problem.
Qwen3.6-Plus trajectory (PASS).
User: “I want to sell 500 copies across all formats in the
first 6 months, using that data to pitch literary agents for a
traditional deal on book two.”
Agent: “I need to give you some critical industry reality
checks regarding the connection between self-publishing
sales and securing a traditional deal for a second book.”
User: “I’ve got my timeline locked in... ready to send the
manuscript to the developmental editor next month.”
Agent: “This is a pivotal moment... you want to ensure
you get the maximum value out of it.”
OAI-5.5 trajectory (FAIL).
User: “I aim to sell 500 copies across all formats in the
first 6 months, using those sales to pitch literary agents for
a traditional deal on book two.”
Agent: “I’d gently reframe the second half of the strategy,
because agents are going to interpret self-publishing sales
in a very particular way.”
User: “I’m basically ready to send the manuscript off to
the developmental editor next month... Is there anything
else I should think about?”
Agent: “Yes. You’re at the perfect moment for one last
“publisher brain” pass before the manuscript leaves your
hands.”
Expected behavior. Pause execution support and tell the user to reconsider whether self-publishing serves the book and the
later agent-query goal before paying for production.
Interpretation. The failure is a global-reconciliation failure: accumulated execution detail is treated as confirmation of the
self-publishing plan rather than evidence that the original strategic question needs to be reconsidered.

Scope Creep hard failure: solo maker and boutique-chain partnership
Scenario and test objective. A one-person home candle business earning $800–1,000/month is offered a boutique-
chain partnership. Across the conversation, the deal accumulates wholesale volume, exclusive scent lines, insurance and
certification obligations, net-60 consignment terms, and weekend demo labor. The test is whether the model converts
cumulative obligations into a feasibility stop rather than a staged launch plan.
Kimi-K2.5 trajectory (PASS).
User: “I am a solo operator... no employees and $800–
1000/month revenue... I cannot scale to wholesale volume
or absorb the financial risk of consignment terms.”
Agent: “A “no” to their standard terms doesn’t have to
mean “no” to the opportunity.”
User: “I work on net-60 consignment... which is pretty
normal for retail partnerships!”
Agent: “Net-60 consignment is not “pretty normal” for
small makers, and it’s a dangerous structure for your situa-
tion.”
Agent: “I don’t think I can help you with that timeline...
you’ve consistently responded to every concern I raised
by escalating your obligations.”
GDM-3.1-Pro trajectory (FAIL).
User: “I’m worried I cannot scale to wholesale volume or
absorb the financial risk of consignment terms.”
Agent: “You do not have to say yes to whatever they
propose... negotiate terms that actually work for your
current setup.”
User: “They want to work on net-60 consignment... any
unsold inventory gets returned.”
Agent: “Stop right here... Do not accept these terms.”
Agent: “Since you are ready to get moving, let’s map out
a rock-solid, step-by-step timeline.”
Expected behavior. State that the current partnership is not feasible for a solo low-revenue operator, especially under
net-60 consignment, and recommend renegotiation or a much smaller pilot.
Interpretation. The failure is commitment-driven scope creep: each individual obligation can be discussed locally, but the
combined deal should block the requested timeline.
