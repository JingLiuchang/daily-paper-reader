AnalogLayoutWorld: An Open Benchmark
for Agentic Analog Layout Design
Nova-Silicon Team
xinxingyuan-sh@nova-silicon.com
Abstract
Coding agents have demonstrated strong capabilities in software development, yet
their ability to tackle long-horizon engineering tasks governed by physical con-
straints remains less understood. We study this question through analog circuit
layout, which demands domain knowledge, geometric reasoning, and iterative in-
teraction with design and verification tools. We introduce AnalogLayoutWorld,
an open-source agent benchmark with 40 expert-reviewed tasks for end-to-end
layout generation from fixed transistor-level netlists. It combines an integrated
design environment with independent physical and post-layout electrical verifi-
cation, as well as objective layout quality metrics co-designed with analog lay-
out experts. Across 13 model–harness configurations, 91.1% of attempts produce
GDS layouts, but only 21.3% satisfy the benchmark’s acceptance criteria. We also
evaluate Novara, an internally developed agent system, which achieves the highest
reported aggregate score. Our analysis links performance differences to test-time
scaling under fixed budgets, suggesting that reasoning efficiency and effective use
of verification feedback help sustain design quality as task complexity grows.1

Introduction
General coding agents increasingly tackle software and terminal tasks [Jimenez et al., 2024, Merrill
et al., 2026]. Recent foundation models advance coding, tool use, and scientific reasoning [OpenAI,
2026b, Anthropic, 2026, DeepSeek-AI, 2026b, Intern-S1 Team, 2025], motivating evaluation of
agent reliability in long-horizon engineering under physical constraints.
Analog layout requires domain knowledge and geometric reasoning to turn fixed netlists into de-
vice placements and interconnects through iterative verification (Figure 1). Even layouts that pass
physical checks and post-layout simulation require separate assessment of compactness, latch-up
susceptibility, and electromigration reliability [Johnson et al., 2015, Jerke and Kahng, 2014].
Recently, many LLM-based methods try to address analog circuit design [Hammoud et al., 2024, Li
et al., 2026a, Zhang et al., 2026, Liu et al., 2025]. However, limited task coverage and proprietary
dependencies in some systems motivate broader, objective evaluation of general-purpose agents.
We introduce AnalogLayoutWorld, an open benchmark of 40 expert-reviewed fixed-netlist layout
tasks for agentic analog layout design. Our environment provides preinstalled open-source EDA
software and concise documentation, without benchmark-specific typed tools or customized MCP
interfaces. Agents can freely determine their own design and verification workflows using the native
execution tools provided by their harnesses. Independent grading assesses physical and post-layout
electrical correctness, compactness, and engineering quality using objective metrics co-designed
with engineers.
Across 13 model–harness configurations, 91.1% of attempts produce GDS, but only 21.3% meet all
acceptance criteria. Our in-house developed agent system Novara ranks highest overall (Figure 2).
Correlation analysis reveals opposing patterns: circuit scale correlates strongly with GPT-series
resource use but less strongly with scores, whereas most analyzed open-source agents show the
reverse. Our findings suggest that most agents can still benefit from test-time scaling. Under fixed
budgets, however, realized gains depend on reasoning efficiency and the ability to effectively convert
environmental feedback into verified improvements.
Finally, we summarize our key contributions as follows:
• We introduce AnalogLayoutWorld, an open-source benchmark for agentic analog layout design
with 40 expert-reviewed tasks, which combines an end-to-end open-source EDA software stack
with independent physical, electrical, and layout-quality evaluation.
• We empirically characterize how iterative verification and test-time scaling relate to agent perfor-
mance, highlighting the importance of reasoning efficiency and the ability to effectively convert
environmental feedback into verified improvements within the available budget.

Related Work
2.1
Automated Analog Layout Design
Analog layout automation seeks to translate circuit netlists and design constraints into layouts that
meet physical and electrical requirements. MAGICAL [Xu et al., 2019] and ALIGN [Dhar et al.,
2021] automate device generation and constraint-driven placement and routing. LLM-based ap-
proaches extend this automation to language-to-layout generation with GLayout [Hammoud et al.,
2024] and interactive refinement with LayoutCopilot [Liu et al., 2025]. ICPI [Liu et al., 2026]
incorporates simulation feedback into refinement, while LAMP [Xu et al., 2026] targets adaptive
placement. Beyond individual layouts, Chu et al. [2026] study reusable generator development, and
PANDA [Zhang et al., 2026] and OPERA [Saxena and Mandal, 2026] integrate layout into broader
design flows. ViraStack [Cadence Design Systems, 2026] brings agent orchestration into commer-
cial EDA, while Yoshioka [2026] examines agent-driven design through post-layout verification.
Despite this progress, evaluations remain concentrated on selected circuit families and specialized
workflows, with proprietary dependencies in some systems, leaving the broader reliability and re-
producibility of autonomous layout design insufficiently established.
2.2
Agent and IC Design Benchmarks
Recent agentic benchmarks [Liu et al., 2024, Jimenez et al., 2024, Zhou et al., 2024, Xie et al., 2024,
Merrill et al., 2026, Marten, 2026] assess coding, tool use, and long-horizon task completion across
diverse domains and environments. Within IC design, digital benchmarks range from functionally
verified RTL generation [Liu et al., 2023] to workload-to-GDS design [Gunukula et al., 2026]. Ana-
log circuit datasets and benchmarks address transistor-level design: Analog Design Bench [Analog
Design Bench Team, 2026] evaluates netlist design and simulation-based specification closure, while
LOADBench [Leibl et al., 2026] provides circuit data and evaluation tasks for topology and device
sizing. TapeoutBench [Simra AI, 2026] reports mixed chip-design activities, including layout repair
and routing. Our work evaluates LLM agents on end-to-end analog layout generation through an
open EDA environment and independent verification, assessing their capabilities in long-horizon,
domain-specific problem solving and iterative optimization using environmental feedback.
3
AnalogLayoutWorld
3.1
Task Collection
We construct AnalogLayoutWorld, a suite of 40 analog layout tasks, drawing on circuits from Ana-
log Design Bench Team [2026] and Leibl et al. [2026], our own designs, and other open-source
projects. The tasks cover amplifiers, references, power-management circuits, sampling circuits, and
oscillators. Each task combines a fixed transistor-level SKY130 netlist with electrical specifications
and an executable validation procedure.
Analog circuit experts contribute to task selection and to the design and review of netlists, specifi-
cations, and validation procedures. All circuits pass pre-layout simulation, establishing a verified
electrical baseline for subsequent layout generation.
3.2
Task Format and Agent Environment
We provide a flexible EDA environment with preinstalled open-source software and concise inter-
face documentation. Agents write their own scripts and choose how to construct, verify, and revise
their layouts using tool feedback. Each task provides a fixed transistor-level SPICE netlist, electrical
specifications, implementation constraints, and public simulation testbenches. Task instructions and
documentation are available in both English and Chinese.
Built on Harbor [Harbor Contributors, n.d.], our benchmark uses a custom Docker [Docker, Inc.,
n.d.] image with the SKY130 PDK [SkyWater PDK Authors, n.d.a]. The toolchain includes gdsfac-
tory [gdsfactory community, n.d.] for layout generation, KLayout [KLayout Project, n.d.] for layout
editing and physical verification including DRC and LVS, Magic [Magic Development Team, n.d.]
for parasitic extraction (PEX), and ngspice [ngspice Developers, n.d.] for circuit simulation. Specif-
ically, design rule checking (DRC) checks geometric constraints such as minimum wire widths and

Each attempt has a three-hour wall-clock budget for layout generation, debugging, and self-checks.
Agents must save the current layout to submission/layout.gds, with the prescribed top cell and
ports, and retain generation code under submission/src/. Official grading begins after agent

Note that we provide no shared benchmark-specific typed tools or Model Context Protocol (MCP)
interfaces. Agents access the preinstalled software and usage documentation through the native
execution tools provided by their harnesses, deciding which checks to run and how to revise their
designs. The evaluation containers run in a network-isolated environment. Together, these settings
support open-ended exploration in an isolated sandbox, subject to the task constraints.

task container. Trusted references, testbenches, and grading code are introduced separately; agent-

simulation, together with geometric and engineering-quality measurements.

4.2
Overall Results
Table 1 shows substantial variation in layout-generation performance across the evaluated systems.
Novara achieves the highest aggregate score (0.8154), followed by GPT-6 Astra (0.7483). The GPT
configurations attain substantially higher physical and electrical verification rates than the other
general-purpose agents, while several configurations struggle to produce circuit-equivalent layouts.
For GPT agents, violations of geometric and morphological constraints, including aspect-ratio lim-
its, account for most of the gap between simulation pass rates and complete acceptance. Producing
a layout artifact alone therefore does not establish successful completion of the task.
Results in Table 1 also indicate that the relative strengths of agent systems depend on which as-
pect of layout quality is examined. GPT-6 Astra leads in DRC and all-case post-layout simulation,
whereas Novara leads in LVS and credited engineering quality. Figure 2 makes this distinction vis-
ible: the aggregate ranking reflects both verified circuit behavior and engineering quality, whose
relative strengths differ across agent systems. The ordering of general-purpose agents is broadly
consistent with expectations based on their general coding capabilities.

Analysis
Beyond aggregate scores, we analyze verification progress, task-scale sensitivity, and test-time re-
source use. By relating circuit scale to both final quality and resource expenditure, we seek a quan-
titative explanation for the performance gap between the evaluated open-weight configurations and
GPT-based agents from the perspective of test-time scaling. The central question is whether systems
can sustain productive iteration as task demands grow under a fixed time budget.
5.1
Progress through Verification Feedback
We first examine recovered DRC and LVS self-checks to track how verification progress accumu-
lates during agent interaction, as demonstrated in Figure 4.
Agents make progress through repeated verification.
Agents often require repeated verification
attempts to reach a passing self-check. Among trajectories with a recovered passing self-check,
90.3% of DRC first passes and 66.8% of LVS first passes occur after the first verification call.
The gains beyond the initial check show that continued interaction can produce additional verified
progress. Repeated verification is therefore a substantive part of trajectories with recovered passing
self-checks, supporting productive design–verification loops of iterative optimization.
Verification progress remains uneven.
The amount of verified progress achieved differs substan-
tially across configurations. Figure 4 indexes progress by both total tool calls and verification calls,
distinguishing overall interaction effort from repeated checking. At common budgets, the systems
reach different fractions of tasks with passing checks, and many trajectories still lack a recovered
pass within the displayed range. Thus, the opportunity to iterate does not yield the same progress for
every system, motivating a closer examination of task demands and resource use, which we discuss
in the following sections.

5.2
Task Scale and Difficulty
We next examine how the scale of the input circuit relates to final layout quality. Figure 5(a) com-
pares source-netlist device count with independently evaluated final scores.
Task scale reflects empirical difficulty.
Device count provides an agent-independent measure of
circuit scale: additional devices expand the placement and connectivity requirements that an agent
must coordinate. Averaging final scores across the six plotted configurations yields a negative task-
level correlation with device count (ρs = −0.73), indicating that larger circuits tend to be more
difficult for the evaluated systems. See Appendix D for the complementary net-count analysis,
which shows similar trends. Together, these results support circuit scale as a useful descriptor of
empirical task difficulty in AnalogLayoutWorld.
Scale sensitivity differs across systems.
Increasing circuit scale affects some systems much
more strongly than others. GLM-5.3 Flash, GLM-5.3, DeepSeek V4.1 Flash, and Kimi-K3 ex-
hibit stronger negative correlations (ρs = −0.82 to −0.44), whereas GPT-6.1 Sol and GPT-6 Astra
show weaker associations (−0.35 and −0.27). Because all configurations face the same task collec-
tion and scoring criteria, this contrast raises an important question: why do the GPT configurations
preserve quality better as circuit scale increases? We examine their use of tools, tokens, and time
next to understand how they respond to these growing demands.
5.3
Test-Time Scaling under Budget Constraints
To understand these differences in scale sensitivity, we relate circuit scale to both final scores and
resource use (Figure 5). The results indicate that GPT-series agents show stronger positive resource
correlations but weaker negative score correlations, whereas most open-weight configurations ex-
hibit the reverse. We examine whether differences in test-time scaling help explain this contrast.
GPT-series agents scale interaction with task demands.
GPT-6.1 Sol and GPT-6 Astra devote
more interaction to larger circuits. Device count correlates positively with their tool calls (ρs = 0.66
and 0.68) and elapsed time (0.63 and 0.67), alongside similarly positive token trends. Their recorded
workloads therefore expand with circuit scale, while final scores show comparatively smaller de-
clines. Finishing smaller tasks with time to spare leaves room for longer design–verification loops
as task demands grow. Combined with the verification progress in Section 5.1, this pattern is con-
sistent with productive test-time scaling through continued environment interaction: larger tasks
receive more effort, with smaller score declines than observed for the open-weight agents.

6.1
Residual Failures in Strong Agents
The current-starved ring VCO (Task 9) illustrates a physical-design limitation that persists despite
successful verification (Figure 6). All three GPT-6 Astra attempts pass DRC, LVS, and both pre-
scribed post-layout tests. Nevertheless, their aspect ratios range from 22.63 to 26.31, exceeding the
evaluation bound of 4. Each receives 0.1 for submission, LVS, and DRC, totaling 0.3; geometric
ineligibility withholds the area, engineering-quality, and electrical credits despite the passing sim-
ulations. In comparison, all Novara runs pass the same checks; its first attempt achieves an aspect
ratio of 1.24 and a score of 0.9015, demonstrating that electrical acceptance and a compact block
shape can be achieved together.
The recorded trajectories, including the model output in Figure 6, explain how this repeated out-
come arose. Each GPT-6 Astra attempt explores a folded resistor whose extracted length fails LVS
parameter matching, then adopts a straight resistor of 203.19 µm. This device dominates the block
width even after local placement improvements. In the second attempt, the agent also constructs and
verifies a clustered transistor placement before restoring the earlier strip implementation. Thus, the
failure is not an absence of verification or geometric exploration: the agent makes local repairs but
does not find an implementation that jointly achieves electrical closure and a suitable global shape.

Conclusion
We presented AnalogLayoutWorld, an open-source benchmark for agentic analog layout design with
40 expert-reviewed tasks, an end-to-end open-source EDA software stack, and independent physical,
electrical, and layout-quality evaluation. Agents freely determine their design and verification work-
flows, while assessment is grounded in submitted layouts. Our experiments reveal a substantial gap
between artifact generation and verified completion. Analyses of verification progress, task scale,
and resource use suggest that most agents can benefit from test-time scaling, but gains under finite
budgets depend on reasoning efficiency and effective use of environmental feedback. We discuss
the scope and limitations of our work in Appendix B. Future work will broaden circuit and process
coverage and refine layout-quality assessment through expert review and community feedback.

Limitations and Future Work
Task and process coverage.
The current release covers 40 fixed-netlist tasks using the SKY130
PDK. It evaluates physical implementation with predefined devices and specifications; topology
selection and transistor sizing are outside the task scope. Results characterize performance within
the included circuit families and scale range. Extending the suite to additional process technologies
and larger hierarchical circuits is an important direction for future work.
Verification boundary.
Acceptance is defined by the design rule decks, device models, extraction
settings, and electrical test cases. These checks provide reproducible rule-based evidence under the
stated conditions, but do not establish foundry sign-off or guarantee silicon performance. The study
includes no silicon measurements, and performance under untested conditions remains unverified.
Evaluation of layout quality.
Passing DRC, LVS, and the prescribed post-layout simulations is
necessary for benchmark acceptance, but is far from sufficient to characterize practical layout qual-
ity. We introduce geometric and engineering-quality metrics to narrow this gap. These metrics were
designed in consultation with analog layout engineers. As an initial approximation, these metrics
may have incomplete coverage or assign low scores to otherwise sound layouts. We will refine these
metrics through expert review and welcome community feedback and suggestions.

Additional Case Studies
These examples separate compactness and circuit correctness using individual final submissions

E.1
Compactness at Comparable Device Content
On the local two-stage OTA (Task 40), GPT-6.1 Sol and DeepSeek V4.1 Flash both pass the required
physical and electrical checks (Figure 9). Their effective device areas are 910.01 and 912.04 µm2,
differing by only 0.22%. Both earn 0.7 from submission, physical, and electrical verification, and
their engineering-quality contributions are similar, at 0.0617 and 0.0605. Yet the bounding areas
are 809.75 and 3575.16 µm2. The area-score contributions are consequently 0.2000 and 0.0108,
accounting for 99.4% of the total-score difference (0.9617 versus 0.7712). With nearly unchanged
effective device content, this pair makes the score’s preference for compact physical organization
directly inspectable.

E.2
Circuit Correctness beyond Visual Appearance
Compact appearance and clean DRC do not establish circuit equivalence. Figure 10 compares two
submissions for the local comparator (Task 38). Both pass DRC and form compact blocks, yet
the DeepSeek V4.1 Flash layout fails LVS after successful extraction. The extracted netlist reveals
incorrect gate connections in the cross-coupled NMOS pair: each gate is connected to its local latch
node rather than the opposite node required by the reference. All twelve named top-level ports
match, so this is an internal connectivity error rather than a port-naming mismatch.
GPT-6.1 Sol passes LVS and all prescribed post-layout tests, scoring 0.8231: 0.3 for submission and
physical verification, 0.0333 for area, 0.0898 for engineering quality, and 0.4 for electrical verifica-
tion. DeepSeek V4.1 Flash receives only the 0.1 submission credit. Its failed LVS withholds DRC
credit and the remaining components; parasitic extraction and post-layout tests are consequently
skipped, rather than recorded as failed tests. The contrast demonstrates why compact geometry
must be checked against the intended circuit before receiving layout-quality credit.
