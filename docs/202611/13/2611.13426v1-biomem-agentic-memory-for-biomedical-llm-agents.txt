Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated considerable potential in biomedical applications,
while agentic memory systems have emerged as a promising approach to preserving and utilizing
information across tasks and interactions. However, a fundamental question remains: how effectively
and reliably do these systems retain, retrieve, update, and utilize biomedical information? In this study,
we systematically investigate agentic memory in the biomedical domain, focusing on task effectiveness,
retrieval quality, information fidelity, and long-term robustness. We evaluate five memory approaches
across four LLMs in three representative settings: biomedical knowledge reasoning, clinical prediction,
and long-term conversational memory. Our analyses reveal how memory construction, evidence
relevance, and conversational history shape downstream biomedical performance, highlighting the
distinct challenges of retrieving relevant information, preserving its fidelity, and effectively utilizing it
for reasoning. Building on these findings, we introduce Hybrid Memory Retrieval and Utilization
(HMRU), a training-free framework that jointly enhances evidence accessibility and task-aware
utilization to improve biomedical reasoning and long-term conversational memory. HMRU achieves
an average relative improvement of 8.6% across biomedical question-answering and conversational
memory benchmarks, with additional gains in selected clinical prediction tasks. It further reduces
conversational answer errors by 24.9% and closes 48.0% of the performance gap to full-context
inference. Our findings provide a systematic characterization of biomedical agentic memory and
practical insights into developing more effective and reliable memory-augmented LLM systems.
1 Introduction
Large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated considerable potential in biomedical applications, including
clinical question answering, medical information extraction, and decision support [1, 2, 3]. Advances in
medical-domain pretraining and instruction tuning have further strengthened their ability to interpret
biomedical terminology and respond to specialized clinical questions [4, 5, 6]. However, biomedical
applications often depend on information that is patient-specific, evolving, or distributed across multiple
sources, which cannot be fully captured by knowledge encoded in model parameters alone. These requirements
motivate approaches that provide language models with access to external information beyond their parametric
knowledge. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) addresses this need by incorporating retrieved evidence
at inference time without updating the underlying model parameters [7].
Beyond accessing external evidence, biomedical agents must also manage information accumulated through
repeated interactions. Patient histories, evolving observations, and previous exchanges may remain relevant to
subsequent tasks, requiring information to be preserved and updated over time. Persistent memory systems

have emerged to address this need by organizing and reusing information across interactions [8, 9]. Recent
approaches have further introduced specialized mechanisms for memory construction, retrieval, and updating.
A-MEM [10],for example, constructs interconnected memory notes and updates contextual representations as
new information is incorporated . Mem0 [9] extracts and consolidates salient conversational information for
persistent, query-dependent retrieval, while LightMem [11] separates lightweight online processing from
longer-term consolidation to improve memory efficiency. These approaches illustrate different strategies for
storing, structuring, and updating information that may be useful across tasks and interactions. Meanwhile,
benchmarks such as RealMem [12] have begun to examine memory capabilities involving temporal reasoning,
information updates, and interactions spanning multiple sessions. However, the effectiveness of external
memory systems depends on several interconnected processes. Information may be transformed during
storage, selected or omitted during retrieval, revised through subsequent updates, and interpreted by the
language model at inference time. This raises a fundamental question: how effectively and reliably do agentic
memory systems preserve, retrieve, update, and utilize biomedical information?
In this study, we systematically investigate agentic memory in biomedical domain, examining four dimensions:
task effectiveness, retrieval and utilization quality, information fidelity, and long-term robustness. We
evaluate five memory strategies with four language models across three representative settings: biomedical
knowledge reasoning, clinical prediction, and long-term conversational memory. The evaluation covers six
biomedical tasks, including reasoning over medical textual knowledge graphs , mortality and readmission
prediction, cancer classification using, and opioid overdose prediction. We additionally evaluate long-term
conversational memory tasks, examining incremental information, dynamic updating, proactive alignment,
temporal reasoning, and cross-session interactions. Beyond overall task performance, we study the effects of
memory construction data volume, retrieval depth, and class distribution. We also trace retrieved evidence to
original records, assess its relevance to the target question, analyze source-grounded fidelity during iterative
memory evolution, and evaluate robustness to conversation length and evidence distance.
Our analyses reveal four key findings. First, successful biomedical knowledge reasoning depends not only
on retrieving relevant evidence but also on its appropriate utilization, as answer-bearing memories do not
consistently lead to correct predictions. Second, semantic similarity between patient records does not
necessarily correspond to similarity in future clinical outcomes, highlighting task-dependent limitations
of memory retrieval. Third, iterative memory evolution is associated with the emergence and persistence
of source-unsupported information, raising concerns about the fidelity of stored representations. Finally,
long-term conversational memory performance depends on more than history length alone, with evidence
accessibility and integration emerging as important factors. Together, these findings identify evidence retrieval,
information fidelity, and memory utilization as distinct but interconnected challenges in biomedical agentic
memory. Motivated by these observations, we introduce Hybrid Memory Retrieval and Utilization (HMRU),
a training-free framework designed to improve access to relevant evidence and its effective utilization in
downstream biomedical tasks. HMRU consistently improves performance on biomedical knowledge reasoning
benchmarks and enhances long-term conversational memory by reducing answering errors and narrowing the
performance gap with full-context inference. Our main contributions are summarized as follows:
1. We systematically evaluate five memory methods using four LLMs across six biomedical tasks and
long-term conversational memory settings, examining the effects of memory construction, retrieval
depth, and class distribution on downstream performance.
2. We investigate memory retrieval, evidence utilization, and information fidelity, identifying key
limitations in biomedical reasoning and memory evolution. We further examine the effects of evidence
completeness, conversation length, and evidence distance on long-term memory performance.
3. We propose Hybrid Memory Retrieval and Utilization (HMRU), a training-free framework that enhances
evidence retrieval and task-aware utilization, improving biomedical knowledge reasoning and long-term
conversational memory performance.

2.1 Memory Performance and Analysis on Biomedical Tasks
Table 1 presents the performance of five memory methods across six biomedical benchmarks using four
language models. The evaluation covers two biomedical knowledge reasoning tasks (RiTeK-PharmKG
and RiTeK-ADint) and four clinical prediction and classification tasks (MIMIC-III Mortality, MIMIC-III
Readmission, TCGA-Reports, and MIMIC-IV OOD). For knowledge reasoning tasks, we report precision,
recall, and F1 scores using exact-match and ROUGE-1 measures. Clinical prediction tasks are evaluated using
AUPRC, AUROC. We compare five memory management methods (A-MEM, MemZero, and LightMem,
NaiveRAG and FullContext) to systematically examine how different memory mechanisms affect downstream
biomedical task performance across model architectures and task settings. All results are reported as
percentages.

backbones. With Qwen3-30B, NaiveRAG outperformed A-MEM on both datasets, achieving F1 scores

2.1.2
Factors Influencing Memory Performance
To better understand the factors influencing memory performance in biomedical tasks, we systematically
investigate three key aspects: training data volume, retrieval depth, and class distribution. First, we examine
how varying the number of training samples used for memory construction affects downstream performance.
Second, we evaluate the impact of retrieval depth by varying the number of memory items retrieved during
inference. Third, we investigate how different positive-to-negative class distributions in memory construction
influence clinical prediction performance. Across these experiments, we aim to characterize the sensitivity
4


of different memory methods to data availability, retrieval configurations, and class imbalance, providing
insights into their effectiveness and robustness across biomedical tasks.
Effect of training data volume.
Figure 1(a) illustrates the effects of memory construction data volume
across six biomedical tasks, using 250, 500, and 1,000 samples. Increasing data volume generally improves
performance on biomedical knowledge reasoning tasks, whereas clinical prediction and pathology classification
exhibit more heterogeneous patterns. On RiTeK-PharmKG, A-MEM with HuatuoGPT-3-27B achieves exact-
match F1 scores of 27.66, 33.23, and 39.27 as data volume increases, while corresponding scores on
RiTeK-ADint rise from 37.83 to 43.08 and 52.94. Similar improvements are observed with Qwen3-30B,
where A-MEM increases from 23.17 to 33.00 on RiTeK-PharmKG. In contrast, performance on clinical
prediction tasks does not consistently improve with additional data. On MIMIC-IV OOD, NaiveRAG with
HuatuoGPT-3-27B achieves AUPRC scores of 7.70, 5.22, and 15.61, whereas LightMem decreases from
5.21 to 4.28 and 3.42. Performance on TCGA-Reports remains comparatively stable across data volumes,
particularly for configurations with already high AUPRC values.
Effect of retrieval depth.
Figure 1(b) illustrates the effects of retrieval depth across six biomedical tasks,
with the number of retrieved memory items varying from 1 to 10. Increasing retrieval depth generally
improves performance on biomedical knowledge reasoning tasks, whereas clinical prediction and pathology
classification exhibit more heterogeneous patterns. On RiTeK-PharmKG, A-MEM with Qwen3-30B achieves
exact-match F1 scores of 27.33, 30.32, 31.81, and 33.00 at Top-1, Top-3, Top-5, and Top-10, respectively.
Similar improvements are observed on RiTeK-ADint, where exact-match F1 increases from 36.71 to 46.20. In
contrast, deeper retrieval does not consistently improve clinical prediction. On MIMIC-IV OOD, NaiveRAG
with HuatuoGPT-3-27B achieves an AUPRC of 3.99 at Top-1 and 15.61 at Top-10, while other configurations
show smaller gains or fluctuating performance. On MIMIC-III Readmission, LightMem reaches its highest
AUPRC of 61.32 at Top-5 before declining to 59.17 at Top-10. Performance on TCGA-Reports remains
relatively stable at higher retrieval depths, particularly for configurations with already high AUPRC values.
Effect of class distribution.
Figure 1 (c) illustrates the effects of varying positive-to-negative class ratios in
memory across two clinical prediction tasks. Changes in class distribution produce heterogeneous effects on
predictive performance, with no single ratio consistently outperforming the natural distribution across memory
methods and language models. On MIMIC-III Readmission, A-MEM with Qwen3-30B achieves an AUPRC
of 57.92 under the 25:75 ratio, compared with 55.01 under the natural distribution. Similarly, LightMem with
Qwen3-30B improves mortality AUPRC from 8.88 to 10.77 under the 10:90 ratio. However, increasing the
proportion of positive samples does not consistently improve performance. With HuatuoGPT-3-27B, A-MEM
achieves its highest readmission AUPRC of 61.91 under the natural distribution, declining to 60.37 under
the 50:50 ratio. NaiveRAG also exhibits substantial performance reductions across several configurations,
particularly for readmission prediction.

For clinical prediction and classification, we assessed whether retrieved examples were enriched for the
ground-truth class relative to its prevalence in the training data.

Relationship between retrieved evidence and answer correctness.
Figure 2a illustrates the relationship
between retrieved evidence categories and answer correctness across the two RiTeK benchmarks using
NaiveRAG. Retrieved evidence is categorized as containing the same fact, related information, unrelated
information with a matching answer, or no answer-matching source. Among the 1,505 questions, 363 contain
a same-fact source, 175 a related source, and 46 an unrelated source with a matching answer, accounting
for 24%, 12%, and 3% of the sample, respectively. The remaining 921 questions, comprising 61% of the
sample, lack an answer-matching source. Overall, NaiveRAG correctly answers 614 questions and incorrectly
answers 891, yielding an accuracy of 41%. Correct answers are more strongly associated with factually
relevant evidence, although errors persist even when answer-bearing sources are retrieved. Conversely, some
questions are answered correctly without an identifiable answer-matching source. These findings highlight the
relationship between evidence availability, factual relevance, and answering performance, while indicating
that retrieving relevant evidence alone does not guarantee correct utilization.

2.1.4
Memory evolution and fidelity.
We further investigated information fidelity during A-MEM construction and evolution across six biomedical
tasks (Fig. 3). Using an LLM-based evaluator, we assessed whether generated memory contexts contained
information unsupported by their original training records. During sequential memory construction, the
proportion of memories containing unsupported context increased in several tasks, reaching 44% in MIMIC-IV
OOD and 35% in RiTeK-PharmKG, while remaining below 2% in both MIMIC-III tasks (Fig. 3a). Higher
rewrite frequency was associated with a greater prevalence of unsupported information, particularly in
MIMIC-IV OOD, where the proportion increased from approximately 10% among memories without rewrites
to 80% among those rewritten at least five times (Fig. 3b). Comparisons between initial memory contexts
and their final states after evolution revealed substantial task-dependent differences (Fig. 3c). In MIMIC-IV
OOD, the proportion of memories containing unsupported context increased from 12% to 44%, representing
a 32-percentage-point increase. RiTeK-PharmKG showed a similar pattern, increasing from 16% to 35%. In
contrast, smaller changes were observed in RiTeK-ADInt and TCGA-Reports, while both MIMIC-III tasks
remained close to zero throughout memory evolution. Longitudinal inspection of the ten most frequently
rewritten memories in MIMIC-IV OOD further revealed extended sequences of unsupported context, although
some memories alternated between supported and unsupported states across successive revisions (Fig. 3d).

2.2.2
Effects of Conversation Length and Memory Distance on Memory Performance
We first investigated the sensitivity of long-term conversational memory to increasing history length using
100 questions from the RealMem medical subset (Fig. 4a). Three experimental conditions were constructed
by selectively removing irrelevant sessions preceding the earliest evidence-bearing session while preserving
all required evidence and subsequent conversational content. This yielded Short, Medium, and Long
histories with median lengths of 23,000, 36,000, and 48,000 tokens, respectively. Memory was independently
reconstructed for each condition to evaluate the effects of history length on both retrieval and response
generation. Contrary to the expectation that longer histories would impair memory performance, increasing
conversation length produced no consistent degradation across either memory system. With Qwen3-30B-A3B-
Instruct-2507, A-MEM exhibited a modest increase in QA performance from 78.3 to 81.7 and 83.0 across the
three conditions, accompanied by improvements in memory recall from 85.2 to 87.4 and 89.1. In contrast,
LightMem maintained relatively stable QA scores of 74.0, 74.7, and 73.0, while memory recall remained
substantially lower, ranging from 53.6 to 55.2. These observations suggest that, within the evaluated context
lengths, additional irrelevant conversational history preceding the evidence does not necessarily compromise
memory retrieval or downstream answering. The contrasting recall levels further indicate that differences in
memory construction and retrieval mechanisms may be more influential than history length alone.

76.7% at the greatest distance. Memory recall showed a similar decline, whereas LightMem exhibited
no consistent distance-dependent trend. Log-distance regression indicated modest negative associations
with QA performance for both methods. We further examined the relationship between evidence retrieval
completeness and answering performance (Fig. 4c). Across 600 question-condition observations, A-MEM

2.2.4
Association Between Conversation History Length and Memory Performance
We examined the relationship between conversation history length and question-answering performance
across all RealMem-Med test questions (Fig. 5c). Questions were divided into five approximately equal-sized
groups according to the number of historical conversation sessions, ranging from 2 to 238 sessions. A-MEM
showed a decline in perfect-answer accuracy from approximately 95% in the shortest-history group to 74% in
the longest-history group. MemZero exhibited a similar downward trend, whereas LightMem and NaiveRAG
maintained lower accuracy with more variable patterns. In contrast, FullContext consistently achieved high
accuracy across history-length groups. Regression analysis further identified significant negative associations
between history length and answering accuracy for A-MEM (adjusted odds ratio [OR] = 0.42, 𝑝< 0.05)
and MemZero (OR = 0.68, 𝑝< 0.05). No statistically significant associations were observed for LightMem,
NaiveRAG, or FullContext. These results suggest that longer conversation histories are associated with reduced
answering performance for certain memory systems. However, because the analysis compares different
questions with naturally varying history lengths, the observed relationships may also reflect differences in
question complexity and information requirements rather than a direct effect of history length.
2.2.5
Evaluating Retrieval and Answering in Long-Term Conversational Memory
We further evaluated the effectiveness of long-term conversational memory using 325 questions from the
medical subset of RealMem. We compared four memory-based approaches, A-MEM, MemZero, LightMem,
12


and NaiveRAG, against FullContext, which provides the complete conversation history to the language
model. Beyond overall answer accuracy, we examined three complementary aspects of memory performance:
question-level differences relative to FullContext, the relationship between evidence retrieval completeness
and answer correctness, and the association between conversation history length and answering performance
(Fig. 5).
Performance gap relative to full conversation history.
FullContext consistently outperformed all four
memory-based approaches (Fig. 5a).
In paired question-level comparisons, FullContext answered 55
questions correctly that A-MEM failed to answer, whereas A-MEM uniquely answered 22 questions correctly,
corresponding to a 10-percentage-point difference in overall perfect-answer rate. Larger gaps were observed
for MemZero, LightMem, and NaiveRAG, with differences of 32, 37, and 39 percentage points, respectively.
All four paired comparisons were significant under exact McNemar tests, with 𝑃< 0.001. These results
indicate that existing memory-based approaches do not fully preserve the answering performance achievable
when the complete conversation history remains accessible.
Evidence retrieval completeness and answer correctness.
To examine the relationship between memory
retrieval and downstream answering, we stratified questions according to whether none, some, or all gold-
standard memories were retrieved (Fig. 5b). Across memory methods, perfect-answer rates generally increased
with retrieval completeness, although substantial differences remained in how effectively retrieved evidence
was utilized. A-MEM showed a pronounced increase in accuracy, from 5% when no gold-standard memories
were retrieved to 57% with partial retrieval and 89% with complete retrieval. Similar patterns were observed
for MemZero and LightMem, which achieved perfect-answer rates of 78% and 76%, respectively, when all
required memories were retrieved. In contrast, NaiveRAG achieved only 70% despite complete retrieval,
highlighting limitations in downstream evidence utilization. FullContext achieved higher accuracy, reaching
94% when all required evidence was available. Notably, MemZero and LightMem correctly answered 33%
and 27% of questions, respectively, even when no gold-standard memories were retrieved. Together, these
findings demonstrate that retrieval completeness is strongly associated with answering performance, but
access to relevant evidence alone does not guarantee correct utilization. Because the retrieval categories
contain different questions, these associations do not establish a causal effect of retrieval completeness on
answer accuracy.
Association between history length and memory performance.
We additionally examined performance
across five groups defined by the number of historical conversation sessions, ranging from 2 to 238 sessions
(Fig. 5c). A-MEM achieved a perfect-answer rate of approximately 95 per cent among questions with the
shortest histories, compared with 67–74 per cent among questions with longer histories. MemZero, LightMem,
and NaiveRAG exhibited similar descriptive declines, whereas FullContext maintained comparatively stable
performance across history-length groups. After adjusting for question category, evidence count, and session
type, history length remained negatively associated with perfect-answer outcomes for A-MEM and MemZero,
with adjusted odds ratios of 0.42 and 0.68, respectively. No statistically significant associations were observed
for LightMem, NaiveRAG, or FullContext.

These results suggest that task-aware memory utilization provides complementary benefits beyond hybrid

A-MEM baseline retained the highest AUPRC of 4.94. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that hybrid
retrieval and task-aware memory utilization improve biomedical question-answering performance, whereas
their effectiveness varies across clinical prediction tasks.

Improving Long-Term Conversational Memory through Hybrid Retrieval

3 Discussion
Biomedical memory performance depends on task characteristics
This study examined how agentic
memory systems construct, retrieve, update and use information across biomedical knowledge reasoning,
clinical prediction and long-term conversational tasks. Across six biomedical benchmarks, four language
models and five memory approaches, no method consistently outperformed the others. The analyses instead
identified three recurring limitations: relevant evidence may not be retrieved, retrieved evidence may be
used incorrectly, and evolving memory representations may diverge from their source records. We further
investigated these issues in long-term conversation and developed Hybrid Memory Retrieval and Utilization
(HMRU), a training-free framework that improves retrieval and evidence use without updating language-model
parameters. These findings suggest that memory quality cannot be characterized by storage capacity or
retrieval volume alone. The relative advantages of memory methods changed with both the task and the
language-model backbone (Table 1). With HuatuoGPT-3-27B, A-MEM achieved exact-match F1 scores
of 39.27 on RiTeK-PharmKG and 52.94 on RiTeK-ADInt, compared with 39.04 and 51.63 for NaiveRAG.
With Qwen3-30B, however, NaiveRAG outperformed A-MEM on both tasks. The differences were more
substantial in clinical prediction: with HuatuoGPT-3-27B, NaiveRAG achieved an opioid overdose AUPRC
of 15.61, compared with 4.94 for A-MEM, whereas MemZero performed best on mortality prediction with
Meditron3-Qwen2.5-14B. Thus, more elaborate memory organization does not necessarily produce better
downstream results. Increasing the memory construction set from 250 to 1,000 records improved A-MEM F1
on PharmKG from 27.66 to 39.27, but larger memory collections, deeper retrieval and class rebalancing did
not uniformly benefit clinical prediction. These task differences may reflect distinct relationships between
semantic similarity and the information needed for prediction. In TCGA-Reports, retrieved examples were
strongly enriched for the same cancer category, with retrieval lift exceeding 15 (Fig. 2f). By contrast,
label enrichment was weaker and less consistent for mortality and readmission. Two patients with similar
diagnoses or clinical histories may nevertheless experience different future outcomes. Consequently, retrieval
objectives that work well for identifying explicit biomedical facts or diagnostic categories may be insufficient
for prospective risk prediction, which requires attention to longitudinal patterns and clinically meaningful
differences between patients. Outcome prediction, however, requires more than identifying a superficially
similar patient. The relevance of historical diagnoses depends on their timing, combinations, severity and
relation to the target event. A memory store assembled from examples with matching labels could also
overrepresent prevalent outcomes without providing meaningful discrimination. This is particularly important
when positive cases are uncommon, as in opioid overdose prediction, where AUPRC and retrieval enrichment
should be interpreted relative to event prevalence. Our findings therefore argue against using a single retrieval
objective across biomedical applications. A useful next step would compare semantically nearest neighbors
with temporally aligned, clinically matched and outcome-aware candidates under the same retrieval budget,
while preserving a separate held-out evaluation cohort.
Evidence availability and evidence utilization are distinct limitations
Our evidence-level analyses help
explain why retrieving additional memories does not always improve question answering. Across the RiTeK
datasets, the presence of an answer-matching source was associated with substantially greater accuracy:
retrieval-based methods generally answered 80–88% of questions correctly when such a source was available,
compared with 12–16% when it was absent (Fig. 2c). Yet an answer-matching record did not necessarily
support the same biomedical fact. On RiTeK-ADInt, A-MEM, LightMem and NaiveRAG each retrieved
same-fact sources for approximately 35% of questions, without a consistent coverage advantage for the more
structured methods (Fig. 2b). Accuracy also increased with the semantic similarity between the question and
its answer-matching source, approaching 100% in the highest similarity bin (Fig. 2e). These observations
underscore the difference between recovering an answer entity and retrieving evidence that addresses the
correct biomedical relation. Evidence utilization introduced a separate challenge. Incorrect predictions
17


exactly matched answers contained in retrieved records for 16–21% of RiTeK-ADInt questions and 24–29%
of RiTeK-PharmKG questions across the retrieval-based approaches (Fig. 2d). Such overlap is consistent
with interference from competing answers, although it does not prove that the model copied a specific
memory. Similarly, even complete retrieval of annotated evidence did not always produce correct answers
in the long-term conversational evaluation. Thus, memory systems must both recover appropriate evidence
and distinguish among potentially conflicting sources during generation. These analyses are observational:
questions with retrievable evidence may be inherently easier, and causal effects require interventions that
remove, reorder or replace retrieved memories. Nevertheless, they support evaluating factual coverage and
downstream evidence use as separate properties of a memory system.
Iterative memory evolution does not guarantee source fidelity
A further concern is whether generated
memory representations faithfully preserve their original information. Agentic memory mechanisms may
summarize incoming records and rewrite existing notes as new information arrives. Although these operations
can improve organization, they can also introduce unsupported contextual statements. In our A-MEM
analysis, the proportion of memories with context not supported by their original training records increased
from 12% to 44% on MIMIC-IV opioid overdose prediction and from 16% to 35% on RiTeK-PharmKG
(Fig. 3c). In contrast, unsupported-context prevalence remained below 2% in both MIMIC-III tasks, indicating
substantial task dependence. Repeated rewriting was associated with the same pattern. In MIMIC-IV OOD,
approximately 10% of memories without rewrites contained unsupported context, compared with 80% among
those rewritten at least five times (Fig. 3b). Longitudinal examination of frequently rewritten memories
revealed extended sequences of unsupported versions, but some notes also returned to source-supported
states (Fig. 3d). These findings do not establish that rewriting causes factual errors: complex records
may be rewritten more frequently, and information unsupported by a particular source is not necessarily
false. They nevertheless challenge the assumption that iterative refinement invariably improves memory
quality. Source-linked provenance, verification against original records and explicit separation of source facts
from generated interpretations merit investigation, especially when updated notes are reused in subsequent
reasoning.
Long-term conversational memory requires accessible and complete evidence
The RealMem exper-
iments revealed a substantial gap between structured memory and access to the full conversation history.
On 325 medical-subset questions, FullContext outperformed all four memory-based approaches in overall
perfect-answer accuracy (Fig. 5a). Relative to A-MEM, it uniquely answered 55 questions correctly, whereas
A-MEM uniquely answered 22; the paired difference was significant under the exact McNemar test (𝑃< 0.001).
This is not evidence that FullContext is correct on every question, but it indicates that existing memory systems
lose information or fail to exploit information that remains accessible in the complete history. Evidence
completeness also mattered: A-MEM’s perfect-answer rate increased from approximately 5% when no gold
memories were retrieved to 89% when all were retrieved (Fig. 5b). However, some errors persisted despite
complete evidence retrieval, reinforcing the distinction between access and utilization. Conversation length
produced a more nuanced picture. Across naturally occurring histories containing 2–238 sessions, longer
histories were negatively associated with answering performance for A-MEM (adjusted odds ratio 0.42)
and MemZero (0.68), but not significantly for the other methods (Fig. 5c). In contrast, when the same 100
questions were evaluated under controlled histories with median lengths of 23,000, 36,000 and 48,000 tokens,
neither A-MEM nor LightMem exhibited a consistent performance decline (Fig. 4a). Relocating relevant
evidence within a fixed history also produced limited overall deterioration, although A-MEM declined at
the greatest tested distance (Fig. 4b). Across 600 question-condition observations, A-MEM achieved a QA
score of 89.7 with complete evidence retrieval versus 43.2 with incomplete retrieval (Fig. 4c). These results
suggest that natural conversation length is entangled with evidence distribution, updating requirements and
question complexity; token count alone is not an adequate explanation for long-term memory failures. The
18


contrast between observational and controlled results is methodologically important. Longer natural histories
may contain more factual revisions, references distributed across sessions, or multiple related entities that
must be reconciled. Removing irrelevant material from a fixed question, by comparison, does not necessarily
reproduce these challenges. Thus, the absence of deterioration in a controlled length experiment should not
be interpreted as proof that memory remains robust in arbitrarily long real-world interactions. Benchmarks
should independently vary history length, number of required evidence items, temporal update frequency and
the placement of contradictory or superseded statements.
Hybrid retrieval and task-aware utilization offer complementary benefits
HMRU was designed to
address the retrieval and utilization limitations identified above. It combines lexical and semantic retrieval with
cross-encoder reranking and task-aware instructions, while retaining the underlying memory bank and frozen
language model. On the two biomedical question-answering tasks, HMRU-I improved exact-match F1 from
39.27 to 43.48 on PharmKG and from 52.94 to 57.64 on ADInt relative to A-MEM (Table 3). The component
analysis indicated different sources of improvement. On PharmKG, BM25 augmentation reduced classified
retrieval failures from 55.3% to 50.0%; on ADInt, task-aware instructions improved F1 from 54.6 to 57.6
after retrieval enhancement (Fig. 6). Across both tasks, identified utilization failures decreased from 99 to 76
following prompt adaptation. These results support combining evidence acquisition with better interpretation
rather than treating retrieval alone as sufficient. The benefits were not universal. HMRU-C modestly improved
mortality AUPRC from 9.81 to 10.03, and HMRU-I increased readmission AUPRC from 61.91 to 62.17,
but neither configuration improved opioid overdose AUPRC over the A-MEM baseline. This limitation
is consistent with the weaker same-label enrichment observed in clinical prediction: identifying relevant
medical information and discriminating future outcomes are not equivalent objectives. Clinical prediction
may require outcome-aware retrieval and uncertainty-sensitive utilization strategies beyond those effective for
factual question answering. In long-term conversation, HMRU-R increased the RealMem perfect-answer rate
from 80.3% to 85.2%, reducing the gap to FullContext (90.5%) by nearly half (Fig. 7). Paired comparison
identified 26 corrected questions and 10 newly incorrect answers (exact McNemar 𝑃= 0.011). Gold-memory
recall was unchanged in 15 of the 26 corrected cases, suggesting that evidence ranking or presentation
may contribute beyond retrieving additional annotated memories. More extensive restructuring was not
consistently beneficial: chronological HMRU-T reduced accuracy on some dynamic updating and temporal
reasoning questions. Thus, utilization strategies should reflect the specific information requirements of the
task, rather than assuming that additional structure always helps.
4 Methods
4.1 Datasets, Memory Baselines, and Language Models
Datasets.
We evaluated biomedical agentic memory on six tasks covering knowledge reasoning, clinical
outcome prediction, and pathology classification. RiTeK-PharmKG [13] and RiTeK-ADInt [13] assess
biomedical question answering over relational knowledge. MIMIC-III Mortality and MIMIC-III Readmission
are clinical outcome prediction tasks derived from MIMIC-III [14].
TCGA-Reports supports cancer
classification from pathology report text [15], whereas MIMIC-IV OOD [16] evaluates opioid overdose
prediction using electronic health records from MIMIC-IV. We further evaluated long-term conversational
memory from the medical subset of RealMem [12], which includes multi-session interactions requiring
information retention, updating, and temporal reasoning. We report exact-match and ROUGE-1 precision,
recall, and F1 for the RiTeK tasks; AUPRC and AUROC are used for the four classification and prediction
tasks. For the RealMem evaluation, we used GPT-4o-mini as an LLM-based judge to assess the correctness
of generated answers.
19


Memory baselines with Language model backbones.
We compared five memory configurations spanning
structured memory management, direct retrieval, and full-context access. A-MEM[10] constructs linked
memory notes and supports dynamic indexing and iterative memory evolution.
LightMem [11] uses
lightweight filtering and memory consolidation to manage persistent information. MemZero was evaluated
as an additional persistent-memory approach, with Mem0 [9] providing the related framework for memory
extraction, retrieval, and updating. NaiveRAG [7] serves as an embedding-based retrieval baseline that selects
relevant source records without evolving a structured memory bank, following the general retrieval-augmented
generation paradigm. FullContext provides the available source records or conversation history directly
to the language model without selective retrieval. The same underlying training records and evaluation
questions were used for method comparisons within each task. We evaluated four open-weight language
models: Qwen3-8B1, Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct-25072, Meditron3-Qwen2.5-14B3, and HuatuoGPT-3-27B4.
4.2 Memory Retrieval Quality and Utilization
Analysis design.
To examine why different memory systems yield different downstream outcomes, we
analyzed test-set retrieval and prediction traces from A-MEM, MemZero, LightMem, NaiveRAG, and
FullContext across six biomedical benchmarks. The analyzed predictions were generated by HuatuoGPT-3-
27B, with each method drawing on the same 1,000 training examples per dataset for memory construction.
Only memory items that actually entered the final inference prompt were included. We organized the analysis
into three layers: answer or label exposure, answer retention following memory construction, and relevance
of the retrieved evidence. The full three-layer analysis was applied to RiTeK-ADInt and RiTeK-PharmKG.
For TCGA-Reports, MIMIC-IV OOD, MIMIC-III Mortality, and MIMIC-III Readmission, we assessed label
exposure and source relevance, but did not apply literal answer-retention checks because class labels were not
consistently preserved as explicit text in the memory representations.

Layer 3: Evidence relevance.
For RiTeK-ADInt and RiTeK-PharmKG, we employed Qwen3-30B-A3B-

cases with matching and nonmatching outcome labels.
Memory utilization.
Finally, we compared prediction correctness across evidence-availability and relevance
categories. For biomedical question answering, we further measured the frequency with which generated
answers matched labels associated with retrieved memory sources, distinguishing correct and incorrect
predictions. Together, these analyses characterize the relationships among answer availability, information
retention, evidence relevance, and downstream model performance.
4.3 Construction of Controlled Conversational Memory
To systematically evaluate the robustness of memory systems to conversation length and evidence distance,
we constructed two controlled experiments from the RealMem benchmark. Each question is associated with a
sequence of historical conversation sessions, categorized as relevant sessions containing information needed
to answer the question or unrelated sessions that do not provide the required evidence. All transformations
were performed at the session level, preserving the original content of retained sessions and the evidence
required for answering each question.
Conversation history length.
We constructed three conditions, Short, Medium, and Long, by selectively
removing unrelated sessions preceding the earliest evidence-bearing session. Relevant sessions and all
sessions occurring after the earliest evidence were preserved across conditions. In the Short condition, all
21


preceding unrelated sessions were removed. In the Medium condition, unrelated sessions were removed
sequentially from the beginning of the conversation until the remaining unrelated content was closest to half of
its original token count. The Long condition retained the original conversation history without modification.
This procedure produced three levels of historical context while preserving the evidence required for each
question, allowing us to examine how preceding contextual information influences memory retrieval and
answer generation.
Evidence distance.
To isolate the effect of evidence position, we constructed Near, Medium, and Far
conditions by rearranging unrelated sessions without removing or modifying their contents. All three
conditions contained the same set of sessions and therefore maintained the same overall context length. In the
Near condition, unrelated sessions were placed before the relevant evidence, positioning the evidence closer
to the final question. In the Far condition, unrelated sessions were placed between the relevant evidence and
the question, increasing the distance between them. The Medium condition distributed unrelated sessions
approximately equally by token count before and after the evidence. This design isolates evidence position
from context length and information availability, enabling a controlled assessment of memory sensitivity to
retrieval distance.

clinical risk factors while accounting for outcome prevalence. We examine complementary configurations
to distinguish the contributions of retrieval and utilization. HMRU-R enhances evidence selection, while
HMRU-I further incorporates task-aware instructions. HMRU-C additionally adopts a compact memory
representation:
𝑅compact(𝑚𝑖) = [Input(𝑚𝑖); Answer(𝑚𝑖); Context(𝑚𝑖)].
(5)
For long-term conversational memory, HMRU-T examines chronological evidence organization.
All
configurations operate without modifying the underlying language model parameters.
5 Conclusion
In this study, we systematically evaluate agentic memory systems across biomedical knowledge reasoning,
clinical prediction, and long-term conversational memory tasks. We examine how memory construction,
22


retrieval, utilization, and evolution influence downstream performance, identifying task-dependent limitations
in evidence relevance, information fidelity, and long-term memory reliability. Our analyses show that
retrieving relevant information does not guarantee correct utilization, semantic similarity does not necessarily
correspond to clinical outcome similarity, and iterative memory updates may introduce source-unsupported
content. We further demonstrate that long-term conversational memory performance cannot be explained
by history length alone. To improve evidence retrieval and utilization, we introduce HMRU, a training-free
framework that enhances biomedical knowledge reasoning and long-term conversational memory performance.
These findings highlight the importance of evaluating memory systems beyond downstream accuracy, with
particular attention to evidence accessibility, source fidelity, and effective utilization.

C Additional Related Work
Retrieval-augmented knowledge integration.
Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) conditions language-
model predictions on evidence selected from external collections, providing an alternative to relying exclusively
on parametric knowledge [7, 17]. Subsequent work has incorporated retrieval and evidence assessment
into the generation process itself [18]. In biomedical applications, retrieved demonstrations have supported
few-shot medical entity recognition [19], while external evidence has been used for healthcare prediction and
cancer-related language understanding [1, 6]. Evaluations of biomedical RAG have also examined robustness
to noisy evidence and the ability of models to recognize their own limitations [3]. Complementing external
retrieval, K2K investigates access to knowledge within model representations through activation-guided
probing and reranking [20]. These studies establish a range of mechanisms for accessing task-relevant
information, but evidence availability alone does not determine whether the information is appropriate for a
particular clinical question.
Structured and relational evidence retrieval.
Biomedical evidence frequently involves relationships
between entities, events, and attributes that cannot be adequately represented by isolated text passages.
GraphRAG [21] organizes entity relationships into hierarchical community summaries for corpus-level
reasoning, while knowledge-graph retrieval has also been explored for tracking changing entity states [22].
HippoRAG [23] leverages knowledge graphs and graph-based ranking for multi-hop retrieval, whereas
condensed relational paths have been used to support zero-shot link prediction [24]. G-Retriever [25]
identifies relevant subgraphs for question answering over textual graphs, and KG2RAG [26] connects related
passages through knowledge-graph structures to improve evidence coverage. LightRAG-Driven Medical
QA [27] further explores graph-based retrieval for medical question answering. RiTeK [13] evaluates complex
biomedical reasoning over textual knowledge graphs, while GLEN-Bench [28] extends graph–language
evaluation to nutritional-health knowledge. These studies demonstrate the value of relational structures for
evidence retrieval, while leaving open how reliably retrieved information is preserved, updated, and utilized
within persistent memory systems.
Adaptive retrieval and interaction-aware reasoning.
Recent approaches increasingly integrate retrieval
into multi-step reasoning rather than treating it as a fixed preprocessing operation. IRCoT [29] interleaves
retrieval with chain-of-thought reasoning, while FLARE [30] introduces active retrieval during generation.
RAG-GD [31] explores in-context optimization to adapt how frozen generators utilize retrieved evidence,
whereas DeepRAG [32] investigates when additional retrieval is warranted during reasoning. RIVO [33]
examines lightweight interventions that redirect agent behavior during longer research trajectories, while
Adaptive-RAG [34] selects retrieval strategies according to question complexity. RICE-PO [35] develops
localized learning signals for interactive retrieval policies by evaluating executable retrieval actions and their
counterfactual outcomes, whereas Self-RAG [18] incorporates self-reflection to guide retrieval and generation.
Together, these methods address complementary aspects of adaptive reasoning, including evidence acquisition,
generator adaptation, trajectory control, and policy learning, motivating the evaluation of retrieval as part of a
broader information-use process.
Implications for biomedical agentic memory.
Unlike one-off retrieval, persistent memory systems may
transform source records into compact representations, revise those representations across interactions, and
reuse them for subsequent questions. This setting raises additional questions about whether stored content
remains supported by its source, whether retrieval identifies evidence relevant to the specific biomedical
fact or clinical outcome, and whether the generator uses that evidence correctly. BioMem examines these
dimensions jointly across biomedical reasoning, prediction, and long-term conversational tasks. HMRU
35


further investigates whether complementary retrieval and task-aware evidence utilization can mitigate selected
limitations without updating the underlying language-model parameters.
