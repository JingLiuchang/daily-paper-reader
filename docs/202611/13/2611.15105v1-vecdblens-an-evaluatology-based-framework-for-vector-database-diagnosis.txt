Abstract
Vector databases are increasingly deployed as retrieval pipelines in which represen-
tation, indexing, and query-time execution jointly determine accuracy, throughput,
and cost. Existing vector database evaluations primarily focus on indexing while
treating embeddings as fixed inputs and ignoring the role of query-time execution,
making it difficult to decide whether a bottleneck should be addressed by changing
the embedding model, the index, or the query-processing functions. To address the
above limitation, we introduce VecDBLens, an Evaluatology-based framework that
treats the function under diagnosis as an evaluated object and all remaining dataset,
workload, parameter, and runtime factors as matched evaluation conditions. VecD-
BLens attributes performance differences to individual evaluated objects through
comparisons under matched evaluation conditions, summarizing their performance
and stability across multiple datasets and workloads. It further analyzes interac-
tions among objects using analysis of variance (ANOVA), thereby turning vector
database benchmarking from leaderboard ranking into a reproducible diagnostic
procedure. VecDBLens reveals two key insights. First, retrieval performance is
fundamentally upper-bounded by embedding quality, revealing that many apparent
indexing failures are therefore representation-side limitations. Second, stronger
index parameterization is not always beneficial: overly aggressive configurations
can reshape neighborhood topology and cause recall regression. These results
challenge the common practice of evaluating vector databases primarily through
indexing and show that real-world limitations arise from constraints across retrieval
functions rather than any single function alone. VecDBLens provides a princi-
pled basis for identifying the true performance drivers of vector databases and for
guiding their design in practical applications. The source code is available from
https://github.com/zhouyutong20/VecDBLens.

Introduction
Background. Vector databases have become core infrastructure for modern retrieval applications,
including semantic search, recommendation, retrieval-augmented generation, and multimodal re-
trieval [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]. Their observed performance, however, is not determined by an ANN index
in isolation [6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11]. A deployed vector retrieval pipeline first maps raw objects into a
representation space, then organizes vectors into an index, and finally executes query-time search
together with runtime constraints.
Limitation and Motivation. Existing benchmarks have produced valuable progress in comparing
ANN algorithms, typically under fixed embeddings and a narrow range of configurations [12, 13, 14].
This design is appropriate for measuring approximation loss inside a given vector space, yet it is

This paper asks a diagnostic question: when a vector retrieval pipeline fails to meet a target Recall–
QPS trade-off, which object is actually responsible? Answering this question requires evaluating
objects under matched evaluation conditions rather than ranking whole databases under a fixed
benchmark setting.
We identify two evaluation biases that prevent such a diagnosis: (1) index-centric attribution.
Existing benchmarks often define ground truth using distance-based nearest neighbors, which may
deviate from the natural relevance relationships in real-world cross-modal retrieval. As a result,
retrieval failures caused by weak or misaligned embeddings may be incorrectly attributed to index
structures. (2) narrow configuration space. Although existing benchmarks vary configurations such
as graph connectivity, probing budget, or search depth, they often treat embeddings, workloads, and
filtering regimes as fixed. This can favor vector databases whose optimal operating regimes happen
to match the benchmark setting, while underestimating systems that are effective under different
evaluation conditions.
Contribution. To address these issues, we propose VecDBLens, shown in Figure 1. VecDBLens
decomposes the retrieval pipeline into embedding, indexing, and query processing functions.
For each diagnostic question, the function under diagnosis is the evaluated object (EO), while all
remaining dataset, workload, parameter, and hardware factors form the evaluation conditions (ECs).
By comparing EOs only over shared or stratified ECs, VecDBLens estimates individual object effects
across matched evaluation conditions instead of conflating them with dataset or workload variation.
VecDBLens separates the individual object effects across matched evaluation conditions from
the extrinsic variation introduced by datasets, workloads, filter selectivity, parameter settings, and
runtime environments. We summarize our contributions as follows:
First, we identify two key biases in existing vector database benchmarks: index-centric attribution
and narrow configuration space. We formulate a diagnostic framework for vector retrieval pipelines
that distinguishes evaluated objects from their evaluation conditions and supports both attribution to
individual objects and analysis of interactions among objects.

Second, we introduce a reproducible diagnostic protocol that attributes performance differences to
individual evaluated objects under matched evaluation conditions and analyzes interactions among
objects using analysis of variance (ANOVA). This protocol goes beyond leaderboard comparison by
explaining whether performance variation is driven by embedding, indexing, or query-time execution.
Finally, we instantiate VecDBLens on two complementary workloads: multimodal text-to-image
retrieval and filtered ANN search. The experiments show that embedding quality imposes an effective
upper bound on retrieval performance, and stronger index parameterization is not monotonically
beneficial.

Related Work
2.1
Vector Databases, Applications, and Benchmarks
The architecture of vector databases [4, 2, 15, 16] has fundamentally evolved from basic Approximate
Nearest Neighbor (ANN) search [17, 7, 6, 3, 11] to complex databases designed for real-world
application demands, such as cross-modal retrieval and filtered ANN search [18, 19, 20, 21, 22].
Early developments predominantly focused on ANN index performance. For instance, Faiss [3]
integrated various indexing algorithms like HNSW [6] and IVFPQ [7], while libraries such as
hnswlib [8] pioneered the standalone, highly optimized implementation of the HNSW algorithm.
However, isolated ANN indexes struggle with the inherent complexities of real-world scenar-
ios—specifically, overcoming the embedding blind spots in cross-modal data understanding and
addressing the overhead of scalar data processing [22, 23]. To resolve these limitations, modern
vector databases have emerged, integrating embedding generation as a retrieval function. Vector
databases like Milvus [2] and Qdrant [1], for example, natively integrate embedding models such as
CLIP [24], SigLIP2 [25] and Qwen3-VL [26] to facilitate seamless cross-modal search. Similarly, to
efficiently handle filtered ANN search, specialized solutions including RangePQ [14], ACORN [12],
and DiGRA [13] have been introduced. These approaches optimize query execution through tech-
niques such as range space partitioning and dynamic adjustment of graph connectivity based on
filtering selectivity.
Diverging from existing benchmarks [27, 28, 29] that primarily focus on end-to-end rankings or
approximation curves, this paper diagnoses the retrieval pipeline as a set of interacting functions: em-
bedding, indexing, and query processing. The goal is to provide a reproducible diagnostic procedure
that identifies whether the limiting factor is the representation space, the index approximation, or the
query-time execution.
2.2
Evaluation Methodology
Traditional ANN benchmarks primarily assess index recall and efficiency within a given vector
space [27, 29]. Iceberg [30] extends evaluation to downstream tasks, including image classification,
face recognition, text retrieval, and recommendation, examining embedding loss, metric misuse, and
distribution sensitivity. These studies motivate further diagnosis of how retrieval functions affect
application-level performance.
Firstly, they predominantly rely on synthetic workloads to reveal how different index structures,
parameter settings, and execution backends affect recall, latency, and system cost [27]. A critical
flaw of these synthetic workloads is that the nearest neighbors are purely derived from mathematical
distance metrics, rather than emerging naturally from real-world applications. Our experiments
demonstrate that in real-world cross-modal retrieval scenarios, in the worst cases, a mere 2.04%2
of the nearest neighbors calculated via distance metrics correspond to the true semantic nearest
neighbors.
Secondly, because existing benchmarks strictly focus on ANN indexing, most existing evaluations
treat embeddings as fixed input vectors and define exact nearest neighbors within the given vector
space as the ground truth by brute-force search [10, 27, 29, 11]. As a result, they can explain

end-to-end retrieval performance to different pipeline functions.
To overcome these limitations, we introduce VecDBLens, a framework that builds on the pipeline
functions decomposition and attribution methods of Evaluatology [31, 32], which has been applied to
the evaluation of CPUs [33, 34], large language models [35], and time-series forecasting models [36].
By systematically sampling across an extensive configuration space of datasets and function param-
eters to construct rigorous evaluation conditions, VecDBLens quantifies both average effects and
stability of distinct functions, making it possible to distinguish consistent function behavior across
conditions from condition-specific wins.
3
The VecDBLens Framework
Existing benchmarks primarily evaluate indexes under fixed embeddings and a narrow range of
configurations. Distance-based neighbors can differ from application-level relevance, while different
retrieval configurations can perform best under different workloads. These limitations make it difficult
to determine whether a retrieval bottleneck originates from embedding, indexing, or query processing.
VecDBLens addresses this diagnostic question by attributing performance differences to individual
evaluated objects under matched evaluation conditions and quantifying interactions among objects
using ANOVA [37].
3.1
Evaluation System Modeling
Problem statement. VecDBLens decomposes the retrieval pipeline into embedding, indexing, and
query processing. For a dataset instance d, let corpus(d) denote its retrieval corpus and Qd its query
set. Given a query q ∈Qd, the pipeline is

The embedding function Ee maps raw objects into dense vectors Ee(corpus(d)) and Ee(q) [26, 24].
The indexing function Ia,β constructs a searchable structure Ia,β(Ee(corpus(d))) using algorithm a
and construction parameters β, such as HNSW [6], IVFPQ [7], or CAGRA [9]. The query-processing
function Sγ searches the index under query-time parameters γ, trading off recall and efficiency. Flat
search provides an exact-search reference for the selected embedding and similarity function.
Evaluated objects and evaluation conditions. Guided by Evaluatology [32, 34, 33, 35, 36], we
construct a Self-Contained System (SCS) for the systematic evaluation of vector databases. The
SCS comprises evaluated objects (EOs) and evaluation conditions (ECs).
The evaluated object, denoted by eo, is the model, algorithm, or configuration under diagnosis.
An evaluation condition instance, denoted by ec, specifies the values of the remaining factors for
that evaluation. A factor selected as the EO in one analysis can therefore be held fixed as part of
ec in another. As shown in Figure 1, when the embedding model is the EO, the dataset, index
configuration, search settings, and runtime environment are ECs. When the index algorithm is the
EO, the embedding becomes an EC. The full notation and condition-space definition are provided in
Appendix A.
3.2
Individual Object Attribution and Cross-object Interactions
Based on the SCS model, VecDBLens first systematically evaluates an object’s performance across
matched evaluation conditions, then examines how its effect depends on other evaluated objects.
Individual object attribution. VecDBLens constructs the EC space from the Cartesian product of the
selected factor values [38], retaining compatible configurations. Each point in this space represents
one evaluation condition instance ec. For individual object attribution, VecDBLens varies eo while
holding ec fixed. For indexing, for example, ACORN [12] and RangePQ [14] are compared under
identical embedded data, queries, search settings, and runtime resources. The evaluated differences
in recall, throughput, or cost quantify the performance effect attributable to the EO choice under the
matched evaluation condition. VecDBLens aggregates these differences with equal weighting across
dataset or workload strata, and reports mean performance and within-stratum variance to characterize
effectiveness and stability.
4


Cross-object interactions. Vector embedding, index construction, and query processing interact.
VecDBLens distinguishes these objects for attribution without assuming independence. All evaluation
results come from complete end-to-end retrieval pipelines with compatible configurations of all three
objects. When one object is selected as the evaluated object (EO), the other two form part of the
matched evaluation conditions (ECs). VecDBLens examines these interactions by testing whether the
performance difference attributed to one object changes with the configuration of another object. For
example, repeating the construction comparison at different search budgets reveals whether more
extensive traversal reduces the recall difference between constructed indexes.
Within each index family, VecDBLens evaluates complete matched grids of embedding, construc-
tion, and query-processing configurations, fixing the dataset, candidate pool, queries, and runtime
environment. Functional ANOVA [37] decomposes Retention@10 and log2(QPS) into main effects
and two- and three-factor interactions. Main effects summarize each factor’s average effect across
the other configurations; interaction terms capture departures from their additive combination. Their
variance shares quantify interaction strength, while conditional comparisons explain its direction.
Formal attribution metrics, grid weights, and interaction definitions are provided in Appendix A.1.
4
Experiments
This section instantiates VecDBLens on two representative scenarios: (1) Multimodal retrieval
scenario (In Sec. 4.2). We diagnose how much retrieval quality is determined before indexing and
how much of this fixed-representation potential is retained by ANN backends. (2) Filtered ANN
search scenario (In Sec. 4.3). We diagnose whether stronger index parameterization consistently
improves performance, and how workload conditions, such as filter ratio and dimensionality, reshape
the useful regime of different methods.
Statistical protocol details. For each EO, VecDBLens summarizes performance across evaluated ECs.
We report mean performance, stability measured by average within-stratum variance, bootstrap
confidence intervals, and observed maximum and minimum values. Direct comparisons between
EOs use matched evaluation conditions where available. For throughput, we report geometric mean
QPS because throughput varies by orders of magnitude.
4.1
Evaluation Setup
We instantiate the VecDBLens Evaluated Object (EO) and Evaluation Condition (EC) framework
across two representative scenarios: multimodal retrieval and filtered ANN search. These scenarios
provide complementary perspectives on ANN evaluation: cross-modal retrieval examines semantic
alignment between queries and retrieved objects, while filtered ANN examines interactions between
scalar constraints and ANN indexes. Both have practical relevance to multimedia search, multimodal
RAG, and vector queries with scalar constraints. Runtime settings and parameter ranges are provided
in Appendix B.
Multimodal Retrieval. The configuration space is defined as Θmm = E × A × B × Γ, representing
embeddings, ANN backends, construction parameters, and search parameters. For embedding
attribution, the EO is an embedding model e ∈{CLIP, SigLIP2, Qwen3-VL} [24, 25, 26], evaluated
against a Flat exact-search EC to expose the representational upper bound.
For ANN attribution, the EO is a backend a ∈{Faiss HNSW, Faiss IVFPQ, cuVS IVFPQ,
cuVS CAGRA} [3, 7, 9] along with its parameters (β, γ), while the selected embedding becomes
an EC. Retention is measured relative to the corresponding Flat reference. The data context spans
text-to-image retrieval tasks across Flickr30K (test5k/full), MSCOCO (test5k/full), and Visual-
News [39, 40, 41].
Filtered ANN Search. The configuration space is Θfilter = Af × Bf × Γf × T , representing
algorithms, construction parameters, search parameters, and runtime settings. The evaluated EOs
include Flat, RangePQ, DiGRA, ACORN-1, and ACORN-28 [14, 13, 12]. Algorithm-level compar-
isons use matched thread settings; ACORN is tested under single- and multi-threaded settings with
matched construction and search configurations to assess parallel execution effects. For parameter
attribution, the EO is a controlled parameter change, such as increasing M or efSearch, with the
remaining factors fixed as ECs. Parameters linked by a configuration rule are changed jointly. The
experiments use million-scale workloads derived from the widely used SIFT1M and GIST1M bench-

4.2
Experiment 1: Embedding Quality and ANN Retention
This experiment applies a two-level attribution. First, we treat the embedding model instance e as the
EO and the dataset-pool setting d as the EC, using Flat search to measure retrieval quality under exact
search. Second, holding the embedding e and dataset-pool setting d fixed, we treat the ANN backend
and its configuration (a, β, γ) as the EO and compare its recall with the corresponding Flat reference,
separating representation quality from changes introduced by indexing and query processing.
4.2.1
Embedding-induced upper bound.
For a fixed embedding model instance e and dataset instance d, Flat exact search yields the best
attainable retrieval quality:
U(e, d, k) = Rk(e, Flat, ∅, ∅, d).
(2)
Rather than replacing ground-truth labels, U(e, d, k) quantifies the intrinsic geometric alignment of
relevant objects in the representation space. If an embedding space maps relevant pairs far apart,
downstream ANN indexes cannot systematically recover this lost relevance.
As shown in Table 1, Qwen3-VL achieves the highest mean Flat Recall@10 across five conditions
(0.6988), outperforming CLIP (0.5230) and SigLIP2 (0.2225). This indicates that a large portion
of retrieval variation is predetermined before indexing. Many observed failures are fundamentally
representation-side limitations.
To distinguish embedding-model effects from differences in output dimensionality and assess the
uncertainty of matched-condition comparisons, we conduct matched-dimension experiments and
cluster-aware significance tests (Appendix C.1).
Insight 1. Embedding quality establishes the strict upper bound for retrieval performance.
4.2.2
Candidate-pool scaling.
We examine whether exact-search retrieval quality is robust to distractor scaling by expand-
ing the candidate pool from test5k to full-pool.
We define Pool-Scaling Retention as
PSR(e, dtest5k, dfull, k) = U(e, dfull, k)/U(e, dtest5k, k), which measures the retention of exact-
search recall under larger pools. We macro-average PSR across Flickr30K and MSCOCO.
Figure 2 shows that Qwen3-VL retains a macro-averaged 72.3% of its Recall@10 after expansion,
compared with CLIP (49.6%) and SigLIP2 (33.2%). Stronger embeddings achieve higher fixed-pool
recall and exhibit greater robustness to candidate-pool expansion in these experiments. Detailed
numerical results are reported in Appendix C.
Insight 2. High-quality embeddings preserve more of their exact-search recall under candidate-
pool expansion.

4.2.3
ANN retention relative to Flat.
We next quantify how much of the embedding’s exact-search recall is retained by the ANN back-
end. Under matched ECs, we define Gap(e, a, β, γ, d, k) = U(e, d, k) −Rk(e, a, β, γ, d) and
Retention(e, a, β, γ, d, k) = Rk(e, a, β, γ, d)/U(e, d, k) for U(e, d, k) > 0. A Retention score near
1 indicates that ANN recall closely matches the corresponding Flat reference.
Figure 2 shows that graph-based indexes are highly effective at retaining exact-search recall: cuVS
CAGRA and Faiss HNSW achieve mean Retention@10 values of 0.9959 and 0.9875, respectively,
across all configurations. In contrast, PQ-based indexes trade recall for memory efficiency, with Faiss
IVFPQ dropping to 0.7932. Detailed retention and Gap-to-Flat statistics are provided in Appendix C.
Insight 3. ANN indexes may lose relevant results through approximation but cannot improve the
underlying embedding’s semantic alignment.
4.2.4
Recall-QPS frontier
VecDBLens further evaluates how embeddings and indexes jointly shape the Pareto frontier of retrieval
quality and system cost. While Qwen3-VL provides the highest exact-search recall, searching its
4096-dimensional vectors incurs substantially lower throughput. Under Flat search, Qwen3-VL is
5.6×–10.0× slower than CLIP across dataset conditions.
However, ANN indexing substantially reduces the search cost while retaining retrieval quality. As
shown in Figure 3, among configurations with Retention@10 ≥0.99, Faiss HNSW boosts QPS
from 13.8 (Flat) to 1898.6 on VisualNews with CLIP. GPU-accelerated CAGRA reaches 29,433.6
QPS while retaining 99.28% of the corresponding Flat recall.
Ultimately, end-to-end performance depends on interactions among retrieval functions. The embed-
ding function determines exact-search recall and influences distance-computation cost, while the
indexing and query-processing functions determine the recall-throughput trade-off relative to this
reference.
Insight 4. The Recall-QPS Pareto frontier is jointly determined: embeddings establish the
exact-search reference, and indexing and query processing determine how efficiently its recall is
retained.

Cross-object interactions
We examine how the performance effect of one evaluated object depends on other objects using func-
tional ANOVA (Section 3.2). Table 2 shows that interactions explain a larger share of Retention@10
variation than of throughput variation. Embedding–query processing dominates IVFPQ’s quality
interactions. For HNSW, the dominant contribution varies between embedding–index construction
and embedding–query processing across build repetitions. Three-factor interactions remain small for
both indexes.
Conditional comparisons illustrate how query processing moderates construction effects. Increasing
HNSW’s efSearch by 8× reduces the mean standard deviation of Retention@10 across construction
configurations by 36.0%–78.0%, showing that larger search budgets can reduce sensitivity to index
construction.
Insight 5. Cross-object interactions explain a larger share of variation in retrieval quality than
in throughput.
4.3
Experiment 2: Parameterization Attribution in Filtered ANN
This experiment investigates filtered ANN from three attribution perspectives. For algorithm-level
comparisons, the EO is the method a, while parameters, filter ratio, dimensionality, and runtime
settings are ECs. For parameter monotonicity analysis, the EO is a controlled parameter change (e.g.,
M or efConstruction), with the remaining factors fixed as matched ECs. Parameters linked by a
configuration rule are changed jointly. For workload analysis, filter ratio and dimensionality define
EC strata for comparing exact pre-filtering with approximate search.
4.3.1
Non-monotonicity of stronger parameterization
We compare adjacent parameter levels under matched ECs and record a violation whenever Re-
call@100 decreases. Table 3 shows that both ACORN and DiGRA exhibit such decreases as
construction parameters increase.
We identify two mechanisms that can reduce filtered recall in the evaluated ACORN implementation.
First, increasing graph connectivity raises the threshold for exploring neighbors of neighbors. As
Table 4 shows, nodes in the denser graph have more filter-compatible neighbors on average, but fewer
nodes qualify for two-hop expansion and Recall@100 falls.
Second, a larger construction budget can allow the candidate set to reach a stopping threshold before
closer candidates replace those encountered earlier. The resulting graph has longer, less directionally
diverse edges. Query traces show that search on this graph visits more nodes but reaches fewer exact
neighbors.
We also observe recall decreases under stronger ACORN construction settings on App Reviews and
in both CC-News time windows. Appendix D.1 reports the real-workload results.
Insight 6. Stronger index construction parameterization is not monotonically beneficial; aggres-
sive graph connectivity can disrupt filtered neighborhoods and regress recall.

4.3.2
Effect of filter ratio and dimensionality
We examine how filter ratio and dimensionality affect the choice between exact pre-filtering and
approximate search. On the 128-dimensional SIFT workload (Figure 4), Flat exact pre-filtering
remains competitive at filter ratios of 0.01% and 0.1% because few vectors satisfy the filter. At
ratios of 1% and above, Flat must scan a larger candidate set, and DiGRA achieves the highest
single-threaded QPS at a target Recall@100 of τ = 0.95.
On the 960-dimensional GIST workload (Figure 5), each distance computation is more expensive.
DiGRA achieves the highest single-threaded QPS at the same recall target across all tested filter
ratios, including 0.01%. For filter ratios from 1% to 50%, ACORN-28 achieves 10.8×–1051.7× the
QPS of single-threaded Flat on SIFT and 97.4×–1842.9× on GIST.

4.3.3
Runtime and cost regimes
Beyond recall and throughput, practical deployment depends on index construction time and memory
use. Figure 6 shows how these costs vary across filtered ANN methods. DiGRA achieves near-perfect
recall in many configurations but requires long build times and substantial memory. RangePQ
builds quickly and has the smallest increase in memory use during construction, but none of its
tested configurations reaches the Recall@100 target of 0.95 on 960-dimensional GIST. Meanwhile,
ACORN strikes a balance, achieving robust Recall-QPS trade-offs—especially when multi-threading
(ACORN-28) yields a 14.2× median QPS speedup. Consequently, filtered ANN performance cannot
be abstracted to a single metric. It requires object-level diagnosis across recall, throughput, build
time, and parameter stability.

Conclusion
This paper introduces VecDBLens, an Evaluatology-based diagnostic framework that shifts vector
database retrieval pipeline evaluation from black-box database comparison to object attribution
across embedding, indexing, and query processing functions. Our analyses yield two critical insights.
First, the embedding space strictly defines the upper bound of retrieval performance; downstream
ANN indexes only dictate the efficiency-cost trade-off in realizing this potential, rather than creating
semantic relevance. Second, in filtered search, stronger index parameterization is not monotonically
beneficial and can surprisingly regress recall. Furthermore, the optimal boundary between exact
pre-filtering and ANN approximation is non-static, jointly governed by dimensionality and filter
selectivity. Ultimately, by isolating bottlenecks to representation limits, index approximations, or
query executions, VecDBLens prevents misdirected system optimizations and guides more robust
vector database design.
6
Limitations and Future Work
VecDBLens currently emphasizes recall, throughput, memory usage, construction cost, and parameter
stability. These metrics characterize retrieval quality and system cost but provide limited insight
into semantic failure mechanisms. Future work will develop finer-grained semantic diagnostics and
incorporate energy efficiency and incremental indexing costs.
Beyond cross-modal and filtered retrieval, we have extended evaluation to Chinese text retrieval and
multi-vector retrieval in scientific and financial domains (Appendix E). Broader language coverage,
temporal data streams, and dynamic workloads remain directions for further evaluation. Extending
attribution to database functions such as update processing and query optimization would also broaden
the framework’s scope.
We are committed to long-term maintenance, including continued updates to the implementation,
evaluation protocols, and workload coverage as vector retrieval evolves. Our goal is to advance evalu-
ation from asking which system ranks first to identifying which retrieval function limits performance,
under which conditions, and why. This perspective supports reproducible research, efficient system
development, and application-aware vector database design.

Experimental Configurations
This appendix describes the configurations for the two primary retrieval scenarios in Section 4.
B.1
Runtime Environment
The primary multimodal measurements use a server equipped with two Intel Xeon Gold 6330 CPUs
and four Tesla V100-PCIE-32GB GPUs. These measurements use 28 CPU threads or one V100 by
default. QPS variance shares in the interaction analysis are computed separately within each run. The
VisualNews protocol for Qwen3-VL uses four separately built CAGRA graphs on V100 GPUs and
merges their retrieval results.
In the filtered ANN comparisons, Flat, RangePQ, DiGRA, and ACORN-1 use one search thread.
ACORN-28 uses 28 search threads. Comparisons of ACORN-28 with Flat therefore describe complete
system configurations.
B.2
Multimodal Retrieval Configurations
The multimodal retrieval experiments evaluate text-to-image retrieval on Flickr30K, MSCOCO, and
VisualNews. Flickr30K and MSCOCO include both small candidate-pool and full-pool settings,
while VisualNews provides a million-scale candidate pool. The evaluated embedding models are
CLIP, SigLIP2, and Qwen3-VL, with embedding dimensions of 512, 768, and 4096, respectively.
Flat exact search is used as the exact-search reference under each embedding space and similarity
metric. The evaluated ANN backends include Faiss HNSW, Faiss IVFPQ, cuVS IVFPQ, and cuVS
CAGRA.

Matched-dimension controls. CLIP, SigLIP2, and Qwen3-VL produce 512-, 768-, and 4,096-

PCA-512, fitted using corpus vectors only and applied to both corpus and query vectors, and first-
512 truncation, which retains the first 512 coordinates. Both transformations are followed by L2
normalization; CLIP remains unchanged at its native 512 dimensions.
The comparisons cover the Flickr30K and MSCOCO test5k and full-pool conditions, plus VisualNews.
Table 10 reports the five-condition mean Flat Recall@10 under native dimensions, PCA-512, and
first-512 truncation. All three settings use the same 1,446-query VisualNews subset shared by the
models.
Significance tests. For each model pair within each condition, we compute the difference between
the two binary Flat Recall@10 outcomes for every query. Queries sharing the same relevant-image
set form one cluster, giving 1,000 clusters per Flickr30K condition, 5,126 per MSCOCO condition,
and 1,446 for VisualNews. The primary analysis uses a two-sided cluster-level sign-flip permutation
test with 100,000 permutations and 10,000 cluster-bootstrap resamples for 95% confidence intervals.
Entire clusters are flipped or resampled together, preserving the query-weighted mean difference. We
additionally use a two-sided paired t-test implemented as an intercept-only regression on query-level
differences with CR1 cluster-robust standard errors and G −1 degrees of freedom, where G is
the number of clusters. Holm correction is applied to the 15 model-pair-by-condition comparisons
separately within each dimensionality setting and test method.
Results. Table 10 shows that model differences remain statistically significant when output di-
mensionality is matched. All 15 comparisons in each setting are significant under both tests after
Holm correction, and all cluster-bootstrap and cluster-robust 95% confidence intervals exclude zero.
The permutation estimates reach the Monte Carlo resolution of 1/(100,000 + 1) before correction.
The two dimensionality controls also affect the models differently: Qwen3-VL’s mean Recall@10
changes from 0.7235 to 0.7213 under PCA and 0.7065 under truncation, while SigLIP2’s changes
from 0.2249 to 0.1252 and 0.1782.

E.2
Multi-Vector Retrieval
We evaluate ColBERTv2 [45] on BEIR SciFact and FiQA [46, 47, 48], with 5,183/57,638 documents
and 300/648 test queries, respectively. Token vectors have 128 dimensions, with query/document
lengths of 32/180 and the model’s masking and normalization. Exact search applies MaxSim [49]
to uncompressed token representations over the full corpus. PLAID [50] generates candidates with
2-bit compression and reranks them using the same uncompressed representations, with budgets
K ∈{100, 250, 500, 1000}. Measurements use one V100 GPU and 16 CPU threads; latency excludes
encoding.
Table 19 reports the exact reference and the smallest and largest evaluated candidate budgets. On
FiQA, K = 100 reduces mean latency from 109.27 to 7.20 ms, with an nDCG@10 difference
of −0.001452 (pointwise 95% query-bootstrap interval: [−0.004604, 0.000182]). Larger budgets
improve exact-neighbor recovery without consistent gains in task quality. On SciFact, K = 1000 is
slower than exact search, showing how corpus size and search budget affect the quality–cost trade-off.

Question: Do the main claims made in the abstract and introduction accurately reflect the

2. Limitations
Question: Does the paper discuss the limitations of the work performed by the authors?
Answer: [Yes]
Justification: We explicitly discuss the limitations of our framework, including its current

specific scope of the evaluated workloads.

Answer: [N/A]
Justification: The paper does not present new theorems or formal proof-based claims. Its

in the main text and appendix.

needed to reproduce the reported results.
