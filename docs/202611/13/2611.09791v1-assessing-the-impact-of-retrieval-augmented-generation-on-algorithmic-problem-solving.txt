Abstract
Competitive programming problems pose distinctive challenges for Large
Language Models, as they demand not only proficient code generation but
also advanced algorithmic reasoning. This study presents a Retrieval-Augmented
Generation approach specifically designed to enhance the capacity of Large
Language Models to address such problems.
Our approach integrates an
embedding-based retrieval mechanism and a similarity search algorithm to
identify analogous problems within a knowledge database, employing a dy-
namically adjusted threshold to mitigate retrieval noise. We conducted an
evaluation using the CodeContests dataset, focusing on the test split prob-
lems that exhibit similarity to instances from the training split. The experi-
mental framework considered three conditions: (i) problems provided solely
with their original descriptions, (ii) problems supplemented with randomly
selected examples from the training split, and (iii) problems augmented with
similar examples retrieved through the proposed method. In addition, we
incorporated a Min-K membership inference analysis to examine whether
the generated code solutions were influenced by potential memorization of
training data within the evaluated Large Language Models. This comple-
mentary perspective enables a deeper understanding of model behavior by
distinguishing genuine generalization through retrieval from implicit recall of
pretraining samples. Performance was assessed using both pass rates and the
CodeForces Elo rating of the problems. The results provide empirical evi-

dence that similarity-based retrieval substantially enhances Large Language
Models performance in competitive programming scenarios, offering an scal-
able framework for improving code generation in algorithmic problem-solving
contexts.

1. Introduction
Large Language Models (LLMs) have demonstrated proficiency in solving
generic problems across various professional domains [1, 2, 3]. Although some
evaluation benchmarks indicate high performance — such as on HumanEval,
where GPT-4o [4] and Claude-3.5-Sonnet [5] achieved precision scores of
90% and 92%, respectively [6] —, other benchmarks show that LLMs exhibit
limitations on competitive programming tasks [7, 8, 9, 10]. For instance, in
the analysis by Zheng et al. [8], Claude-3.5-Sonnet achieved a 0% precision
rate on medium and hard problems and only 14.1% on easy ones. In the
same benchmark, even the best-performing LLMs, such as Gemini-2.5-Pro
[11] and DeepSeek-R1 [12], could not solve a single hard problem.
These errors typically arise due to a lack of specific contextual knowledge
and a limited understanding of underlying data structures, leading to halluci-
nations — outputs that are syntactically invalid or logically inconsistent [13].
Under a deeper epistemological analysis, it becomes evident that Large Lan-
guage Models (LLMs) operate on opinion-based trajectories—that is, on the
formulation of statements not necessarily grounded in verifiable evidence, but
rather derived from inferences or interpretations constructed within a limited
context of knowledge. Hallucination, in this sense, may be understood as the
generation of a perception or belief that, while internally coherent, does not
correspond to empirical reality. In LLMs, this phenomenon arises because
the model “believes” it is producing a consistent and contextually appropri-
ate response, even though such a response lacks factual support.To mitigate
this, Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) [14, 15] has been proposed as a
mechanism to ground the model’s outputs in relevant external knowledge by,
with an Information Retrieval (IR) algorithm, retrieving contextually similar
information from a structured corpus [14, 15]. This work investigates how
RAG can be used to enhance the problem-solving capabilities of LLMs.
The proposition for applying RAG in this context is based on two key
2


observations. First, studies indicate that problems within competitive pro-
gramming repositories often share underlying algorithmic patterns and solu-
tion structures [16, 17]. Second, the practice of providing relevant examples
in the prompt (few-shot prompting) is an established technique for improving
LLM performance on complex tasks [18, 19]. Based on this,the hypothesis
is that a RAG system that retrieves previously solved problems with analo-
gous characteristics to a target problem can provide the model with relevant
context and reduce hallucination.
To achieve this, we developed an Information Retrieval (IR) pipeline that
employs embedding similarity with a dynamic threshold [20]. We evaluated
this system on the CodeContests dataset [21], which is divided into a train-
ing set of 13,328 problems and a test set of 165 problems. In addition to
the problem title and statement, each sample includes metadatdynamig pro-
gramming, bitwise operations). The training split served as the knowledge
base from which we retrieved similar examples to augment the LLM prompts
for solving the problems in the test set. The correctness of the generated solu-
tions was then evaluated using the CodeForces [22] judge system, allowing us
to measure the impact of the retrieved context on the model’s performance.
The evaluation involves three distinct prompting strategies: (i) a zero-shot
baseline using only the original description, (ii) a random retrieval baseline
retrieving unrelated problems from the knowledge base, and (iii) the pro-
posed similarity-based RAG approach retrieving contextually aligned exam-
ples. Our analyses demonstrate that the similarity-based retrieval consis-
tently outperforms both baselines, validating the hypothesis that retriev-
ing analogous problems improves LLM reasoning on complex programming
tasks.
Furthermore, the Min-K membership inference results reveal that
many generated code lines correspond to known training patterns, under-
scoring the importance of disentangling memorization effects from genuine
reasoning improvements. Together, these findings provide a dual epistemic
safeguard: RAG anchors outputs in external knowledge, while membership
inference verifies their originality and data provenance. See Figure 1 for a
graphical overview of the system architecture and execution pipeline.
The main contributions of this paper are summarized as follows:
1. We design a RAG framework tailored to competitive programming
tasks, integrating an embedding-based similarity search with an adap-
tive thresholding mechanism to retrieve contextually relevant problems
while minimizing retrieval noise.

2. Related Works
Several works have investigated the ability of LLMs to solve competitive
programming problems, experimenting different evaluation metrics and ap-
proaches to maximize the correctness of generated solutions [23, 24, 25, 8,
16, 9, 7].
2.1. Evaluation Strategies
A main challenge in this domain is developing evaluation metrics that
go beyond simple pass/fail rates and accurately reflect a model’s problem-
solving skill [24]. While simply annotating the number of correctly solved
problems is informative, it fails to distinguish between solving a trivial prob-
lem and solving one that challenges even the best human competitors [8].
To address this gap, recent benchmarks [24, 26, 27] have adopted the Elo
rating system [28], a method widely recognized for assessing the relative skill
levels of players in competitive games — and is also used in CodeForces plat-
form. In the context of competitive programming platforms, the Elo system
provides a unified scale to quantify both problem difficulty and competitor
skill. The difficulty rating d assigned to a problem is calibrated against the
entire community of human participants; a contestant with an Elo rating of
d has a 50% probability of solving this given problem [27].
The Elo rating of a contestant, in turn, provides a clear measure of their
standing within the competitive community [8]. For example, a rating of
1600 (an “Expert” on CodeForces) places a user in the top 11% of active
participants, while a rating of 2400 (“Grandmaster”) signifies performance
in the top 1%.
An elite rating of 2700 indicates a skill level superior to
approximately 99.9% of competitors. By framing LLM performance in this
way, we can move from asking “how many” problems a model solved to “how
skilled” the model is.
Following this evaluation method, our study treats each LLM as a virtual
contestant and calculates its equivalent Elo rating. We specifically employ
Bayesian Maximum-A-Posteriori (MAP) Elo rating [29], a method also uti-
lized in [8], to estimate the most probable skill level of a model given its
performance.

2.2. Performance Enhancement Approaches
Prior research has explored enhancing LLM performance through two
main strategies: granting models access to external knowledge sources or
fine-tuning them on specific datasets. For instance, augmenting models with
tools like terminal and internet access has enabled systems such as o3 and o4-
mini-high to achieve Elo ratings of 2706 and 2719 respectively [26]. Another
strategy involves fine-tuning LLMs on competitive programming datasets to
train them on problem-solving patterns, a notable example being Google’s
AlphaCode [21]. Since these benchmarks also use Elo for evaluation, we used
it as a common metric to assess the effectiveness of our technique in relation
to these prior work.

3.2. Information Retrieval Algorithm
The IR algorithm retrieves similar solved problems from the database.
First, each problem description in the database is transformed into a high-
dimensional vector embedding using the ‘all-MiniLM-L6-v2’2 model. These
embeddings capture the semantic essence of the problems [35]. The collec-
tion of embeddings is then indexed using FAISS (Facebook AI Similarity
Search) [36], employing an ‘IndexFlatL2’ structure that computes Euclidean
distances. Because the vectors are already normalized, we can compute the
similarity directly from the Euclidean distance (on a 0-to-1 scale).
A distinctive aspect of our IR system is its adaptive threshold mechanism,
introduced by [20]. Instead of a fixed cutoff, the threshold for deeming a
retrieved problem “similar enough” is dynamically adjusted based on the
2https://huggingface.co/sentence-transformers/all-MiniLM-L6-v2

highest similarity score. The initial threshold used for the similarity is 70%,
but if the highest similarity is strong (above 85%), the threshold becomes
more stringent; if weaker (less than 75%), it relaxes, but never below the
70% minimum. Similar adaptive strategies are used in RAG to reduce noise
[37], and we apply it with the same intent. As an additional measure to
mitigate noise, the system is configured to return a maximum of the three
most similar problems.
3.3. Dataset Overview
We utilized the CodeContests dataset [21] for this study, designating its
training set as the knowledge base for our RAG system and its test set
for evaluation. The dataset, sourced primarily from CodeForces problems,
is structured as a JSON file where each entry contains the problem’s title,
its full description, input/output examples, the official CodeForces rating
(when applicable), relevant thematic tags, and verified code solutions. These
problems do not require a specific programming language for implementation,
allowing for flexibility in the solution approaches. This section describes how
we leveraged this dataset to construct our RAG pipeline and analyzes the
distribution of difficulties and categories of the problems used.
Of the 165 problems in the CodeContests test set, 81 had at least one
similar problem found in the knowledge base by our Information Retrieval
algorithm. The evaluation was therefore performed on this subset, as the
remaining problems had no retrieved counterparts and would not receive any
additional information from the RAG system. Furthermore, none of the prob-
lems in this evaluation set were part of the RAG’s knowledge base, ensuring
a fair assessment of its generalization capabilities [14, 15, 38, 39]. Problems
can be classified into one or more categories (e.g., dynamic programming,
sorting, binary search), and the distribution of these thematic tags across
both datasets is shown in Figure 2.
To better understand the difficulty distribution of the problems, we per-
formed a classification based on their CodeForces Elo ratings. While the
Elo system provides a continuous scale, we grouped the problems into three
discrete categories for a better analysis: Easy, Medium, and Hard. Prob-
lems with an Elo rating between 0 and 1000 were classified as Easy, those
in the range (1000, 2000] as Medium, and any problem rated above 2000 as
Hard. It is worth noting that while every problem in the test set is from
CodeForces, the training set (our knowledge base) also contains problems
from other sources, so not all of them had the CodeForces Elo information

3.4. Min-K-Based Membership Inference Analysis
To complement the RAG evaluation and assess whether any code so-
lutions in the test set were potentially present in the training corpora of
the evaluated LLMs, we implemented a Min-K Membership Inference ap-
proach [40]. This method estimates the likelihood that a given code sample
was part of a model’s pretraining data, thereby quantifying exposure bias
and memorization within large language models.
The core intuition behind this algorithm is that language models assign
systematically higher confidence to samples encountered during training com-
pared to unseen ones. Training data typically yield higher average token
probabilities (or equivalently, lower perplexities).
Rather than relying on
mean likelihoods, the Min-K approach focuses on the lowest K% of token
log-probabilities, representing the model’s most uncertain predictions, be-
cause these regions provide stronger discriminatory signals between memo-
rised and novel samples [40]. By analyzing the extremal uncertainty pattern
rather than overall fluency, the method enhances detection precision and
reduces false positives.

code snippets. Given a tokenized code sequence x = (x1, x2, . . . , xn), the
model fθ computes the conditional log-probabilities for each token. These
values are sorted in ascending order to extract the Min-K% subset, from
which a stability-based membership score is computed as:

To transform continuous Min-K scores into binary membership classifica-
tions, we apply a percentile-based thresholding strategy [41]. The threshold
τ is defined as the average of the 75th percentile of known member scores and
the 25th percentile of non-member scores. This empirically driven approach
efficiently separates the two distributions while maintaining robustness to
outliers and noise.
Compared to fixed or heuristic thresholds, percentile-
based selection adapts better to model variance and dataset composition,
leading to more stable and interpretable decision boundaries. The overall
inference workflow comprises three main phases:
1. Problem Evaluation Phase: For each competitive programming prob-
lem, the corresponding verified solution code is tokenized and evaluated
by the target LLM. In this phase, we considered the best solution for
each problem (a total of 81 problems) across the six selected LLMs.
Owing to the high computational and API costs associated with prob-
ability estimation, only one representative solution per problem was
analyzed. The best solution was determined according to the short-
est and earliest correct submission among all generated outputs. This
choice ensures consistency across models while maintaining a fair and
cost-efficient evaluation process.
2. Detection Phase: The Min-K algorithm computes per-token log-probabilities,
sorts them, and derives SMinK(x) for each solution. This score is then
compared against τ to classify each code sample as Member or Non-
member.
12


3. Analysis Phase: Membership inference outcomes are aggregated to es-
timate model exposure levels across problem categories and difficulty
levels, complementing the RAG-based performance evaluation.
By integrating this step, our methodology not only measures performance
improvement via retrieval augmentation but also accounts for potential mem-
orization biases in LLMs. This enriches the interpretability of results, dis-
tinguishing genuine reasoning gains from possible recall of pretraining data
in competitive programming contexts.
3.5. Experiments
To perform the experiment, we designed an experiment involving the six
selected LLMs and the 81 problems from the filtered test set. Each problem
was presented to every LLM under three distinct conditions. The first sce-
nario, a zero-shot baseline, provided the LLM with only the original problem
description. The second, a random-retrieval baseline, augmented the prompt
with randomly selected problems from the knowledge base to analyze the
effect of simply adding more context. The third scenario, our proposed RAG
approach, enhanced the prompt with the most similar problems identified by
our IR algorithm. For the RAG scenario, each test problem was matched
against all problems in the knowledge base using the IR system.
The semantic similarity was computed exclusively using the textual con-
tent of the problems’ descriptions. This was a choice to simulate a scenario
where, upon encountering a new problem, one only has access to its textual
statement, not pre-existing metadata like a solution or tags. After gener-
ation, all outputs were submitted to the CodeForces judge system, which
verifies correctness by running the solution against a comprehensive set of
hidden test cases and returns whether the solution was accepted or rejected
(rejection occurs if any test case fails).
The structure of the prompts was kept consistent in all experiments to en-
sure a fair comparison. For the zero-shot baseline, the prompt contained the
problem’s title, its full textual description, and any provided input/output
examples, followed by an instruction to generate a solution. For the few-
shot conditions (random and RAG-based), the prompt began with the target
problem’s information, followed by a clear demarcation for the upcoming ex-
amples. Each retrieved example included its original title, description, tags,
similarity score, and verified solution. In the prompts that had examples, an
13


additional instruction was included to direct the model to take advantage of
the provided examples when formulating its solution.
The primary objective was to quantify the performance improvement at-
tributable to the quality of the examples. We measured performance using
two metrics: the overall pass rate (pass@1), calculated as the percentage of
problems correctly solved on the first attempt, and the Elo rating of each
model — which is translated from the Elo data of the solved and unsolved
problems. The pass rate provides a direct measure of success, while analyz-
ing the Elo ratings offers a nuanced understanding of the model’s capability
to solve problems.
In addition to these benchmarks, we performed a complementary Min-K
Membership Inference analysis on the generated code solutions. This step
estimated the likelihood that a given solution resembled or originated from
the model’s pretraining data. We calculated a probability confidence score for
each line of code within a problem’s best solution, indicating its likelihood of
being a member (i.e., part of the model’s pretraining data) or a non-member,
as determined by Algorithm 1.
4. Results and Analysis
This section presents the results of our experiments, analyzing the per-
formance of six LLMs on 81 problems across three prompting conditions:
zero-shot, random-retrieval, and our RAG approach. We evaluated perfor-
mance using two primary metrics: the pass@1 rate and the Elo rating. Other
factors like the categories and difficulty of the problems are also considered.
On average, our RAG approach achieved a 20.37% success rate, out-
performing both the zero-shot (17.9%) and random-retrieval (17.28%) ap-
proaches. These results demonstrate a crucial trade-off. On the one hand,
the retrieval of any additional problem inevitably introduces a degree of noise
to the prompt [42]. On the other, selecting the right examples can provide
useful content and insights to the model [43].
The performance decrease
seen with random retrieval (-0.62%) suggests that when examples are irrele-
vant, the negative impact of this noise reduces the model’s reasoning process.
However, the 2.47 percentage point gain from our RAG method demonstrates
that when the examples are contextually relevant, the value of the provided
insights outweighs this negative impact, leading to performance gains.

5. Conclusion and Future Work
This paper investigated whether Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)
can systematically improve Large Language Models (LLMs) on competitive
programming — a setting where success depends on algorithmic reasoning,
robustness to hidden test cases, and the ability to avoid hallucinated or log-
ically inconsistent code. We proposed a similarity-based RAG pipeline that
retrieves up to three relevant solved problems from a large knowledge base
using embedding similarity and a dynamic, query-dependent threshold de-
signed to reduce retrieval noise while preserving useful exemplars. We eval-
uated six representative LLMs on a filtered subset of 81 CodeContests test
problems for which at least one similar training example could be retrieved,
comparing three conditions: zero-shot, random-retrieval augmentation, and
similarity-based RAG.
Across models, similarity-based retrieval delivered consistent net gains
over both baselines. On average, our RAG condition increased pass@1 to
20.37%, compared to 17.9% (zero-shot) and 17.28% (random retrieval), indi-
cating that example relevance is critical: adding unrelated context tends to
introduce harmful noise, whereas aligned examples provide actionable algo-
rithmic structure that outweighs this cost. In Elo terms, the proposed RAG
approach produced an average rating of 1014, improving over the zero-shot
(925) and random-retrieval (927) conditions, reinforcing that RAG benefits
are not only about solving more problems but also about solving harder ones.
19


Model-level analysis showed that stronger reasoners benefited most reli-
ably from retrieval. DeepSeek-R1 achieved the highest overall performance
and improved from 61.7% to 64.2% pass@1 under RAG, while random re-
trieval reduced it to 59.3%, again highlighting the importance of retrieval
precision. Category-level results suggested that RAG can be especially valu-
able in domains where a single key insight unlocks the solution (e.g., num-
ber theory, where DeepSeek-R1’s Elo increased markedly under RAG), while
other categories exhibited occasional degradation, consistent with remaining
sensitivity to misleading or weakly matched exemplars.
To complement performance evaluation with an epistemic perspective, we
applied a Min-K membership inference analysis to estimate whether gener-
ated solutions were likely influenced by memorized training patterns. The
analysis indicated that a substantial portion of code lines across the best
solutions had membership confidence above 0.5, suggesting meaningful ex-
posure to competitive-programming-like code in model pretraining corpora.
Importantly, this does not imply that models learned exact problem–solution
mappings; rather, it supports the interpretation that models may have seen
fragments of similar code patterns without robust alignment to the corre-
sponding statements — helping explain why retrieval of structured, relevant
worked examples can still provide measurable gains in problem-solving.
Despite these findings, the study has limitations. First, evaluation was
necessarily restricted to the subset of test problems with retrieved neighbors,
which focuses the analysis on settings where retrieval is feasible. Second, we
used a fixed embedding model and a bounded number of retrieved examples
(up to three), leaving open questions about the optimal representation, re-
trieval depth, and prompt packaging under tight context budgets. Third,
the membership inference is an indirect signal and should be interpreted as
evidence of exposure patterns rather than definitive proof of duplication.
Future work will extend this framework along four directions. (i) Re-
trieval quality: explore hybrid retrieval (dense + sparse), reranking, and
retrieval-time verification to further reduce harmful exemplars, especially
for categories where RAG occasionally degrades performance. (ii) Adapta-
tion and personalization: learn per-model and per-category retrieval policies
(thresholds, number of examples, and formatting) to maximize gains while
controlling noise. (iii) Broader benchmarks: replicate the pipeline on more
recent and contamination-aware datasets, enabling a clearer separation be-
tween genuine reasoning improvements and latent memorization. (iv) Next-
generation models and safety diagnostics: evaluate upcoming models under
20


the same dual protocol—performance plus membership inference — to better
understand how post-training advances (e.g., alignment and reasoning-tuned
training) interact with retrieval grounding in competitive programming con-
texts.
