Frontier language models are increasingly deployed as agents, and how well an agent performs depends not
only on its model but also on its harness: the prompts, tools, memory, and control loop through which the
model acts. When an agent is applied to a new problem, a natural question is whether it can use the feedback of
the task to improve the way it works, and not merely its answer. Most existing benchmarks keep the agent’s
harness fixed and score the solution it produces, or target large-scale model training. We introduce Flex-RSI, a
benchmark for the recursive self-improvement of agents on vision and robotics tasks under a fixed budget. In
each round, an executor improves the task solution, which is kept only if it scores higher on held-out validation
data, and an improver then revises the harness, including the executor and itself. Every run starts from the same
general-purpose harness, and the final solution is scored on hidden test data. Evaluating closed-source and
open-weight models on tasks spanning perception, reasoning, robot action, 3D reconstruction, visual creation,
and infrastructure, we find that self-improvement helps most when the agent builds its own solution, that
feedback recording only success or failure holds back open-weight models, and that open-weight models fall
furthest behind where outputs must pass strict checks of physical stability or exact correctness. Paired runs
further show that editing the harness itself contributes to the gains.

Introduction
Frontier large language models now routinely act as agents that write code, conduct experiments, and read the
results over multiple steps (Anthropic, 2026a; OpenAI, 2026b; Google DeepMind, 2026; Qwen Team, 2026b;
DeepSeek-AI, 2026; GLM-5 Team, 2026). An agent’s performance depends not only on the underlying model
but also on its harness: the prompts, tools, memory, and control loop through which the model acts. This raises
a practical question for anyone who deploys an agent on a new problem: given only the task, can the agent
use the task’s feedback to improve the way it works under a fixed budget, and not merely its answer?
Most existing benchmarks run each model inside a fixed harness, often the vendor’s own, and score only the
solution or artifact it produces, mostly on machine-learning research tasks with a continuous metric (Chan
et al., 2025; Wijk et al., 2024); those that also let the agent revise its harness (RSI-Exam Team, 2026) or that
center on upstream model training at scale (OpenRSI Foundation, 2026) remain within machine-learning
research. We introduce Flex-RSI to measure how well a frontier agent improves both its solution and its own
harness on general vision and robotics tasks, a setting in which agents are increasingly deployed, each attempt
consumes real compute, and the goal is a better solution within hours to days.
In Flex-RSI, the harness consists of two components that alternate over rounds (Figure 1a). The executor
improves the task solution: it modifies the solution, runs experiments, and evaluates them on the labeled
development data, and its candidate is kept only if it also scores higher on held-out validation data. The
improver then reviews the round and modifies the harness itself, including the executor’s prompts, tools, and

The tasks differ in what the agent must produce (a single answer or a solution it builds), how the evaluator
scores it (on a scale or only as success or failure), and how strictly the output is checked. From experiments on
24 tasks spanning perception, reasoning, robot action, 3D reconstruction, visual creation, and infrastructure
(Figure 1b), with 18 closed-source and open-weight models, we find:
1. Tasks in which the agent builds the solution leave more room for self-improvement: tasks whose
solution is a single answer to each question leave little room to improve, while tasks in which the agent
builds its own solution leave the most, and there models pull apart (Figure 1c).
2. The granularity of feedback matters: feedback that scores each episode on a scale helps all models,
whereas feedback that records only success or failure leaves open-weight models barely improving on the
solutions they build.
3. Strict checks of physical stability and exact correctness pose the greatest challenge: outputs that look
plausible often fail checks of physical stability or exact correctness, and open-weight models fall furthest
behind on them.
Paired runs further show that letting the improver edit the harness raises the test score in six of seven pairs of
otherwise identical runs (Section 5.4).

Related work
2.1 Benchmarks for self-improving agents
Benchmarks for machine-learning engineering and scientific research measure whether agents can turn a
task description into a working result. MLE-bench evaluates machine-learning engineering on competition
tasks (Chan et al., 2025); RE-Bench compares agents with human experts on research-engineering prob-
lems (Wijk et al., 2024); and PaperBench assesses the replication of machine-learning research (Starace et al.,
2025). Related evaluations examine the design and execution of machine-learning experiments (Huang et al.,
2023b) and the generation of executable programs for data-driven scientific discovery (Chen et al., 2025).
Their primary evaluation targets are the models, code, and research results that an agent produces.

Evaluations framed around recursive self-improvement cover several different objects of improvement. RSI-
Exam supports executable research on both task methods and harnesses for frozen models, followed by hidden-
test evaluation (RSI-Exam Team, 2026). OpenRSI Index spans foundation-model development, including large-
scale training and lighter-weight inference, agent, and systems tasks (OpenRSI Foundation, 2026). Autonomous
post-training is evaluated through controlled training runs and assessments of the resulting models (Rank
et al., 2026; Huang et al., 2026c), while algorithm-design tasks ask agents to improve training procedures
that are subsequently evaluated by retraining (Chi et al., 2026). Sustained machine-learning research with
repeated validation feedback and a separate hidden test is evaluated in AutoResearchExam (Ramesh et al.,
2026). Harness engineering also appears alongside model-training tasks in Vals RSI Index (Chen et al., 2026b).
TraceTrial-Bench evaluates the reliability of agent harnesses themselves rather than their improvement (Cao
et al., 2026). These settings share an interest in autonomous experimentation, but differ in whether the
optimized artifact is data, a training algorithm, model weights, or the harness around a model.
Complementary studies examine how experience becomes reusable improvement. Data-centric research under
a fixed post-training stack is studied in RSIBench-Data (Meng et al., 2026). Procedural-ability transfer and
the conversion of episodic experience into reusable skills are evaluated in EvoAgentBench (Gao et al., 2026b)
and SkillEvolBench (Lei et al., 2026), respectively. EvolveTool-Bench checks whether evolved tools satisfy
independent conformance tests beyond completing the current task (Kaliyev and Maryanskyy, 2026). The
contribution of persistent experience to later personal-agent behavior is isolated through matched persistence-
on/off comparisons in PAST-Bench (Xue et al., 2026). Whether self-evolving language-model agents learn new
tasks without forgetting earlier ones is evaluated in a related study (Chen et al., 2026e). EmbodiedCLBench
evaluates whether embodied agents can reuse the experience gained on basic tasks to solve unseen compositions
of them (Huang et al., 2026b), and Embodied Skill Bench measures how packaged embodied skills help agents
on physical tasks (Han et al., 2026c). Together, these studies motivate evaluating both immediate task gains
and improvements that remain useful on subsequent tasks.
Flex-RSI studies task-solution and harness improvement together on vision, robotics, and related downstream
tasks. For each task, every model starts from the same general-purpose harness and starter solution, then
alternates execution and harness revision under a fixed budget. The improver can edit the executor’s prompts,
tools, and workflow as well as its own prompts, settings, and lessons. This shared protocol separates three
questions: how much the task solution improves, what additional gains come from editing the harness, and
whether a harness built by one model helps another model on the same task. A review of the evidence behind
recursive self-improvement claims finds that few studies compare improvement procedures from identical
starting artifacts at a matched budget (Chen et al., 2026g); the paired runs with and without the improver
(Section 5.4) make this comparison.
2.2 Frontier agents
Language-model agents connect reasoning to actions and environmental feedback through an execution loop.
ReAct interleaves reasoning and tool use (Yao et al., 2023), Reflexion carries verbal feedback into subsequent
trials through episodic memory (Shinn et al., 2023), and Voyager accumulates executable skills through
open-ended interaction in Minecraft (Wang et al., 2023). Coding interfaces such as Claude Code and Codex
CLI expose file editing and command execution (Anthropic, 2026c; OpenAI, 2026d). OpenHands provides
infrastructure for software-development agents (Wang et al., 2025b), while SWE-agent studies how an agent–
computer interface supports repository navigation, editing, and testing (Yang et al., 2024). Research agents
extend these capabilities to experimentation: AIDE searches over candidate solution code (Jiang et al., 2025),
and The AI Scientist combines research ideation, experiments, paper writing, and review in an automated
workflow (Lu et al., 2024). Frontier models have also been evaluated as robot agents that act in the control
loop (Huang et al., 2026a; Zhou et al., 2026b; Yao et al., 2026c) or write reusable robot tools (Ge et al., 2026),
across many embodiments (Yang et al., 2026b) and against learned policies (Zhang et al., 2026f), and coding
agents have been asked to reconstruct dynamic scenes as executable graphics programs (Shen et al., 2026a).
Automated agent design makes parts of this execution machinery optimization variables. Promptbreeder
evolves both task prompts and the mutation prompts that revise them (Fernando et al., 2023), while GEPA uses

reflection on execution traces to propose and select prompt updates (Agrawal et al., 2026). ADAS searches over
agent programs using an archive of prior designs (Hu et al., 2025), and AFlow searches over code-represented
workflows (Zhang et al., 2025b). A builder model can also learn principles for constructing the execution
environment of a fixed target model (Qian et al., 2026). AlphaEvolve applies evolutionary program search to
scientific and algorithmic discovery, with executable evaluators selecting candidate task programs (Novikov
et al., 2025). These approaches optimize objects ranging from prompts to complete programs, with different
boundaries around the optimization procedure itself.
Self-modifying agents extend this search to their own implementations. STOP applies its code improver to the
improver itself (Zelikman et al., 2024); Gödel Agent and SICA likewise revise agent logic and orchestration
code (Yin et al., 2025; Robeyns et al., 2025). The Darwin Gödel Machine retains an archive of empirically
evaluated, self-modified agents (Zhang et al., 2025a), while the Huxley–Gödel Machine also considers a
candidate’s potential to produce better descendants (Wang et al., 2026d). Meta-Harness searches harness
code using previous candidates, scores, and execution traces (Lee et al., 2026b). RHI represents the harness
as a prompt-level specification of the agent loop and refines it using pairwise feedback over its own revision
history (Lee et al., 2026a). Self-Harness uses execution failures to guide revisions to the model’s own
harness (Zhang et al., 2026a), and AIDE2 recursively revises research-agent code (Srikanth et al., 2026).
Several systems explicitly distinguish task improvement from improvement of the process that produces
it. Hyperagents combine a task agent and a meta agent in a shared editable program, allowing the self-
modification mechanism itself to evolve and studying transfer of meta-level improvements (Zhang et al.,
2026b). HSI separates a task harness, an evolver, and a meta-evolver under a fixed outer anchor (Zhou, 2026).
In robotics, RegenHarness revises harness configuration from execution records, with regression checks and
release gates governing acceptance (Wang et al., 2026a). Harness evolution around frozen models now spans
video understanding (Luo et al., 2026; Liang et al., 2026; Lin et al., 2026), image generation with a frozen
generator (Han et al., 2026a), 3D reconstruction (Yao et al., 2026a), and embodied agents, including robot
manipulation, where the harness is revised from diagnosed execution failures (Tian et al., 2026a; Feng et al.,
2026; Yu et al., 2026b; Du et al., 2026), experience is organized into knowledge or executable stacks shared
across tasks (Bao et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2026f; Wen et al., 2026), the system decides where to spend robot
trials (Song et al., 2026), and changes are tested in simulation before they reach the robot (Hu et al., 2026).
Studies of these loops find that the number of rollouts the optimizer sees in each round and the selection
rule determine whether an evolved robot harness generalizes (Wei et al., 2026b), that accepting any edit
that raises an aggregate validation score admits regressions and favors noise (Wang et al., 2026e), that proxy
improvements need not hold where the update is deployed (Wang et al., 2026b), and that an exact validation
score after every attempt opens the validation split to adaptive selection (Yao et al., 2026b). RRSI addresses
the risk that harness evolution overfits its training tasks, with gains that shrink or vanish on out-of-distribution
benchmarks, by constraining how edits are proposed and selected (Xia et al., 2026). Other studies ask which
failures harness evolution can repair and when weight training should take over (Tian et al., 2026b; Wang
et al., 2026c), argue that rewriting the scaffold around frozen weights avoids diminishing returns but not an
eventual ceiling (Bobadilla-Suarez et al., 2026), and report that weak evaluators and early failures in long skill
chains stall improvement over long robot runs (Wang, 2026). Surveys organize these developments by the
components that evolve and the feedback that drives their updates (Gao et al., 2026a; Fang et al., 2025; Chen
et al., 2026a), and recent surveys and position papers cover recursive self-improvement in physical AI (Han
et al., 2026b; Zhou et al., 2026a), agent harnesses for video generation (Fan et al., 2026), and the use of
frontier models for 3D modeling, computational design, and robotics (Dou et al., 2026).
These systems propose particular ways to accumulate experience, search for agent designs, or revise an
improvement procedure. Flex-RSI evaluates the ability to make such revisions through a shared executor–
improver harness (Section 3.2). Each frontier model operates both components from the same initial harness
and, on each task, the same starter solution and budget. The executor changes the task solution; the improver
changes the executor and its own instructions and lessons. The evaluator, data splits, and budget accounting
remain fixed. This setting makes the model’s capacity to improve its operating machinery the object of

comparison across vision, robotics, and related tasks, while keeping changes to the solution distinct from
changes to the harness.
2.3 Flex-RSI tasks
Flex-RSI draws its 24 tasks from six areas, combining established benchmarks, source datasets, and our own
curated creation tasks. Table 1 summarizes their sources and metrics. Each task provides a starter solution,
development data, hidden validation and test splits, and a fixed improvement budget, so that these resources
support the evaluation of changes to both task solutions and agent harnesses.
Action. The manipulation tasks use simulated environments from RoboDojo (Chen et al., 2026d) and Robo-
Casa365 (Nasiriany et al., 2026), which extends RoboCasa’s household simulation platform (Nasiriany et al.,
2024). Active Search uses H*Bench’s panoramic environments for visual search (Yu et al., 2026a), while
RoboLab uses the RoboLab-120 suite to evaluate a generalist tabletop manipulation policy (Yang et al., 2026a).
The LIBERO-based tasks (Liu et al., 2023) examine robustness to changes in initial conditions and task spec-
ifications through LIBERO-PRO (Zhou et al., 2025), and adaptation to changes during execution through
LIBERO-MAX (Zhang et al., 2026e). Embodied-SWE draws on EmbodiedSWE-Bench, where agents write
robot-control programs and receive scores based on physical task progress (Shen et al., 2026b). Related
simulation benchmarks extend such evaluation to whole-body humanoid loco-manipulation, as in SIMPLE (Wei
et al., 2026a).
Reasoning. Ego-Exo Memory uses the Ego-Exo4D portion of EgoExoMem for memory reasoning across
synchronized first- and third-person videos (Liu et al., 2026b; Grauman et al., 2024). Kinematics uses QuantiPhy
to infer physical quantities such as sizes, velocities, and accelerations from video (Puyin et al., 2026); Mass
adapts product photographs and metadata from Amazon Berkeley Objects for weight estimation (Collins
et al., 2022). Abstract reasoning spans grid transformations in ARC-AGI-1 and ARC-AGI-2 (Chollet, 2019;
Chollet et al., 2025) and interactive exploration in ARC-AGI-3 (ARC Prize Foundation, 2026). Spatial-VSI uses
VSI-Bench’s spatial questions about indoor videos (Yang et al., 2025).
Creation. Image Editing uses our own curated images and editing instructions, and Text-to-Image uses original
Chinese and English prompts. Multi-turn Editing follows the five-turn sessions of MSE-Bench, introduced with
VINCIE, in which each instruction acts on the previous result (Qu et al., 2026). Video Generation uses the
compositional prompts and evaluation criteria of T2V-CompBench (Sun et al., 2025).
Reconstruction. 3D Tracking adapts IGGT’s instance-tracking setting (Li et al., 2026) on ScanNet and Scan-
Net++ (Dai et al., 2017; Yeshwanth et al., 2023). Scene from Photo uses HARMONY300, introduced with
HARMONY for reconstructing furnished rooms from single images (Sun et al., 2026); its synthetic scenes derive
from 3D-FRONT (Fu et al., 2021). Scene from Video uses the indoor scenes released with HoloScene (Xia
et al., 2025), drawn from Replica and ScanNet++ (Straub et al., 2019; Yeshwanth et al., 2023), together with
CoDimRecon’s geometry, rendering, and physical-stability evaluation setting (Xie et al., 2026).
Perception. Visual Search combines high-resolution questions from V*Bench and HR-Bench 8K (Wu and Xie,
2024; Wang et al., 2025a). Video Quality combines subjective quality scores for YouTube-UGC videos (Wang
et al., 2019; Yim et al., 2020) with human preferences on generated-video pairs from Rapidata’s Veo 3.1
comparison dataset (Rapidata, 2025).
Infrastructure. VAE Decode uses the variational autoencoder released with MiniMax H3 (MiniMax, 2026b)
and asks the agent to accelerate its decoder while preserving the reference outputs on unseen inputs.

3.1 Problem setup
Each task provides the agent with a starter solution, compute resources, a budget, and data partitioned into
three disjoint splits. The development split, including its labels, is available to the agent for experimentation
and evaluation. The validation split is withheld from the agent and determines which candidate solutions are
retained. The test split is likewise withheld and is used only once, after the run terminates, to evaluate the
final solution. The objective of the agent is to maximize the test score of its final solution within the budget.
On a given task, every run starts from the same starter solution and is subject to the same budget, which limits
wall-clock time, GPU hours, model usage, the number of validation evaluations, and the number of rounds.
The 24 tasks are listed in Table 1 and described in Section A.
3.2 The self-improving harness
A harness is the program that mediates between a language model and a task. Through its prompts, tools,
control loop, and memory, it determines the context presented to the model and the actions available to it.
Since the harness consists of code and text, the agent can modify it in the same manner as any other program,
while the model weights remain fixed. In Flex-RSI, the harness 𝐻= (𝐸, 𝐼) comprises two components that query
the same model. The executor 𝐸operates on the task solution: it modifies the solution, conducts experiments,
and evaluates the results on the development split. The improver 𝐼operates on the harness: it analyses the
executor’s trajectories and results, diagnoses failures, and revises the prompts, tools, and workflow of the
executor as well as its own instructions and accumulated lessons.

Experiments
This section reports what the agents achieve, in the order in which a reader would ask about it: how well each
model does over all tasks and within each area (Section 4.2), how much of that is due to self-improvement
(Section 4.3), in which rounds the gains arrive (Section 4.4), and what they cost (Section 4.5). It reports what
happens; Section 5 examines why.

How results are reported. Each model is run once on each task it is evaluated on; where a run was repeated,
the latest run is reported. RoboDojo and RoboCasa are suites of 10 and 8 robot tasks, each run separately for
10 rounds and aggregated into one suite score. A run’s result is the test score of its final solution on the task’s
primary metric, on a 0–100 scale. Because the metrics differ across tasks, models are compared through ranks:
on each task, a model scores the share of the other models evaluated on the task that it beats, from 0 (last) to
100 (first). Its score in an area is the mean over the tasks of the area it was run on, and its overall score is
the mean of its five area scores. To measure self-improvement itself, we also report the validation score of
the starter solution and the best validation score reached during the run. The main results cover the 20 tasks
evaluated with at least six models; the four tasks evaluated with two models so far are reported in Table 12.

4.3 How much self-improvement adds
The scores above combine what a model can do from the start with what it gains by improving. Figure 3
separates the two by comparing the starter’s validation score with the best validation score reached during the
run. Self-improvement raises the validation score in 150 of the 177 runs (85%), by a median of 10.4 points.
The gain depends on how much the task leaves to build. It is largest where the agent builds most of its solution
from a weak starter: on 3D Tracking, RoboLab and Active Search the median validation score rises from 19.4,
8.3 and 23.0 to 62.2, 45.8 and 52.7 (Figure 3a). It is smallest where the starter already solves most of the
task, as on ARC-AGI-1 and ARC-AGI-2 (median starter 91.9 and 82.5, a median gain of 0 points on both).
Two tasks do not fit this pattern: ARC-AGI-3 and Scene from Video start low (3.8 and 17.3) and still barely
improve (to 4.1 and 18.5).

validation score in 94% of their 85 runs, by a median of 14.9 points, against 76% of 92 runs and 8.5 points for
open-weight models. Per model (Figure 3b), the mean gain ranges from 26.7 points for Claude Opus 5.5 and

4.4 Improvement over rounds
Figure 4a shows in which rounds the gains arrive. For each run that improves, we express its best validation
score after every round as a share of the run’s whole gain, and take the median over the runs of each area.
Most of the gain comes early: the median run reaches 90% of its gain by round 3 in Perception and Reasoning
and by round 4 in Creation. Action and Reconstruction take longer, reaching 90% only by rounds 6 and 8.
These are the two areas with the weakest starters (median validation score 16.7 and 19.4, against 60.0 to
78.8 in the other three), where the agent likely has to build a working solution before it can refine it.
4.5 Cost
Runs differ widely in what they spend. A run on one task, counting each robot task of RoboDojo and RoboCasa
separately, uses a median of 49.7 million tokens, and the middle 80% of runs use between 8.6 and 93.4
million. We compare tokens rather than prices, since the open-weight models are served on our own GPUs. To
compare models on the same footing, Figure 4b expresses each model’s token use relative to the median model
on the same task. Spending more does not buy a higher score: across the 12 models, the rank correlation
between relative token use and score is −0.05. Claude Opus 5.5, GPT-6.1 Sol and GPT-6 Astra, three of the
four highest-scoring models, use 0.35 to 0.61 times the median, whereas Gemini 4 Argon reaches a score close
to that of Opus with 1.66 times. Five of the six open-weight models use at least the median (1.0 to 1.75 times),
and all six score below 50.

Analysis and takeaways
Table 7 lists, for every task evaluated with at least six models, the quantities on which the findings of Section 1
rest. The tasks fall into two groups according to what the agent has to produce. On seven tasks, the solution

the output. These thirteen tasks are divided further according to how the task evaluator scores an episode:
on nine it assigns a score on a scale, and on four it records only whether the episode or level succeeded.
Sections 5.1 to 5.3 examine the three findings in turn, and Section 5.4 examines whether editing the harness
contributes to the improvement.

5.1 Tasks in which the agent builds the solution leave more room for self-improvement
Finding 1. When the agent builds the solution, self-improvement raises the score the most and separates the models;
when the solution is a single answer, the starter solution already scores high and the models end about as far apart
as they began. On 3D Tracking, every run starts from 19.4 and the best run gains 65.4 points.
On tasks whose solution is a single answer, the starter solution already passes each question to the model,
so it scores high from the start and its score depends on the model: its median validation score over these
seven tasks is 78.2, and the models are 27.5 points apart before the first round (medians over tasks). The

largest gain that any run achieves is 14.8 points at the median, and at the end of the runs the models are 25.0
points apart, about as far as at the start. On the tasks in which the agent builds the solution, every model starts

5.2 The granularity of feedback matters
Finding 2. When the evaluator scores each episode on a scale, open-weight models also improve the solutions they
build; when it records only success or failure, they barely improve, while closed-source models still do. On RoboCasa,
the median open-weight run gains 5.6 points and the median closed-source run 51.2.
On the nine tasks in which the agent builds the solution and the evaluator scores each episode on a scale, the
median gain over the open-weight runs of a task is 9.9 points at the median over tasks, against 28.4 for the
closed-source runs, and on 3D Tracking and Active Search it is close to that of the closed-source runs (39.8
against 42.8, and 29.3 against 28.4). On the four tasks whose evaluator records only whether an episode or a
level succeeded, namely RoboCasa, LIBERO-MAX, LIBERO-PRO, and ARC-AGI-3, the median open-weight gain
falls to 1.6 points, while the closed-source runs still gain 16.9. The median open-weight run does not improve
at all on LIBERO-PRO and gains 0.2 points on ARC-AGI-3. Since a candidate is retained only if it raises the
validation score (Equation (2)), a score on a scale rewards a change that improves part of an episode, whereas
a score that records only success or failure rewards a change only once it turns failed episodes into successful
ones.
5.3 Strict checks of physical stability and exact correctness pose the greatest challenge
Finding 3. Outputs that look correct often fail checks of physical stability or exact correctness, and tasks scored
by such checks are where open-weight models fall furthest behind. On Scene from Video, no object placed by an
open-weight model remains stable in simulation, against 70.7% for Claude Opus 5.5.
The difference between the best closed-source and the best open-weight model is largest on tasks whose
evaluator applies such a check: whether a robot task succeeds in simulation (RoboCasa, 50.0 points; RoboLab,
40.3; RoboDojo, 37.3), whether the output grid is exactly right (ARC-AGI-2, 49.2) or a level is cleared (ARC-
AGI-3, 43.3; on ARC-AGI-1, whose puzzles the starter solutions already nearly solve, it is 8.8), and whether a
reconstructed scene is physically and geometrically consistent (Scene from Video, 36.4; Scene from Photo,
26.5). It is smallest on tasks scored by a judge or with a tolerance: Video Quality (0.1), Mass (0.8), Multi-turn
Editing (1.0), Video Generation (2.5), and Image Editing (4.2). The component scores of the two scene tasks
show how plausible outputs fail these checks (Tables 29 and 30). On Scene from Photo, the renderings of
the open-weight scenes resemble the photo, with an Appearance score of up to 81.8, but the furniture is not
recovered as complete and correctly placed objects: their Objects score is at most 16.5, against 62.5 for both
closed-source models. On Scene from Video, the Physics score, the fraction of objects that remain stable in
simulation, is 0.0 for all four open-weight models, against 70.7 for Claude Opus 5.5 and 37.7 for GPT-6 Astra.
The two LIBERO tasks are the exceptions among the robot tasks: the best open-weight model matches the best
closed-source model on LIBERO-MAX (45.8) and trails it by 10.0 points on LIBERO-PRO.

6
Conclusion
We introduced Flex-RSI, a benchmark that measures whether a frontier agent can improve both its task solution
and its own harness within a fixed budget, with every run starting from the same general-purpose harness
and every final solution scored on a hidden test split. Across 24 tasks and 18 models, three findings emerge.
Self-improvement raises the score most on tasks in which the agent builds its own solution, and on these tasks
models that start from the same program end far apart, whereas on tasks whose solution is a single answer
the starter solution already scores high and the models end about as far apart as they began. Open-weight
models improve when the evaluator scores each episode on a scale, but barely improve when it records only
success or failure. The largest gaps between closed-source and open-weight models arise where the evaluator
checks physical stability or exact correctness, even when the outputs look plausible. Paired runs further show
that editing the harness contributes to the improvement, most where the unchanged starting harness makes
little progress, whereas a harness built by a stronger model raised the score of a weaker model on only two of
four tasks.
The improvement measured here is mostly at the level of the harness. The evaluated model’s weights never
change, and on most tasks no model is trained; on some, the solution may train or fine-tune helper models on the
development split. Flex-RSI therefore does not measure a model that modifies its own parameters. Extending
the benchmark to weight-level self-improvement, and studying how it interacts with changes to the harness, is
left to future work. The leaderboard is maintained on the project page, https://flex-rsi.github.io.

RoboDojo
Purpose. Whether the agent can turn a general multimodal model into a closed-loop policy for a dual-arm
robot. No policy is trained: the model decides the motions at evaluation time, and the agent improves the
program that queries it.
Source and construction. Ten simulation tasks of RoboDojo (Chen et al., 2026d). Full credit (success) requires:
four bottles in a dustbin, using a handover between the hands; a garment folded with sleeves in and hem
aligned to the shoulders; digit tiles placed in the order forming the largest number; three tubes inserted upright
into a rack; all five own pieces placed in tic-tac-toe against a randomly playing opponent arm; a T-shaped
block pushed onto its pad without lifting; eight xylophone keys struck left to right with a mallet; the conveyor
object shown on a board placed in a lifted basket; four eggs in a holder with its lid closed; and five nesting
dolls lined up by size. Several tasks also require a return to the start pose. All layouts come from RoboDojo’s
evaluation seed 0: per task, validation takes the first five, test the next five, and development the rest, of which
a fixed panel of five is evaluated. Validation layouts of three tasks are those of Galbot Team et al. (2026). Tasks
with a randomized variant (fold clothes, largest number, Push-T, nesting dolls) use two standard and three
randomized layouts per split. The agent sees full development results, the validation score only, and no test
results.

and 0.15 rad per step. It may call the run’s model at most 80 times per episode and no other model; scripted
motions and its own inverse kinematics are allowed.

320 × 240, the robot state, and its notes from earlier calls.

Active Search
Purpose. Active visual search: a humanoid robot that sees part of a 360-degree scene turns its head to find a
named object (object search) or the direction to move in (path search), with as few movements as possible;
most targets start out of view.
Source and construction. H*Bench (Yu et al., 2026a) annotates search tasks on real panoramas of transport
hubs, shops and streets: an instruction, a target yaw range (and, for objects, a pitch range), and starting
directions and difficulty levels; its public benchmark split has 2,400 object-search and 1,600 path-search
episodes. Development and validation come from it: each task becomes one episode, with one start drawn by
a fixed seed from its annotated starts; each panorama yields at most one episode, so the splits share no scene;
both are stratified by search kind and difficulty in proportion to H*Bench. Development has 80 episodes (40
object, 40 path) and validation 100 (50 and 50). The test split uses the non-public in-the-wild panoramas

models. A target was annotated by clicking its center (objects, widened by ±30◦in yaw and ±20◦in pitch) or
the direction to move in (±10◦), with starts at multiples of 90◦; one start per task gives 47 episodes (33 object,
14 path) in 15 scenes. The agent sees the development episodes with their annotations and panoramas.
Setting. The solution receives the instruction and a 100-degree view (960 × 720 pixels), never the panorama.

the episode; no submission fails. As in H*Bench, success requires the submitted direction to lie in the target
ranges widened to at least the tolerances above. Up to 20 model calls per episode on average and open models
on one GPU are allowed; other H*Bench data are not.
Starter solution. The H*Bench baseline with its prompts: one model call per step on the whole conversation
(every view with the environment’s feedback), answered with one rotation or submission.

Purpose. Whether the agent can write, offline with a simulator, a control program for a long-horizon, contact-

Ego-Exo Memory
Purpose. Memory reasoning across two synchronized views of one activity. The evidence for an answer is
visible only at particular moments, often in one view, and the model sees few frames per call, so the solution
must choose which moments and views to show.
Source and construction. The questions are the Ego-Exo4D portion of EgoExoMem (Liu et al., 2026b): 764
human-verified four-option questions on 125 recordings of cooking and bicycle repair from Ego-Exo4D (Grau-
man et al., 2024). Each recording has an egocentric view from the glasses of the person performing the activity
(448 × 448 pixels) and an exocentric view from a fixed camera (796 × 448 pixels), synchronized at 30 frames
per second and lasting from 20 seconds to 23 minutes. The questions concern habitual location, instantaneous
position, resulting location, egocentric direction, object state, allocentric relation, and temporal order. All
recordings of one session (the same room and people) are assigned to one split, stratified by activity, with
about 35%, 25%, and 40% of the questions in the development, validation, and test splits. Each split then
keeps a fixed-seed sample stratified by activity and question type and spread over its videos: 80 questions on
38 videos, 80 on 33, and 120 on 47. The answer options are shuffled once and the videos are anonymized.
With each development question the agent also receives the annotated moments at which the answer is visible
and the view that shows it.
Setting. Given the question, both videos, and frames of both views at one frame per second, the solution
returns an option letter. It may call the model three times per question on average over a split, with at most
16 × 448 × 448 pixels per call; further calls are refused. It may run open-weight models, for example for
frame retrieval, on one GPU, has no network access during evaluation, and may not use the annotations of
EgoExoMem or Ego-Exo4D.
Starter solution. Shows the model eight evenly spaced frames of each view, each labeled with its view and
time and downscaled so that all sixteen fit the pixel limit, followed by the question, in a single call.
Metric. Accuracy over the questions of a split; a question without a valid letter counts as wrong. Chance is
0.25.
Prior work. EgoExoMem evaluates multimodal language models on 2.6K cross-view memory questions of eight
types. On the full benchmark, the best existing model reaches 55.3%, and the training-free frame selection
method E2-Select, which allocates the frame budget by relevance and samples frames per view with a 𝑘-DPP,
reaches 58.2% (Liu et al., 2026b).

3D Tracking
Purpose. Whether the agent can assemble a system that segments every object in a few widely separated
views of an indoor scene and gives each object the same identity in every view, from RGB images alone.
Without camera poses or depth, the views must be associated through appearance and implicit geometry: the
cross-view instance tracking that IGGT (Li et al., 2026) introduced as an evaluation of instance-grounded 3D

Source and construction. The validation and test clips are the 3D tracking benchmark released with IGGT,

consistent across views. Each clip has six to nine RGB views, resized to 592 × 448 pixels, typically with four to
eight annotated objects such as furniture, lamps, and decorations; walls, floors, and ceilings are not annotated.

are kept (visible in at least two views, at most a quarter of any view, and at least 0.1% of the clip’s pixels); a
clip with fewer than four is drawn again. The agent sees the development views with their instance maps.
Setting. The solution is a program that receives the views of one clip and returns one instance map per view,
with 0 for the background and identities consistent across views. It may use any publicly released pretrained
model or library and needs no training; it does not call the model being evaluated. It runs on one GPU without
network access, and a whole split must finish within 45 minutes.

Visual Search
Purpose. Whether the agent can build a visual search procedure around a model with a limited field of view:
questions concern small objects in photographs of 2K to 8K resolution, while each model call may contain at
most one megapixel, so the solution must decide where to look.
Source and construction. The questions come from V*Bench (Wu and Xie, 2024) (191 multiple-choice questions
on about 2K photographs: 115 on object attributes with four options, 76 on relative position with two) and
HR-Bench 8K (Wang et al., 2025a) (200 questions with four options on 151 photographs of about 8K resolution,

with a fixed seed). V*Bench is split by category and HR-Bench by photograph, so no photograph appears
in two splits. The test split has 140 questions (70 from each benchmark). Development and validation are
stratified subsets of 70 questions (33 and 37) and 80 questions (40 and 40). The agent sees the development
photographs, questions, answers, and, for V*Bench, the boxes of the queried objects.
Setting. The solution answers each question with an option letter. It may call the model being evaluated on
average at most five times per question, with at most 1024 × 1024 pixels per call in total; it may crop, resize,
and run open-weight models on one GPU, and may not use either benchmark beyond the development split.
Starter solution. Shows the model the whole photograph reduced to one megapixel with the question, in a
single call.
Metric. Accuracy over the questions of the split; a question without a valid letter counts as wrong. Accuracies
on V*Bench and HR-Bench 8K are reported separately. Chance accuracy on the validation split is 0.303.

Purpose. Whether the agent can turn a general multimodal model into a judge of perceptual video quality that

A.6 Infrastructure
VAE Decode
Purpose. Whether the agent can lower the latency of a fixed numerical computation, the video decoder of a

Protocol and harness
This appendix specifies the parts of the protocol that Sections 3.2 and 3.3 describe only in outline: the structure
of a round, the capabilities of the executor and the improver, the acceptance rule, the budget and the conditions
under which a run ends, and the measures that keep the evaluation intact. The rules are identical for all
models and tasks; the budget and two settings of the executor phase are fixed per task and listed in Table 10.
B.1 Round structure
A run begins by scoring the starter solution on the validation split, which gives the score against which the first
candidate is compared. Each round then proceeds in five steps: an executor phase that produces a candidate
solution; a check that reverts every change the executor made outside the files it may modify; the evaluation
of the candidate on the validation split; the acceptance decision of Equation (2); and an improver phase that
revises the harness.
The executor phase normally ends when the executor declares its work finished, and the solution in its final
state becomes the candidate. It ends earlier when one of the executor’s evaluations on the development split
exceeds the development score of the accepted solution by a fixed share of the distance between the starter’s
validation score and the validation score of a strong known solution (Table 10); the solution as it was in that
evaluation is then submitted. If the phase is interrupted before the executor finishes, because it reached its
call limit, its time limit, or the budget, the best state that the executor’s development evaluations showed to
exceed the accepted solution is submitted, and nothing is submitted if there is none. A round that leaves the
solution unchanged does not use a validation evaluation.
B.2 Executor
The executor works in a copy of the run’s repository. Its tools allow it to run shell commands; to read, write,
and edit files; to view images; to run experiments on the compute allocated to the task, including GPUs where
the task provides them; to score the solution, or several variants of it at once, on the development split; and
to query the remaining budget. It may write only to the solution, to a notes file that persists across rounds
and is shared with the improver, and to a scratch directory that is emptied before each round. It observes
development scores directly and the validation scores of earlier rounds through the run records, but it has
no access to the validation or test data or to the evaluator. The starting harness allows 60 model calls per
executor phase. Independently of the harness, the protocol stops a phase after the number of model calls given
in Table 10 or after 180 minutes (600 minutes on the ARC tasks), and an experiment after 240 minutes.
B.3 Improver
After each round, the improver receives the executor’s transcript, its experiments and notes, and the scores of
all rounds so far. It may modify the executor’s prompts, settings, control loop, and tools, as well as its own
prompts, settings, acceptance rule, and a file of lessons that it maintains across rounds. A single improver
phase may change at most 400 lines of the harness; a larger change is reverted in full. Every four rounds, the
improver additionally reviews its own process: which kinds of harness changes were followed by accepted
candidates and which by stalls, whether the acceptance rule is too strict or too permissive, and which lessons
to record. Changes to the harness take effect from the following round. In the frozen-harness runs of Table 8,
the improver is not run.

B.4 Acceptance rule
The margin 𝛿in Equation (2) is determined by the acceptance rule, which is part of the improver’s harness. In
the starting harness, a candidate is accepted if its validation score exceeds that of the accepted solution by
more than 2𝜎, where 𝜎is the standard deviation of the starter’s validation score over repeated evaluations
(Table 10). On tasks for which 𝜎was not measured, it is set to zero, and any improvement on the validation
split is accepted. Because the rule belongs to the harness, the improver may revise it. If the rule fails to return
a decision, and in every run with a frozen harness, the starting rule 𝛿= 2𝜎is applied.
B.5 Budget and termination
A run ends at the first of the following events: the wall-clock time, GPU hours, tokens (input, including cached
input, and output), or cost in dollars reach the task’s limit; the number of validation evaluations reaches its
limit; the round cap is reached; or five consecutive rounds produce neither a candidate nor a change to the
harness, a rule that is not applied on LIBERO-MAX and LIBERO-PRO. The budget is checked before each model
call, experiment, and development evaluation and is charged when each of them ends, so that calls running
concurrently can exceed a limit by at most their combined cost. A submitted candidate is evaluated on the
validation split even when the budget is exhausted. Model calls that the evaluator makes on the validation and
test splits, for tasks whose solution calls the model, are recorded but not charged. Periods during which the
model provider is unavailable are not charged either, and a phase interrupted by such an outage is repeated.
After the run, the final solution and every accepted solution are scored on the test split (Section 3.3).

B.6 Isolation and integrity
After every phase, any change outside the paths that the phase may modify, including changes made through
shell commands, is reverted and recorded as a violation; the evaluator, the data splits, and the protocol
therefore cannot be altered by the agent. Files larger than 5 MB are excluded from the run’s repository. If a
phase crashes in two consecutive rounds, the harness is restored to the last version that completed a round
without a crash. When a phase ends, all processes it started are terminated, so that no process left running
can modify the solution afterwards. All model calls, experiments, and development evaluations are routed
through the benchmark, which charges them to the budget and records them.
