Calibrated Early Termination with Bounded
Optimality Gaps for Constrained Vector Search

1Fudan University, Shanghai, China

Abstract—Vector search over learned embeddings is the re-
trieval layer of recommendation and retrieval-augmented gen-
eration, and many applications cap how many answer items
one category may contribute. Graph indexes answer a query by
best-first traversal, and where it stops determines both its cost
and whether its answer is exact. We study early termination
for such constrained k-nearest-neighbor (k-NN) queries, with
plain k-NN as a special case, and seek the cheapest stopping
rule whose answer is exact with probability at least 1 −ε.
Learned early-termination methods instead target average recall,
which does not guarantee exact answers. Multiple testing can
calibrate any parameterized stopping rule to our target, but
it cannot tell how much a better rule with the same risk and
observations would save. Answering this needs a lower bound
on the optimum of a rule family too large to enumerate, valid
for the query population rather than only for the sample. Our
key idea is to declare the observations a rule may use and to
consider every rule monotone in them. Risk-constrained stopping
then becomes a linear program over an order polytope, whose
Lagrangian minimizers form a nested chain of staircase rules
computed exactly by dynamic programming. Fixed-sequence
binomial tests along this chain calibrate our rule, CaST, whose
failure probability is at most ε with confidence 1−δ. Lagrangian
weak duality bounds the optimum of the declared class from
below at all risk levels at once, without a union bound over the
rules. The ratio of CaST’s cost to this bound limits its distance
from the best rule of the class. On two datasets of one million
items each, CaST replayed on logged searches costs at most
1.132× the class optimum for risk levels from 0.03 to 0.10, at
95% joint confidence per setting. Larger savings thus need a rule
outside the class. At a common risk target on held-out queries,
CaST is faster than every other calibrated rule we time, which
take 4.8% to 45.5% more mean time.
Index Terms—Vector search, approximate nearest neighbor

I. INTRODUCTION
Vector search over learned embeddings underlies recom-
mendation and retrieval-augmented generation. Graph indexes
such as HNSW [1] and DiskANN [2] answer a query by
best-first traversal, iteratively exploring points based on their
proximity to the query. However, because these graphs are
sparsified heuristically and the search terminates once its
bounded candidate list no longer improves, termination itself
provides no guarantee that the exact answer has been found.
Many applications also constrain the answer. For example, a
product search should not return too many variants from the
same category, and a retrieval pipeline should not draw too

a set of categories and the answer must admit an assignment of
each answer item to one of its categories such that no category
receives more than κ items [3], [4]. The greedy algorithm

selecting an item whenever the selected items, including the

Where the search stops thus determines the trade-off be-
tween search efficiency and answer correctness. Our objec-
tive is a stopping rule that minimizes the expected search
cost while ensuring that, for a query drawn from the query
distribution, the returned answer is exact with probability at
least 1 −ε, i.e., with risk at most ε. A fixed visit budget is
inefficient because the number of graph visits required to reach
the exact answer, which we call the query’s completion time,
varies substantially across queries. On an HNSW graph for the
Amazon (arXiv) dataset, the budget calibrated to risk ε = 0.05
is 6,781 (5,978) visits, i.e., 7.1× (5.8×) the median completion
time over 10,000 evaluation queries. Learned early-termination
methods, such as LAET [5] and DARTH [6], instead predict

when to stop, but they target average recall, and high average
recall does not guarantee a high probability of returning
the exact constrained answer. On the same graphs, DARTH,
configured for a target average recall of 0.95, returns the exact
constrained answer for only 71% (68%) of queries.
Finding the minimum-cost stopping rule and quantifying
how close a given rule is to optimal pose three challenges.
First, the stopping time is data-dependent: the rule decides
when to stop based on the search trajectory observed so far.
Consequently, guarantees established at individual prediction
points do not necessarily hold at the selected stopping time.
Second, the completion time is unknown at run time. More-
over, under the cap of 215=32,768 visits, between 0.10% and
0.58% of the Amazon and arXiv queries, depending on the
dataset and graph, never complete, so no rule can return their
exact answer. Third, quantifying the optimality gap requires a
lower bound on the minimum expected cost over an enormous
class of rules, which contains approximately e266 deterministic
rules in our setting. The lower bound must hold for the
underlying query population, rather than merely for the finite
evaluation sample drawn from it.
Existing methods provide partial solutions to the first
challenge but do not address the optimality-gap problem.
LAET [5], DARTH [6] and DARTH+ [7] learn stopping
predictors and tune them using validation queries; DARTH+
reports recall coverage at individual prediction points, but this
coverage does not necessarily extend to the data-dependent
stopping point. ConANN [8] and Learn-then-Test [9] control
the risk of a selected parameter. Such calibration resolves
the first challenge for any parameterized rule, and for a fair
comparison we apply it to every baseline in our experiments.
None of these methods provides a lower bound on the min-
imum achievable expected cost: a calibrated rule meets the
risk target, but how much cheaper a rule with the same
risk and observations could be remains unknown. The same
challenge of quantifying the optimality gap under statistical
risk constraints arises in calibrated early-exit methods for
language models and classifiers [10], [11].
Our key idea is to make this question well posed by fixing
what a stopping rule may observe and restricting it to a natural
monotone class. At each checkpoint, our engine exposes a
stopping interface: the running minimum of a learned score
(low when the answer is likely complete), discretized into
buckets, together with a flag recording whether k answer
items have been selected. A rule is monotone if, whenever
it stops on an observation, it also stops on every observation
that indicates completion more strongly. This class contains
the common threshold, cap, and budget rules aligned to the
interface. With randomized rules allowed, risk-constrained
stopping over the class becomes a linear program over an
order polytope, whose Lagrangian minimizers form a nested
chain of deterministic staircase rules, i.e., rules whose score
threshold varies monotonically across checkpoints.
This chain addresses all three challenges. First, fixed-
sequence tests along the chain count failures at the actual
stopping point, and thus bound the failure probability of the
returned answer despite the adaptive stopping time. Second,
the completion time enters only offline, through the labeled
samples, i.e., sample queries whose completion times are com-
puted from their exact answers, and the deployed rule reads
nothing but the interface. Third, the same linear program yields
a population lower bound on the class optimum via Lagrangian
weak duality and empirical-Bernstein bounds, without a union
bound over the rules of the class. Our technical novelty lies
in exploring the structure of the monotone class to obtain this
lower bound. The ratio of the calibrated cost to the lower
bound is a data-dependent optimality-gap bound within the
class, which holds end-to-end, from the fitted score to the
returned answer. At risk ε = 0.05 on one graph per dataset,
eight other rules calibrated to the same target spend 6.2% to
70.6% more visits than our calibrated staircase, which lies
within 1.087× of the lower bound with 87.5% confidence.
The bound covers only rules restricted to the interface: a
clairvoyant rule that knows each query’s completion time
is 2.8× to 3.3× cheaper than the empirical class optimum
on the evaluation queries, which upper-bounds what richer
observations could gain at these checkpoints. We evaluate in-
memory search on a single machine with unfiltered traversal
for queries drawn i.i.d. from the distribution of the labeled
samples.
Our contributions are as follows.
• We show that risk-constrained early termination over a
declared interface is a linear program over an order poly-
tope, whose optima mix at most two nested staircases.
Its Lagrangian minimizers form a nested chain computed
exactly by dynamic programming in O(J(B + 1)2) time
per multiplier.
• We calibrate the chain into our rule, CaST, by fixed-
sequence exact binomial tests, which bound the failure
probability of the returned answer by ε with confidence
1 −δ. We also give a lower bound on the class-optimal
cost that holds simultaneously for all risk levels. Together
they yield an end-to-end optimality-gap bound within the
declared class.
• On two datasets of one million items each, CaST costs at
most 1.132 times the class optimum at risk levels from
0.03 to 0.10, at confidence 95%, and this near-optimality
carries over to most further sample splits, workloads and
indexes. At a common risk target of 0.05 on held-out
queries, CaST is faster than every other calibrated rule
we time, which take 4.8% to 45.5% more mean time.
II. RELATED WORK
a) Early termination for vector search: LAET [5] pre-
dicts, after a fixed amount of search, how much further search
a query needs and scales the prediction by a tuned factor,
and Tao [12] predicts it from static features of the query.
DARTH [6] predicts the current recall of an HNSW search
with gradient-boosted trees, calls the predictor at adaptive
intervals, and stops once the prediction reaches a declared tar-
get. DARTH+ [7] replaces the point prediction by a quantile-
regression lower bound on recall, whose coverage at each


prediction point is measured on test queries. Ada-ef [13] sets
the list size of HNSW per query from a model of the distances
between the query and the data, so that a target recall is
met. Patience in Proximity [14] stops once the top-k answer
has not changed for a number of steps. Terminus [15] stops
a disk-based search once recent I/Os add little utility, and
E2E [16] learns when to terminate a filtered search. These
methods choose their stopping parameter by its average effect
on validation queries. Adaptive Beam Search [17] stops on a
distance-based condition that provably returns an approximate
nearest neighbor on navigable graphs, which bounds the
distance of the answer rather than how often it is incomplete.
b) Guarantees for approximate search: ConANN [8]
uses conformal risk control [18] to choose how many IVF
clusters each query probes, so that the expected false-negative
rate stays below a target. Li et al. [19] test a finished HNSW
search and fall back to an exact recovery, Auncel [20] meets
a per-query error bound through an error profile, and AD-
Sampling [21] ends distance computations by hypothesis tests.
These methods control the quality of the result, and our work
bounds the cost of the calibrated rule relative to the best rule
of a declared class at the same risk.
c) Risk control and constrained stochastic programs:
Risk-controlling prediction sets [22] and Learn-then-Test [9]
control the risk of a selected parameter, and fixed-sequence
testing is one of the instruments of the latter. Calibrated
early exit applies such tests to the depth at which a language
model or a classifier stops [10], [11]. Sample approximations
of chance-constrained programs yield feasible solutions and
statistical bounds on the optimal value [23], [24], from which
Monte Carlo bounding estimates optimality gaps [25]. Our
calibration and lower bound are of these kinds, and what
we add is structure: an order polytope whose Lagrangian
minimizers form a nested chain, which yields a lower bound
along the whole cost-risk frontier.
d) Diverse and constrained nearest-neighbor search:
Query result diversification trades relevance against mutual
dissimilarity [26]. Diverse similarity search caps the answer
items per category, with graph indexes for one category per
item [27] or color bucketing over black-box indexes [28]. With
several categories per item, capping the number of members
of each category gives a family that is not a matroid in
general, and finding a feasible answer of a given size is NP-
hard [29]. The assignment constraint we use keeps the matroid
structure [3], [4]. For filtered vector search, the single-category
case of our constraint, Filtered-DiskANN [30], NHQ [31]
and ACORN [32] design predicate-aware graph indexes and
searches. We take the search as given and decide when it stops,
so our results apply to any search that visits items one at a
time and maintains their greedy answer.
e) Early stopping in query processing and optimal stop-
ping: Instance-optimal aggregation stops a top-k scan over
sorted lists [33], probabilistic pruning drops candidates that
are unlikely to qualify [34], and PAC nearest-neighbor queries
stop on the distance distribution of the query [35]. A graph
search offers neither a proof that the answer is final nor
a distance model, so our rule is calibrated on samples. In
optimal stopping, martingale duality bounds the best stopping
rule for an unconstrained objective by simulation [36], [37],
and our bound covers a risk-constrained objective over the
declared class with a finite-sample guarantee. Mixing two
nested staircases is the analogue for one risk constraint.
III. PROBLEM FORMULATION
A. Constrained nearest neighbors and graph search
Let V = {1, . . . , N} be a set of items. Each item v has
an embedding xv ∈Rd of unit norm and a set of categories
c(v) ⊆C, which may be empty. A query q ∈Rd ranks the
items by the distance d(q, xv) = 1 −⟨q, xv⟩, with ties broken
by item index. Capacities κ : C →Z≥1 bound how many
answer slots each category may fill.
Definition 1 (Feasible answer). A set S ⊆V is feasible if
there is an assignment ϕ : S →C with ϕ(v) ∈c(v) for every
v ∈S and |ϕ−1(γ)| ≤κ(γ) for every γ ∈C.
Each answer item thus represents one of its categories, and
no category is represented more than κ times. For example,
with κ ≡1, two items that both carry the categories {γ1, γ2}
may both be returned, one representing γ1 and the other γ2.
This is the semantics of a result page with one section per cat-
egory, where each item appears in one section and each section
shows at most κ items. The feasible sets are the independent
sets of a transversal matroid [38]. For A ⊆V, let Greedy(A)
scan A in increasing distance and add an item when fewer than
k items are kept and adding it preserves feasibility. The exact
constrained answer is S⋆(q) = Greedy(V), and we assume
that k does not exceed the rank of the matroid, which holds
in all our settings. The feasible sets of at most k items form
a matroid, so S⋆(q) is a feasible set of k items with the least
total distance [38], made unique by the order of the scan. Two
common queries are special cases. Unconstrained k-NN gives
every item one shared category with capacity k. Attribute-
filtered k-NN [30], [32] gives the items that pass the filter one
shared category with capacity k and the others none.
A graph search visits items one at a time by a best-first
traversal of a proximity graph from an entry point. Each visit
computes one distance. Let At be the set of items visited
after t visits. The search maintains Greedy(At) exactly as
visits arrive, and stopping after t visits returns it. Visits arrive
out of distance order, so a new item may displace a farther
member of the answer. Our engine keeps the answer with one
assignment and decides each new item by an alternating-path
search between answer items and category slots, and its cost
is part of every reported latency. Whether the returned answer
is exact then depends only on which items have been visited,
as the following lemma shows. It lets us express the failure of
a stopping rule through a single completion time.
Lemma 1. If S⋆(q) ⊆A, then Greedy(A) = S⋆(q).
Every item outside A is rejected by the scan over V, because
every item that scan keeps belongs to S⋆(q) ⊆A. By induction
over the distance order, both scans therefore hold the same kept

B. Checkpoints and cost
A stopping rule acts at J checkpoints, defined by a grid of
visit counts g0 < g1 < · · · < gJ−1. An expansion removes
the closest unexpanded candidate from the list and visits its
unvisited neighbors. Checkpoint j is the first expansion at
which the number of visits reaches gj, and cj denotes the
number of visits at that moment. If the search ends before
it reaches gj, checkpoint j and all later ones are placed at
its end, with cj equal to the final number of visits. One
expansion adds at most ∆visits, where ∆is the maximum
out-degree of the graph (∆= 64 for our graphs). Hence
0 < c0 ≤· · · ≤cJ−1 ≤H = gJ−1 + ∆on every trajectory,
and H bounds the cost of every rule. Stopping at checkpoint
j fails exactly when cj < X(q).
C. Declared interface and monotone rules
A rule observes the search only through a declared interface.
A score model, fit once on separate queries, maps the trajectory
up to checkpoint j to a score sj ∈[0, 1], where a low score in-
dicates that the answer is likely complete. Let mj = mini≤j si
be its running minimum. Fixed edges e1 < · · · < eB−1 map
mj to the bucket bj = |{i : ei ≤mj}| ∈{0, . . . , B −1},
with the same edges at every checkpoint. The flag aj ∈{0, 1}
records whether the current answer holds k items. The rule
observes zj = (bj, aj) ∈Z = {0, . . . , B −1} × {0, 1}.
Along every trajectory, bj is nonincreasing in j and aj is
nondecreasing in j. The bucket cannot rise because mj is
a running minimum and the edges are fixed. The flag cannot
fall because Greedy(At) holds min(k, r(At)) items, where r
is the matroid rank, which never decreases as items are visited.

D. Samples and confidence
We use five disjoint samples of queries, each consisting of
independent draws from the query distribution. FIT trains the
score model and sets the bucket edges, SEL fits the candidate
rules, CAL calibrates them, and EVAL computes the bounds.
HOLD, which no fitting or calibration step reads, serves a
final check and the timing. Probability statements are over
the draws of CAL and EVAL and over the stopping draws,
conditional on FIT and SEL. Refitting the score or the chain
therefore requires new CAL and EVAL samples. Each setting,
a dataset searched on one graph, carries its own statements
at a joint level 1 −αL −αU −δ, and statements of different
settings are not joint. Here δ, αU and αL are the error levels
of the calibration, of the upper bound on cost and of the lower
bound on the optimum. Fig. 2 summarizes the approach.

nothing so far prevents its optimum from being fractional in
every coordinate. The next theorem shows that the region of
monotone rules, before the risk constraint is imposed, is an
order polytope [39], and this fixes the form of optimal rules.
It justifies the staircase form of the deployed rule and shows
why exhaustive search over deterministic rules is out of reach.

A. Experiments Setup
1) Datasets: We use four public corpora (Table I). Amazon
is the Sports and Outdoors category of Amazon Reviews
2023 [46], where the categories of an item are the second-level
node and the leaf of its category path. arXiv is a metadata
snapshot,1 where the categories are the subject classes of
a paper. Both are embedded with all-MiniLM-L6-v2 [47].2
YFCC-10M is the filtered-search dataset of the NeurIPS’23
Big-ANN competition [48], with CLIP [49] embeddings of
YFCC100M images [50]. Its categories are the 1,000 most
frequent tags, and we also use its 200,000 official queries
without their tag predicates. YouTube-8M [51] holds video
features, with machine-generated entity labels as categories.
The main workload asks for k = 10 items with capacity
κ ≡1 on Amazon and arXiv. There the constrained answer
differs from the unconstrained top 10 for 98.6% and 98.2%
of queries, against about 70% on YouTube-8M and 4.4% on
YFCC-10M. For Amazon and arXiv, one random permutation
of the pool selects the base, 2,500 FIT queries and 200,000 fur-
ther queries, which are split at random into SEL, CAL, EVAL
and HOLD. We model the pool as independent draws from
an item distribution, the query distribution of our guarantees,
so the base and the five samples are independent samples of
it. Exact constrained answers are computed by a full scan.
Between 0.10% and 0.58% of EVAL queries of the main
workload do not complete within 32,768 visits.
Thirteen further settings test the breadth of the method.
On graph A of Amazon and arXiv, they raise the capacity
to κ ≡2, drop the constraint, which gives plain k-NN, or
query with only the titles of the 200,000 further items, split
again at random. Two run the main workload on a Vamana
graph of each dataset. On YFCC-10M and YouTube-8M, they
ask constrained and unconstrained queries with k = 10, and
YFCC-10M also constrained official queries. Each workload
has its own exact answers, logs and samples of the same sizes,
without HOLD for the title settings, YFCC-10M and YouTube-
8M.
2) Metrics: Cost is the mean number of bottom-layer visits.
The upper-layer descent adds about 170 distances per query
to every rule, and latency includes it. Risk is the fraction
of queries whose returned answer differs from the exact
1https://huggingface.co/datasets/librarian-bots/arxiv-metadata-snapshot
2https://huggingface.co/sentence-transformers/all-MiniLM-L6-v2
constrained answer, or, for a recall target ρ, holds fewer than
⌈ρk⌉of its members. Latency is the mean over queries of the
median wall-clock time of five passes, including the evaluation
of features and models. The optimality-gap bound is the ratio
Um/L(εm), and 1 means optimal within the class.
3) Compared methods: The compared rules fall into four
groups, all calibrated by the same test on CAL.
The first group reads the same seven features on the same
FIT queries, which isolates the value of the staircase shape.
Level stops when the running minimum of the score falls
below a threshold, the stopping principle of DARTH applied
to our score, and Level+cap adds a visit cap chosen on SEL.
Both read the score at a finer resolution than zj. LAET-style
regresses the logarithm of the completion time at a checkpoint
chosen on SEL, as LAET does, and stops at a multiple of the
prediction. Fixed uses one visit budget.
The second group ports DARTH and DARTH+ [7] into
our engine, with recall measured against the exact constrained
answer. DARTH uses the authors’ eleven answer features, re-
gressor settings and adaptive intervals, trained on 10,000 SEL
queries. Its recall target is calibrated to 0.9942 on Amazon and
0.9939 on arXiv, above every target its authors evaluate. At
the literal targets 0.90 and 0.95, the port reaches mean recalls
of 0.908 and 0.956 to 0.959, the average-recall goal DARTH
is designed for. DARTH+ adds eight query statistics and stops
on a learned lower quantile of the recall, with its level chosen
on unused SEL queries. Its code is not public, so we port it
from the paper. We check only that the engine evaluates the
fitted forests as scikit-learn does.
The third group tunes the search. List size runs the traversal
with a list of ef entries, replayed from the logged traversal as
the top-ef prefix of the longer list. On the 160,000 CAL and
EVAL queries of each main setting, native runs at the selected
list size differ from the replay in the visits of at most two
queries and the exactness of two. Post-filtering the final list
of the same traversal, the usual two-stage approach, is exact
only when List size is, by Lemma 1. Distance-adaptive adapts
Adaptive Beam Search [17]: it stops once the answer is full
and the next candidate is farther than 1 + γ times the k-th
distance of the answer. They are calibrated over 46 list sizes
from 8,192 down to 10 and over 129 values of γ from 2 down
to 0, 246 on YFCC-10M. Ada-ef [13] looks up a list size from
a Gaussian model of the distances of the query, with success
meaning the exact answer. Patience [14] stops once the answer
is full and unchanged for a calibrated number of visits.
The fourth group holds richer learned policies, which test
whether the restriction to MZ costs much. By Longstaff-
Schwartz style backward induction [52], gradient-boosted
models estimate the further cost of continuing and the chance
that it turns a failure into an exact answer. A policy stops when
the former exceeds λ′ times the latter. The fully fitted policy
reads the seven features, the score and its running minimum
on all of SEL. The cross-fitted policy fits each half of SEL on
the other half, and the declared-state policy reads only zj.

Deployed rule. Calibration and the bounds use outcomes
computed offline from logged trajectories. On 10,000 EVAL
queries of Amazon and arXiv, the online stopping point of

queries, and exactness always agrees. For our rule at ε = 0.05
on graph A, under either calibration, the stopping point differs
on 1 of the 30,000 EVAL and HOLD queries of Amazon and

risk at most ε+p and cost at most Um+Hp. At 0.05 this raises

arXiv, the distance-adaptive rule, which was designed for plain
k-NN, is 2.7% and 0.2% cheaper at a similar failure rate.
On YFCC-10M, 3.9% of the queries do not complete within
32,768 visits, so we evaluate the levels from 0.05 to 0.10 only.

share, and it falls to 1.124 and 1.125 at 0.10. On 10,000 EVAL
queries of YFCC-10M with tags, the first group takes 1.2% to
22.7% more time than our rule. The list size takes 12.1% less,
at 0.18 µs per visit against our 0.24 µs on the list of 8,192.
With a list of 2,048, chosen among four sizes without reading
CAL and with both bounds split over the four, our staircase is
within 1% of the list size in time. Its bound is 1.402 at 0.05
and 1.129 at 0.10. On the main workload, the same change
makes our rule 1.2% and 2.2% slower on HOLD. With k = 50,
15.8% and 30.2% of the queries of Amazon and arXiv do not
complete within the horizon, above every evaluated risk level.
c) Recall target: Under the recall target ρ = 0.9, our
rule is again the cheapest, and every other rule of Table II
is significantly costlier at ε = 0.05. By Lemma 2, the pipeline
applies with the completion time X0.9 and a score model
retrained on the new label. On the main workload, the ratio
stays at most 1.106 at every level from 0.03 to 0.10, and at 0.05
the staircase needs less than half of its exact-answer visits. On
graph A, DARTH and DARTH+, which predict recall, spend
1.23 to 1.60 times our visits under the same test.
d) Cost and latency at a common risk target: At ε =
0.05, our rule has the lowest mean latency in every timing
window. On the 20,000 HOLD queries, every other calibrated
rule takes significantly more mean time than ours, by 4.8%
to 45.5% (Table III). The saving in visits carries over to end-
to-end time, and the stopping check of the staircase, score
model included, takes 7.5% and 7.1% of its query time. At 16
threads, our throughput over that of each rule of the third
window is 1.07 to 1.66, within 0.06 of the visit ratio, so
the saving holds under load. These savings are in the mean.
The distance-adaptive rule and, on Amazon, Level+cap have a
lower median. In the third window, our 99th percentile is 3,423
and 3,417 µs, above the list size (2,670 and 1,811), Patience
(3,033 and 2,682) and, on arXiv, Level+cap (2,449).
In visits, our rule is the cheapest at every level on graph
A (Fig. 4). On graph B, the six rules of Table II calibrated the
same way spend at least 2.5% more visits at every level from
0.02 upward, so the advantage does not hinge on the chosen
risk level. Cheaper rules appear only at 0.01 on Amazon graph
B, where the distance-adaptive rule spends 0.80 times our
visits and Level and Level+cap 0.84 times. On graph A, the
gap to Fixed is largest at 0.01. One budget for all queries must
reach the (1 −ε)-quantile of the completion time, while the
staircase stops each query by its own progress.
The saving does not come from failing more often.
Interpolated to equal EVAL failure rates from 0.03 to 0.08,
the first group still spends 1.04 to 1.68 times our visits and
the distance-adaptive rule 1.08 to 1.44 times. Patience, swept
over its parameter, spends 1.17 to 1.40 times, and DARTH
and DARTH+ on graph A 1.28 to 1.63 times. On all EVAL
queries, Patience and DARTH+ spend 1.32 to 1.52 times our
visits wherever we run them, each significantly more. Ada-ef
passes the test only with its table built on all of SEL, at 1.49
to 1.81 times our visits.
The staircase shape explains part of our saving over
Level+cap. At ε = 0.05 on Amazon, the calibrated staircase
stops only in buckets up to 20 until 702 visits and then relaxes
its threshold step by step. On arXiv it never stops before 1,318
visits. Level keeps one threshold for the whole search and
Level+cap adds a single cap, so neither can follow such a
profile. A threshold curve of the class with an offset, a break
point, a slope, a flag gap and a cap can follow it. Fit on SEL
and calibrated by the same test, it spends 0.976 to 1.074 times
our visits over ten random splits per setting, at failure rates
within 0.3 points. Its median per setting is 1.012 to 1.053, and
it recovers a median of 41% to 85% of the gap to Level+cap.
The rest of this gap comes from long queries, where the
thresholds of the chain rise faster than the single slope of the
curve. On HOLD, the curve of the reported split takes 5.8%
and 6.1% more mean time than ours, within 0.004 of its visit
ratio. The reported split favors our rule only on Amazon graph
A, where its EVAL visit ratio of 1.059 exceeds all ten.

f) Query shift and index updates: When a query shift
or an index update breaks the risk target, recalibration on
new CAL queries restores it at every level where the chain
returns a rule. The guarantees hold for queries drawn like the
samples, and text queries can follow another distribution [53].
In Fig. 5, we query graph A with only the titles of the CAL
items and of 40,000 EVAL items, while the base items embed
their full text. We also insert 1% or 5% new items in hnswlib,
or replace as many base items in place. Title queries complete
later, and replacement prunes the bottom layer, whose mean
degree falls by 10% and 25%. The rules calibrated before these
changes thus stop too early, and a one-sided exact binomial
test at level 0.05/9 rejects the target at 43 of the 54 levels of
the title queries and the two replacements. Inserts leave the
rules within the target.
Recalibration uses 40,000 CAL queries labeled and logged
after the change, and the chain returns no rule only for title
queries at 0.01. The guarantee needs only this step, since the
test holds for any candidate sequence fixed before CAL is
drawn. The recalibrated rule spends 0.92 to 1.03 times the vis-
its of a full refit on FIT, SEL and CAL samples logged on the
updated index, so the old chain stays efficient. Without labels,
a Kolmogorov-Smirnov distance on the stopping checkpoints
of 1,000 queries, thresholded for 5% false alarms, flags 54%
to 100% of the title and replacement batches. It does not test
the risk, so an alarm still calls for new labeled CAL queries.
The official queries of YFCC-10M give a second shift,
where the constraint changes the answer of 1.6% of the
queries. Rules calibrated on held-out queries keep the target
at all five levels on the official EVAL queries at 2.0% to 2.8%
higher cost. With all samples from the official queries, the
ratio is 1.177 at 0.05 and 1.084 at 0.10.
g) Sample size and labeling cost: Labels are cheap, and
a quarter of the CAL queries costs at most 5.6% more
visits. Per 10,000 sampled queries over one million items, the
exact answers and logged trajectories take under 5 seconds on
one GPU and 50 to 90 seconds on one 32-core server. With
10,000 instead of 40,000 CAL queries, the rule at ε = 0.05
spends 2.3% to 5.6% more visits, and with 30,000 instead of
120,000 EVAL queries its ratio grows by 0.036 to 0.044. A
deployment that needs no bound uses only FIT, SEL and CAL,
62,500 queries.
2) Ablation study:
a) Richer learned policies: The strongest richer policy,
the fully fitted one, is at most 0.5% cheaper than our
rule and at most 5.4% costlier, with a median of 1.6%
more over four settings and seven risk levels. These policies
and ours fit the same SEL queries, are calibrated on one half
of CAL at level δ/M and are compared on the other half.
Per setting, the cross-fitted and declared-state policies cost
a median of 1.6% to 3.5% and 1.2% to 2.8% more. The
advantage of the class is consistent with sample efficiency.
When SEL shrinks from 20,000 to 5,000 queries, the out-of-
sample Lagrangian value of the class changes by at most 1%.
Over the same change, the gap of the cross-fitted policy to the
class in this value, before any calibration, grows from between
5.6% and 7.2% to between 18.6% and 26.7%.
b) Interface and lower bound: The results do not hinge
on a tuned interface, and bounding all risk levels at once
loses at most 0.4%. On graph A we vary the three parameters
of the interface, with 40, 60 or 200 buckets, J = 50 or 25,
or a logistic-regression score. The rule calibrated at ε = 0.05
then costs between 0.8% less and 2.5% more than with the
main interface, and the ratio stays at most 1.134 at every level
from 0.03 to 0.10. With the checkpoint and the flag alone, the
empirical class optimum at 0.05 is 58% and 51% higher on
Amazon and arXiv, yet its bound of 1.098 and 1.099 is about
as small as that of the main interface. The bound therefore
measures what remains within a class, and only the cost ranks
classes. From 0.03 to 0.10, a lower bound for one risk level
only lies within 0.4% of the Lagrangian bound for all levels.
VII. CONCLUSION
Declaring the interface of a stopping rule turns early termi-
nation into a problem with structure. Over a monotone class,
risk-constrained stopping is a linear program over an order
polytope, and its Lagrangian minimizers form a nested chain
of staircases that dynamic programming computes exactly.
This structure yields both a calibrated rule and a lower bound
on the class optimum, and their ratio is a data-dependent
optimality-gap bound within the declared class. In our experi-
ments, the rule replayed on logged searches stays within 1.132
of the class optimum at the risk levels 0.03 to 0.08 and 0.10,
at confidence 95%. Larger savings thus need a rule outside the
class. At a common risk target, our rule also has a lower mean
latency than every other calibrated rule we time. We measure
in-memory search on one machine with unfiltered traversal
and fixed k and capacities. The guarantees hold for queries
drawn like the samples, so a query shift or an index update
calls for recalibration.
