## ABSTRACT 

Traditional long-horizon LLM agents often hand off context through full-history prompting, retaining all previous action–observation interactions. As execution proceeds, this history keeps growing, increasing inference cost and potentially degrading accuracy. Existing methods control this growth by preserving compact conclusions while removing earlier observations, but doing so can discard evidence that later becomes relevant. We introduce Semantic Context Handoff (SCH) ,whose central abstraction is state–evidence separation : a compact semantic state carries the agent’s current interpretation, while supporting action–observation evi-dence remains separately addressable and can re-enter context when later execution makes it relevant, without separate summarization or retrieval-model calls. Empirically, SCH improves long-horizon agent performance while substantially reducing context cost. In a controlled 150-task SWE-bench study, SCH’s evidence recovery improves Pass@1 from 80 .0% to 86 .9% at essentially unchanged token use and execution time. In the complete-system comparison, SCH achieves 86 .9% 

Pass@1 while using 43% fewer tokens and 41% less wall-clock time than the strongest baseline by task performance. Across models and benchmarks, SCH consistently reduces token use relative to full-history prompting while remaining competitive with or improving upon the evaluated baselines. Trajectory analy-sis confirms that SCH recovers evidence from substantially earlier parts of the interaction history rather than merely preserving a recent context window. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Long-horizon agents repeatedly interact with an environment and carry the resulting information into subsequent prompts. Retaining every action and observation preserves the complete execution history, but repeatedly resubmitting an ever-growing context is expensive and can bury currently relevant information (Liu et al., 2024). Compressing or summarizing the context controls this growth, yet creates a different failure mode: a compact conclusion may remain in context after the observations needed to reconsider that conclusion have been removed (Wu et al., 2025; Kang et al., 2026; Huang et al., 2026). The usefulness of historical evidence is not determined solely by chronological order. A software-engineering agent may inspect a file, form a root-cause hypothesis, modify the repository, and many steps later encounter a failed verification that makes the original inspection relevant again. A recent-window strategy may already have discarded that observation, while an irreversible summary may preserve only the resulting hypothesis. This creates two retention requirements: the agent’s current understanding should remain compact and continuously available, while detailed historical observations need only remain recoverable when later execution makes them relevant again. Full-history prompting preserves both at growing context cost, whereas irreversible compression can sacrifice recoverability. We therefore separate two questions: what semantic information should remain continuously visible, and what historical evidence should remain recoverable when it becomes relevant again? 

We introduce Semantic Context Handoff (SCH) to address these requirements. At each interaction step, SCH hands the LLM a compact context containing a semantic state —its current understanding 1arXiv:2611.24357v1 [cs.AI] 18 Nov 2026 Preprint and work status—together with evidence relevant to the current decision. The semantic state captures task constraints, revisable facts and hypotheses, and work lifecycle, while evidence consists of stored action–observation records that support the current state or may become relevant to later decisions. These records are preserved separately in an evidence archive, allowing earlier observations to leave the routine prompt and later return when subsequent execution signals that they are relevant again. Rather than regenerating the full state after each interaction, the model proposes only a sparse state delta alongside its next action, while a deterministic controller updates the state and selects evidence for subsequent handoffs. Our experiments test both the recovery mechanism and the resulting complete system. In a controlled ablation with semantic-state machinery, controller policy, action interface, and evidence budget fixed, recovering evidence associated with previously completed work increases mean Pass@1 from 

80 .00% to 86 .89% at essentially unchanged token use and execution time, with significant paired improvements across all three repeated runs. In the complete-system comparison, SCH reaches 

86 .89% mean Pass@1 while using 43 .3% fewer tokens and 40 .8% less wall-clock time than the strongest baseline by task performance. Across models and benchmarks, SCH attains the highest observed Pass@1 in three of four conditions while providing a strong effectiveness–efficiency trade-off. Trajectory analysis further confirms that SCH recovers evidence from substantially earlier parts of the interaction history rather than merely preserving a recent context window. The key contribution is a different representation boundary for long-horizon context management: 

state–evidence separation . SCH separates the agent’s mutable interpretation of execution from the append-only action–observation evidence on which it was formed. The former is maintained as compact semantic state; the latter may leave routine context while remaining addressable through provenance. Lifecycle-aware revision reconnects them when later execution invalidates a conclusion or reopens completed work. Structured state and external memory have precedents (Kim & Hwang, 2025); SCH’s contribution is this boundary together with revision semantics that operate across it. Concretely, we make the following contributions: • State–evidence separation for semantic handoff. SCH keeps mutable current under-standing in compact semantic state and executed action–observation evidence in a separate append-only archive. Provenance links state entries to the archive, so compact context need not losslessly preserve its source evidence. • Revision-aware evidence recovery. Evidence associated with completed work can leave routine context and later become available again when execution indicates that the corre-sponding state should be reconsidered, without an additional memory or retrieval-model call. • Controlled and broad evaluation. Matched ablations isolate the contribution of historical evidence recovery, while complete-system, cross-model, cross-benchmark, component, and trajectory analyses evaluate effectiveness, efficiency, portability, and non-local evidence recovery. Appendix B provides additional motivation, and Appendix C provides expanded related-work distinc-tions omitted from the main paper for space. 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

Compression and context management. Long-context methods reduce active history through several forms of context compression. LongLLMLingua (Jiang et al., 2024) compresses prompts by selectively retaining informative content, while ReSum (Wu et al., 2025) periodically summarizes accumulated interaction history and ACON (Kang et al., 2026) learns compression strategies for long-horizon agents. PACT (Huang et al., 2026) represents interactions as compact action–state records, whereas CAT (Liu et al., 2026) and MemPO (Li et al., 2026b) learn when and how context or memory should be condensed and retained. SCH differs in what it carries forward: rather than maintaining a compressed interaction history, it maintains a single representation of the current execution state—task constraints, revisable facts and hypotheses, and work lifecycle—and updates it through sparse deltas. Detailed action–observation evidence is stored separately, allowing the routine handoff to remain centered on the agent’s current task state while historical details remain recoverable when needed. 2Preprint

The controller updates the state, executes the action, stores the resulting action–observation evidence, and selects evidence for subse-quent handoffs. 

Agent memory and recoverable information. A broader line of work stores, abstracts, retrieves, or reuses prior experience to support later reasoning. Generative Agents (Park et al., 2023) and MemoryBank (Zhong et al., 2024) maintain long-term experience or conversational memory. Re-flexion (Shinn et al., 2023) and ExpeL (Zhao et al., 2024) retain feedback or reusable experience from previous attempts, while Agent Workflow Memory (Wang et al., 2025) and Voyager (Wang et al., 2023) reuse learned workflows or skills across tasks. MemGPT (Packer et al., 2023) organizes information across memory tiers, whereas ReadAgent (Lee et al., 2024) and OCR-Memory (Li et al., 2026a) preserve access to higher-fidelity source content after compaction. These methods establish the value of retaining and selectively recovering information rather than continuously exposing the full history. SCH focuses specifically on linking a compact representation of the current execution state to the underlying action–observation evidence that may later be needed to reconsider it. 

Structured and revision-aware memory. Recent work also moves beyond free-form summaries toward explicit state representations. HiSA (Li et al., 2026c) hierarchically transforms interaction history into compact semantic states, while CoEx (Kim & Hwang, 2025) maintains an evolving neurosymbolic belief state. SCH therefore does not claim that structured or semantic state itself is new. The distinction is what the state is responsible for preserving: SCH deliberately makes the semantic state non-self-sufficient, because observations needed to verify or revise it remain separately addressable. CoEx evolves the belief state; MAGE (Chen et al., 2026) can restore an earlier execution-state boundary; and Proactive Memory Agent (Wu et al., 2026) uses a separate memory agent to decide what stored information to surface. SCH instead keeps current interpretation and source evidence as distinct persistent objects and defines revision as selective re-exposure of completed-work provenance. Additional distinctions are provided in Appendix C. 

## 3 SEMANTIC CONTEXT HANDOFF 

As shown in Figure 1, SCH organizes each agent interaction around three components: a semantic state , an evidence archive , and a deterministic controller . The semantic state compactly represents the agent’s current understanding and work status, while the evidence archive preserves executed action–observation records outside the routine prompt. The controller connects the two by updating the state and determining which archived evidence should become visible again. 3Preprint Each interaction follows the same handoff loop. At step t, the controller constructs the LLM handoff prompt 

Ct = [ x, S t, P t, R t],

where x is the task/system instruction, St is the current semantic state, Pt contains recently produced evidence that remains visible until processed by the LLM, and Rt ⊆ Lt contains historical evidence recovered from evidence archive Lt for the current decision. The LLM returns two outputs in one response: a sparse state delta ∆t describing changes to St, and the next environment action at.The controller applies the delta to obtain St+1 , executes at, stores the resulting action–observation record in Lt+1 , and constructs the next handoff Ct+1 . The controller is a deterministic, non-LLM component; state reconciliation, evidence storage, and evidence selection therefore require no separate summarization or retrieval-model call. 3.1 SEMANTIC STATE AND SPARSE DELTAS 

The semantic state St represents the agent’s current execution status rather than a compressed transcript of previous interactions. It contains four types of information: task constraints , facts and hypotheses , work items , and a task phase . Task constraints capture requirements or assumptions expected to remain stable. Facts capture environment-grounded information that the agent currently treats as established—for example, that an inspected component has a particular property or that a tool returned a particular result—whereas hypotheses capture provisional interpretations that may later be revised. These labels organize the agent’s current state rather than certify semantic truth. Work items represent responsibilities or subgoals and follow a simple lifecycle: open work remains unresolved, blocked work cannot currently proceed, and completed work is considered resolved. For example, “identify the cause of failure F ” may begin as open, become blocked while required information is unavailable, and later become completed after a diagnosis is established. The task phase gives a coarse description of the current execution stage, such as information gathering, acting, or verification. Facts, hypotheses, and work items can retain provenance links to the evidence on which they were based. Concretely, these links are evidence-record identifiers stored with a state entry. Support 

identifies evidence directly cited for a fact, hypothesis, or work item, while lineage preserves evidence accumulated as a work item progresses through its lifecycle. For example, if observation e7

motivates hypothesis h1, the LLM can attach e7 as support for h1. If the corresponding work is later completed, its provenance links remain associated with that work rather than being discarded. Instead of asking the LLM to reproduce the entire state after every interaction, the prompt requests only a sparse update, {"delta" : ∆ t, "command" : at}. This preserves unchanged state entries rather than asking the LLM to repeatedly reconstruct them, reducing both redundant generation and unintended modification of state that is not being revised. The delta therefore contains only changes caused by the current interaction, such as adding or revising a fact or hypothesis, changing a work item’s status, reopening completed work, or marking evidence as processed. The controller computes 

St+1 = V(St, ∆t, L t),

where V deterministically reconciles the proposed delta with the previous state. Evidence identifiers referenced by the delta are checked against Lt, and requested lifecycle transitions are checked for structural validity. The controller therefore maintains state structure and provenance, while semantic reasoning remains the responsibility of the LLM. Additional state fields and reconciliation rules are provided in Appendix D.1–D.2. 3.2 EVIDENCE ARCHIVE AND ACKNOWLEDGEMENT 

The evidence archive Lt stores the actions actually executed in the environment and the observations they return. After the environment executes at and produces observation ot, the controller forms evidence record et = ( t, a t, o t) and updates the archive by adding et to Lt to obtain Lt+1 . The step 4Preprint number t serves as the evidence identifier used by the support and lineage links defined above. Model reasoning is not stored as execution evidence. A newly produced record remains visible in Pt until the LLM has processed it. The LLM then 

acknowledges the record, indicating either that its durable implication has been incorporated into the semantic state or that the observation does not need persistent visibility. For example, an observation may support completing an open work item, in which case its implication is retained in that work item’s state; alternatively, an inspection may prove irrelevant and require no persistent state update. In either case, acknowledgement removes the record from pending context Pt but not from the evidence archive Lt.Thus, acknowledgement changes visibility , not storage. Evidence can leave the routine handoff without being deleted and remain available for later recovery. 3.3 REVISION -A WARE EVIDENCE RECOVERY 

The provenance links defined above provide the primary route from semantic state back to historical evidence. During ordinary execution, the controller can select support and lineage associated with currently retained facts, hypotheses, and unresolved work for inclusion in Rt.A core SCH mechanism handles cases in which previously completed work must be reconsidered. A 

revision signal can arise in two ways. First, the controller can detect a recognized failure in newly produced environment evidence while revisable state is present. Second, the LLM can explicitly revise the state through its delta, for example by retracting a fact or reopening completed work. In either case, provenance associated with the affected completed work becomes eligible for recovery in addition to evidence associated with the currently active state. This allows observations from much earlier execution stages to return even after the work they supported has left the active handoff. A concrete example illustrates the mechanism. Suppose an agent inspects a system at step 6 and obtains evidence e6 indicating that configuration A may cause failure F . It records hypothesis 

h1 with e6 as support, modifies the system, and later marks the corresponding work completed. After acknowledgement, e6 no longer needs to remain in the routine prompt. At step 24 , however, verification produces a new failure. The controller recognizes the failure while revisable state exists, or the LLM responds by reopening the completed work in its delta. Either event triggers revision, making the completed work’s provenance—including e6—eligible for the next handoff. The LLM can then reconsider the earlier diagnosis using the original observation rather than only its compact state representation. Provenance cannot cover every observation that may later become useful. Some evidence may never have been attached to a persistent state entry, or its previous state entry may no longer be retained. SCH therefore supplements provenance with deterministic orphan recovery : archived records without a current provenance link can become candidates when their paths or terms overlap with the current state. Recent and safety-relevant records provide additional fallback candidates. These sources complement rather than replace provenance: directly linked evidence receives highest priority, while revision additionally activates completed-work provenance. Putting the pieces together. Consider a generic task in which an agent must modify a system to satisfy requirement R while preserving constraint C. After several interactions, a handoff might contain:                           

> TASK: satisfy requirement Rwhile preserving C.SEMANTIC STATE: constraint: preserve C;fact: the target currently uses option A;hypothesis: option Amay cause failure F[support: e4]; work: determine whether Acauses F[open]. > sparse state delta + next environment action.

The archive may contain many other action–observation records, but only evidence selected for the current decision appears in the handoff. Here, the semantic state carries the agent’s current understanding, e7 provides evidence relevant to the immediate decision, and the sparse delta allows the LLM to update only the affected state entries. If later execution invalidates a conclusion or reopens completed work, the lifecycle-aware mechanism described above can bring its earlier supporting evidence back into a subsequent handoff. 

## 4 EXPERIMENTS 

Setup. We evaluate SCH on AppWorld (Trivedi et al., 2024) and SWE-bench Verified (Jimenez et al., 2024) with MiniMax and DeepSeek. Because repeating every method–model–environment combination is computationally prohibitive, we use three provider runs in one primary setting for controlled mechanism attribution and frozen single-run breadth settings to test transfer. SWE-bench uses a 150-task sample from the 450 tasks remaining after a separate 50-task development cohort. For stratification only, we compute a deterministic difficulty proxy from gold-patch changed lines, files, and hunks, divide candidates into terciles, and sample jointly by repository and difficulty, yielding 49 easy, 50 medium, and 51 hard tasks shared by all SWE-bench methods and both model families. AppWorld uses the 417-task test-challenge cohort for MiniMax and a separate frozen random 200-task sample for DeepSeek, so its model blocks are not a controlled cross-model comparison. AppWorld and SWE-bench also use different host-agent interfaces (the AppWorld agent interface and SWE-Agent (Yang et al., 2024)); SCH keeps the same state–evidence architecture and controller logic across them, with adaptation limited to environment-facing prompt/action formatting and execution-event handling. Exact task lists, sampling metadata, and run manifests are in the anonymous repository. DeepSeek SWE-bench is the primary setting . Full History, PACT, ACON, SCH, and controlled evidence selectors each receive three runs on the same 150 tasks; breadth settings are single-run. PACT/ACON are matched single-agent adaptations. The primary setting also includes MAGE matched, a paper-guided implementation because sufficient public reference code was unavailable, and Proactive Memory, adapted from its official implementation. Primary runs use temperature 0 and top-p = 1 . SWE-bench controller limits are 12,000 characters each for state and recovered evidence, 12 historical records per handoff, candidate caps of six revision-associated/three recent/four orphan/six safety records, a 12-inspection anti-overresearch threshold, 16,384 output tokens, and 100 calls/task. These limits are fixed across controlled selector variants; further details are in Appendix E and the anonymous repository.

4.1 CONTROLLED MECHANISM STUDY : R EVISION -A WARE RECOVERY 

The central mechanistic claim of SCH is that evidence associated with previously completed work should become available again when later execution requires the current state to be reconsidered. We therefore compare historical-evidence selection policies while holding the semantic-state machinery, work lifecycle, task cohort, model, action interface, controller limits, and maximum evidence budget fixed. In particular, active-state provenance can recover support and lineage associated with currently active state, whereas revision-aware SCH additionally makes provenance from completed work eligible when revision is triggered. Active-state provenance reaches 80 .00 ± 1.33% mean Pass@1, whereas revision-aware SCH reaches 

86 .89 ± 1.39% , a 6.89-point difference at essentially unchanged token use and runtime. Active-state provenance uses 209 .8 ± 6.2k tokens/task versus 208 .6 ± 5.8k for SCH, with corresponding runtimes of 233 .8 ± 7.3 and 237 .1 ± 7.4 seconds/task. The paired task outcomes consistently favor revision-aware SCH across all three runs. SCH-only versus active-state-only outcomes are 15–4 in Run 1, 14–4 in Run 2, and 14–4 in Run 3, with exact McNemar p = 0 .0192 , 0.0309 , and 0.0309 , respectively. Thus, under matched state machinery and evidence budgets, making completed-work provenance available under revision produces a consistent and statistically significant paired improvement in all three runs. The generic selectors provide additional context. Revision-aware SCH exceeds recent-window recovery by 6.00 points and lexical/BM25 recovery by 3.56 points in repeated-run mean Pass@1. Active-state provenance alone does not outperform these generic selectors, so the results do not support a monotonic hierarchy in which provenance is always preferable to recency or lexical retrieval. Instead, they support the narrower mechanism motivating SCH: provenance becomes substantially more useful when revision can reactivate evidence associated with previously completed work. Recent-window and lexical recovery use fewer tokens, while active-state provenance is marginally faster; the direct active-state-versus-SCH comparison should therefore be interpreted primarily as an effectiveness comparison at essentially matched resource cost. 4.2 MAIN SYSTEM RESULTS IN THE PRIMARY SETTING

## 5 DISCUSSION 

The selector, component, and complete-system studies answer complementary questions. Semantic State Only is deliberately incomplete: its weaker result supports the state–evidence separation 

premise rather than arguing against semantic state. SCH allows state to be lossy with respect to historical detail because underlying execution evidence remains recoverable. Recent-history variants retain local evidence but lack durable state/provenance links for reactivating completed-work evidence. The matched selector study isolates this distinction: revision-aware recovery raises Pass@1 from 

80 .00 ± 1.33% to 86 .89 ± 1.39% over active-state provenance at essentially matched token use. Thus state and recoverable evidence are complementary, with the matched gain specifically supporting revision-triggered completed-work recovery. At system level, SCH uses 43.3% fewer mean tokens and 40.8% less mean runtime than PACT; its 3.11-point mean Pass@1 gap remains descriptive because the Run 1 paired McNemar test is not significant. 

## 6 LIMITATIONS 

Repeated statistical evaluation is concentrated in primary DeepSeek SWE-bench; frozen single-run cross-model/environment settings provide breadth evidence, so we do not claim universal transfer. The controller needs lightweight host-interface adaptation, but the same semantic-state, provenance, acknowledgement, lifecycle, and recovery design spans the AppWorld agent interface and SWE-Agent; only prompt/action formatting and task-specific event handling change. Run-level SDs reuse the same cohort; active-state-provenance versus SCH paired tests cover all three runs, while other paired analyses are primary-run unless stated otherwise. PACT/ACON are matched adapta-tions, MAGE is paper-guided, and Proactive Memory follows its released implementation. Pending evidence is uncapped, provenance preserves addressability rather than verifying semantic entail-ment, and historical-evidence-cap sensitivity is not exhaustively studied. Appendix G gives further controller/interface limitations, including the fixed anti-overresearch heuristic. 

## 7 CONCLUSION 

SCH introduces state–evidence separation for long-horizon context: compact mutable state carries current interpretation while executed evidence remains separately addressable. Revision-aware recovery improves mean Pass@1 from 80 .00 ± 1.33% to 86 .89 ± 1.39% at essentially unchanged token use; in the primary complete-system comparison, SCH also reduces mean token use by 43.3% and runtime by 40.8% relative to PACT. Across settings, recovery is genuinely non-local. The central result is that compact state need not preserve every historical detail when its supporting evidence can return under revision.

## B ADDITIONAL MOTIVATION 

Why recoverability matters after a belief is formed. Consider a software-engineering agent that inspects several files and concludes that a particular function is the source of a failing test. After implementing a fix, it may compress the earlier repository observations into the durable belief that the root cause has been identified. If later verification contradicts this belief, the useful question is no longer simply “what did I conclude?” The agent may need to ask why that conclusion was reached, which observations supported it, whether those observations admit another interpretation, and which previously rejected hypothesis should now be reconsidered. A compressed summary can preserve the conclusion while removing precisely the evidence needed for this revision. The issue is not that compression necessarily produces an incorrect state. Rather, a compact state may outlive the evidence from which it was formed. Once raw observations leave the accessible context, the model can no longer inspect them when subsequent execution reveals that a belief may need to be reconsidered. Keeping a recent-history window only partially addresses this issue because the usefulness of historical evidence is not determined by recency: an observation from many steps earlier can suddenly become more informative than the immediately preceding interactions. This motivates separating two questions that are conflated by transcript retention and irreversible summarization: 

What semantic information should the agent carry forward by default, and what historical evidence must remain recoverable if that information later needs to be revised? 

## C ADDITIONAL RELATED -W ORK DETAIL 

The main paper summarizes the closest method families. We preserve here the additional distinctions removed for space. 

Compression and communication. ReSum periodically converts accumulated interaction histories into compact reasoning states, allowing web-search agents to continue beyond a fixed trajectory context (Wu et al., 2025). ACON optimizes natural-language compression guidelines from cases in which full-context trajectories succeed but compressed trajectories fail, and also studies distillation of the resulting compressor (Kang et al., 2026). CAT treats context management as an explicit agent action: it maintains stable task semantics, condensed long-term memory, and high-fidelity working memory, and trains a context-aware model to decide when historical interactions should be compressed (Liu et al., 2026). MemPO similarly learns a policy for proactively summarizing and managing memory during long-horizon interaction (Li et al., 2026b). PACT was introduced for multi-agent communication: it projects unconstrained messages into compact action–state records before they enter shared history (Huang et al., 2026). In our experiments, we adapt its action–state representation to the corresponding single-agent temporal-history setting. This is useful because PACT and SCH both replace unconstrained historical text with semantic representations, but differ after information leaves the active record: PACT primarily communicates a compact action–state history, whereas SCH maintains a materialized current state whose revisable items remain linked to separately stored canonical execution evidence. 

Agent memory beyond compression. Generative Agents store streams of experiences, construct higher-level reflections, and retrieve relevant memories for planning (Park et al., 2023). MemoryBank maintains and updates long-term conversational memories (Zhong et al., 2024), while MemGPT treats context as a hierarchy of memory tiers and moves information between them as needed (Packer 13 Preprint et al., 2023). Reflexion carries forward verbal feedback from prior failures (Shinn et al., 2023). ExpeL extracts reusable knowledge and experiences from previous tasks (Zhao et al., 2024); Agent Workflow Memory induces reusable workflows (Wang et al., 2025); and Voyager stores executable skills that can be retrieved and composed during embodied exploration (Wang et al., 2023). These systems establish the broader usefulness of selecting, abstracting, and retrieving information rather than continuously exposing all prior experience. ReadAgent compresses long documents into gist memories while allowing look-back to the source text (Lee et al., 2024); OCR-Memory stores historical trajectories in a compact visual representation and uses locate-and-transcribe retrieval to recover verbatim historical text (Li et al., 2026a). These systems share SCH’s motivation that context reduction need not imply irreversible information loss. SCH differs in focusing on when execution evidence becomes relevant again and on maintaining provenance between that evidence and a persistent task state. 

Structured and revision-aware execution memory. CoEx is an important prior precedent for treating an agent’s evolving interpretation of the environment as an explicit structured state rather than only as transcript or free-form summary. Its adaptive belief state combines code-based symbolic memory with structured textual memory; after each subgoal episode, a verification-and-synthesis module incorporates task-relevant experience into the next belief state, and the planner conditions on belief-state history (Kim & Hwang, 2025). This establishes that evolving structured belief state itself is not unique to SCH. SCH targets the state–evidence separation boundary: the controller maintains one current semantic execution state with explicit semantic roles and work lifecycle, applies sparse model-proposed deltas, and stores action–observation records separately. Facts and work items retain support/lineage handles into those immutable records, so changing the current interpretation does not overwrite the evidence from which it was formed. CoEx, by contrast, jointly introduces hierarchical subgoal planning, symbolic world memory, and verification/synthesis; we therefore discuss it as architectural prior work rather than claiming a controlled matched implementation. Two concurrent 2026 works provide especially close comparisons for revision and selective resurfac-ing. MAGE organizes interactions into a hierarchical execution-state tree, compresses completed subgoals, and provides a revision operation that can restore an earlier state boundary before exploring a new branch (Chen et al., 2026). Proactive Memory Agent maintains a structured memory bank using a separate memory agent and selectively injects reminders when information is likely to mat-ter for the action agent (Wu et al., 2026). SCH instead keeps current interpretation and executed action–observation evidence as distinct persistent objects: revision selectively re-exposes supporting evidence rather than restoring an entire prior state or continuously invoking another memory model. For experiments, MAGE is a paper-guided matched implementation because sufficient public refer-ence code was unavailable; Proactive Memory is adapted from its released implementation under the same primary evaluation protocol.

D.1 PERSISTENT SEMANTIC STATE : F IELD SEMANTICS 

The persistent state distinguishes semantic roles that a free-form summary can blur. In the released implementation it contains: • constraints : task requirements and assumptions expected to remain valid across turns; • active facts : repository or environment observations, hypotheses, root causes, edits, and verification results; • open work : concrete unresolved responsibilities; • completed and blocked work : responsibilities whose status should not be repeatedly rediscovered; and • current phase : whether the agent is primarily inspecting, implementing, verifying, or preparing to submit. A durable fact conceptually has the form 

f = (id , kind , scope , statement , support) ,

14 Preprint where support contains identifiers of evidence steps in the evidence archive; work items analogously retain support and lineage references. The point of this typing is that an unverified hypothesis, an observed repository fact, and a verified edit remain distinguishable even after the raw observations that produced them leave routine context. D.2 SPARSE SEMANTIC HANDOFF : A DDITIONAL RATIONALE 

The model proposes only the state changes that occurred on the current turn rather than regenerat-ing the complete persistent state. Supported lifecycle transitions include adding or revising facts, dropping invalidated facts, opening work, completing or blocking work, reopening prior work, and acknowledging evidence that no longer needs to remain visible. The controller remains the owner of the materialized state: 

St+1 = V(St, ∆t, L t),

where V is deterministic reconciliation against the prior state and available evidence. This ownership split has two practical consequences omitted from the compact presentation. First, the model does not spend output tokens repeatedly reproducing unchanged memory. Second, semantic persistence is separated from semantic proposal: a proposed update can be rejected or partially recovered without replacing the authoritative state wholesale. D.3 EVIDENCE ARCHIVE , P ROVENANCE , AND VALIDATION BOUNDARY 

For every executed action and observation, the controller stores the canonical evidence record 

et = ( t, a t, o t), Lt+1 = Lt ∪ { et}.

The evidence archive stores operational action–observation evidence rather than hidden model reasoning. Each record receives a stable identifier that can be referenced by semantic facts and work items. For each revisable semantic item z, SCH preserves 

support( z) ⊆ dom( L),

so removing an observation from the active prompt does not destroy its addressability. The controller checks semantic-state transitions against this canonical evidence. A proposed durable fact must reference evidence identifiers available to the controller, and transition-specific rules can require appropriate evidence categories before lifecycle changes are accepted. For example, edit/diff evidence can be required before completed edit work is materialized, and validation evidence can be required before verification work is marked complete. These checks verify witness existence, evidence type, and transition consistency; they do not prove that arbitrary model-authored fact text is semantically entailed by the cited observation. For revisable facts and completed work, the intended invariant is therefore 

z ∈ St =⇒ the provenance needed to revise z remains addressable in Lt.

D.4 EVIDENCE ACKNOWLEDGEMENT AND CONTEXT COMPACTION 

New evidence initially remains pending. After the model either incorporates its durable implication into semantic state or determines that it is non-durable, it can acknowledge the corresponding step. For an acknowledged step ei,

ei /∈ Pt+1 , ei ∈ Lt+1 .

Acknowledgement therefore controls visibility , not deletion. The intended distinction from irreversible summarization is 

Ht −→ (St, L t)

rather than only Ht −→ St: semantic state becomes the normal handoff, while the evidence archive remains a provenance-preserving backing store. Equivalently, evidence may leave the prompt ̸ ⇒ evidence is destroyed .

Support and lineage scope. During ordinary continuation, direct support and lineage for active facts, open work, and blocked work remain eligible replay handles. On an explicit revision event, completed-work provenance is added so that the causal path behind a previously completed imple-mentation or verification can be reconstructed. Pending evidence is already visible through Pt and is excluded from historical replay selection.

This rule is lexical and interface-specific; it does not infer a new root cause. 

Recent trace, rendering, and deduplication. The controller permits up to three already-acknowledged recent evidence archive steps as an exact causal tail. Pending evidence and steps already selected through explicit state evidence or safety anchors are excluded from this tail. Direct-support, explicit revision, and recent records receive bounded exact excerpts; other records use bounded relevant-line excerpts. Candidate sources are merged before the global step limit is applied, so duplicate evidence archive identifiers do not consume multiple replay slots. 

Audit metadata. D.8 ROBUST STRUCTURED INTERFACE 

Different models vary in how reliably they follow the structured update protocol. To avoid turning recoverable formatting errors into failed environment interactions, the parser tolerates supported variants such as fenced JSON and minor JSON-like syntax errors and attempts to recover a unique executable action even when part of the semantic delta is malformed. If an action-format retry is necessary, an already valid semantic delta is preserved and the model is asked only to repair the executable action. Thus, whenever possible, formatting failure ̸ ⇒ loss of a valid semantic update ,

and malformed semantic bookkeeping ̸ ⇒ failed environment action .

A model that repeatedly omits or corrupts semantic updates can still execute useful actions while maintaining lower-quality persistent state. The benchmark results do not isolate parse-error, retry, acknowledgement, or invalid-provenance rates, so aggregate cross-model accuracy differences should not be interpreted as direct measurements of interface compliance. D.9 COMPLETE SCH I NTERACTION LOOP 

The complete controller lifecycle, omitted from the compact main-text pseudocode, is: A semantic delta can only be validated against evidence already available when that delta is accepted; the observation produced by the current action becomes evidence for subsequent turns. D.10 DESIGN PRINCIPLE 

Full History retains evidence according to existence : every interaction remains visible. Sliding-window and recent-history methods retain evidence primarily according to recency . SCH instead carries semantic conclusions by default and exposes historical evidence according to revision rele-vance . The method is therefore not intended to construct a perfect compressed transcript. Its narrower objective is to maintain a compact semantic working state while preserving access to the exact evidence required to revise that state as the task evolves.

## G ADDITIONAL LIMITATIONS 

Beyond the limitations stated in the main paper, three scope details are worth preserving. First, the detailed per-call prompt distribution is reported only for the primary 150-task DeepSeek SWE-bench Full-History arm; we do not tabulate the same distribution for every Full-History condition. Second, MAGE is a paper-guided matched implementation, while Proactive Memory is adapted from its released implementation; the evaluation still does not instantiate every structured-memory or selective-retrieval system discussed in related work. Third, the anti-overresearch hint can contribute to the absolute behavior of the full controller even though it is held fixed across controlled selector variants; the mechanism audit therefore reports its firing frequency separately from explicit revision events rather than attributing every runtime or success change to replay. 

## H ADDITIONAL REPRODUCIBILITY AND ETHICS 

Reproducibility. The released SCH controller preserves the JSON action transport, parser tolerance, scoped retry-delta carry, and semantic-state schema while making historical evidence projection explicit and auditable. The release includes behavior tests for revision-triggered support/lineage replay, deterministic recovery of unlinked path/term-overlap evidence, exclusion of pending evidence from the recent replay tail, and initialization-order regression coverage for the explicit revision hint. Controller audit metadata records revision triggers, replayed support and lineage, orphan-retrieval scores, recent replay, step-budget application, and the anti-overresearch trigger. Exact model/provider identifiers, evaluation dates, task IDs/hashes, prompts, per-run manifests, and token-accounting records are included with the released experiment outputs, including the primary DeepSeek SWE-bench provider runs for the main baselines, the MAGE matched and Proactive Memory comparisons, and the updated active-state provenance control. 

Ethics. The work evaluates agents in AppWorld’s simulated applications and on SWE-bench software-engineering tasks; it does not require deployment on real user accounts. More reliable context and work-state tracking may reduce unintended actions, but the method is not a formal safety guarantee. Production use should retain authorization checks, least-privilege tools, auditable execution logs, and human confirmation for consequential actions.
