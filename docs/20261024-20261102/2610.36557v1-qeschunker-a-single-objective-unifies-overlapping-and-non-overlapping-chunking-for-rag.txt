ABSTRACT 

Chunking is a fundamental preprocessing step in retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), yet most existing chunkers rely primarily on heuristics. We introduce the Question–Evidence-Supervised Chunker (QESChunker), to our knowledge the first method for constructing static chunk indices from question–evidence su-pervision. QESChunker constructs document-specific chunk indices by optimiz-ing over human-annotated or LLM-generated question–evidence pairs, without access to held-out test queries. We formulate non-overlapping and overlapping index construction through a unified question–evidence utility-maximization ob-jective, turning chunking into a supervised combinatorial optimization problem. Under the non-overlap constraint, the objective decomposes over chunks and can be optimized exactly in polynomial time by dynamic programming. When over-lap is allowed, the objective becomes monotone submodular; exact optimization is NP-hard, but our greedy achieves a (1 − 1/√e)-approximation. Across two self-constructed and three public datasets, both variants outperform existing chunking methods in complete-evidence recall in nearly all evaluated settings, under the same overlap regime and a fixed hybrid retriever. These results recast chunking as supervised, budget-aware index construction for evidence retrieval. 

1 INTRODUCTION 

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) conditions a language model on passages retrieved from an external corpus (Lewis et al., 2020; Gao et al., 2023). Before any retrieval can occur, each document must be segmented into chunks that are embedded and indexed. Where the chunk boundaries fall largely determines whether the evidence for a question can be retrieved completely .Despite its importance, chunking is overwhelmingly heuristic in practice. The baselines evaluated in this paper span fixed-length and fixed-overlap passages used by retrieval systems (Lewis et al., 2020; Guu et al., 2020; Karpukhin et al., 2020), natural paragraphs, lexical-cohesion segmentation with TextTiling (Hearst, 1997), embedding-based ClusterSemanticChunker (Smith & Troynikov, 2024), LLM-predicted boundaries with LumberChunker (Duarte et al., 2024), sentence-window re-trieval (LlamaIndex, 2024b), and hierarchical Parent-Document and Auto-Merging retrieval (Lla-maIndex, 2024a). These practical alternatives cover non-overlapping, overlapping, semantic, LLM-based, and hierarchical designs, but none optimizes an explicit question–evidence objective. We cast chunking as an explicit optimization problem and show that a single objective, subjected to two different structural constraints, yields two algorithms with complementary guarantees. We call the resulting framework QESC HUNKER (Question– Evidence Supervised Chunker): questions and their evidence blocks supervise where boundaries should fall, and the same objective is then solved exactly or near-optimally under each constraint. We model a document as a sequence of atomic blocks and define a two-term objective comprising a coverage term and a structural term (§3). 2. Two algorithms under different constraints. 2 RELATED WORK 

Chunking and adjacent segmentation. Our evaluated baselines are fixed-length, natural-paragraph, TextTiling, ClusterSemanticChunker, LumberChunker, fixed-window, Sentence-Window, Parent-Document, and Auto-Merging. Classical topic and discourse segmenters such as C99 and supervised neural boundary models optimize segmentation labels rather than RAG evidence recall (Choi, 2000; Koshorek et al., 2018; Lukasik et al., 2020). Other adjacent systems change more than chunk boundaries: Dense X replaces passages with generated propositions, Late Chunk-ing changes contextual embedding computation, and RAPTOR introduces recursive summaries and hierarchical retrieval (Chen et al., 2024; G¨ unther et al., 2024; Sarthi et al., 2024). We therefore exclude them from matched comparisons that isolate boundary selection under a fixed retriever and original-atom budget. 

Submodular optimization. Our overlap solver draws on classical monotone-submodular maxi-mization (Nemhauser et al., 1978; Krause & Golovin, 2014); the corresponding non-overlapping partition instead admits an exact dynamic program.

7 LIMITATIONS 

On one hand, our gains are measured at the retrieval level as complete-evidence recall. Providing more complete and effective evidence can plausibly improve downstream generation, but the rate at which recall gains translate into final answer quality remains unknown. This conversion depends on the generator, prompting, context ordering, evidence redundancy, and answer-level evaluation criteria; we therefore do not claim a fixed or proportional end-to-end improvement. On the other hand, boundary fitting depends heavily on the quality and coverage of existing question–evidence supervision. It is thus best suited to documents that are queried repeatedly and have accumulated sufficient supervision—for example, policy reports, FAQs, or frequently discussed papers. For one-off, cold-start, or sparsely annotated documents, such supervision may be unreliable or unavailable, and performance may be driven mainly by the structural prior and the retriever rather than by learned boundaries. 

8 CONCLUSION 

QESChunker casts chunk construction as supervised, budget-aware optimization rather than heuris-tic preprocessing. A shared hit-gated coverage objective yields two complementary solvers: exact dynamic programming for non-overlapping partitions and benefit-cost submodular greedy for over-lapping indices. Across five corpora, the two variants improve complete-evidence recall in nearly all displayed settings, with the largest gains appearing at tight returned-context budgets and smaller margins near saturation. The parameter sweeps further show that performance remains stable across broad structural-weight and candidate-length ranges.
