Abstract 

Efficient vector retrieval requires both a corpus geometry that supports retrieving the right documents through vector similarity, and a query encoder that can embed queries near their desired documents in the embedding space. Recent work has studied geometric capacity through the lens of the minimum embedding dimension needed to realize all top-k answer sets of n documents. We study a different notion of geometric capacity—the maximum recall achievable for a frozen document index—and explore whether learned query encoders can reach this ceiling. On several real-world retrieval benchmarks, we show that retrieval quality of single-vector query encoders often lies far below what the document indices can support. Motivated by this observation, we give theoretical evidence that learning query encoders can be computationally hard. In particular, we construct a retrieval task that (1) admits a query encoder with perfect recall which is representable by a small one-hidden-layer ReLU network, but (2) any statistical-query learner (a class capturing learners that access training data through aggregate statistics) provably requires exponentially many statistical queries to achieve non-trivial recall advantage over the random baseline k/n . Taken together, our results suggest substantial unrealized geometric capacity in retrieval benchmarks and establish query encoder learnability as a possible barrier in embedding-based retrieval. 

# 1 Introduction 

Efficient, large-scale modern vector retrieval systems are based on the bi-encoder architecture (Karpukhin et al., 2020; Lewis et al., 2020) in which documents are embedded offline as vectors and stored in a frozen index that can be reused across a stream of queries. At retrieval time, a query encoder maps the incoming query into the shared vector space, and the system returns the k best documents by similarity score (e.g. inner-product, cosine, or inverse ℓ2 distance). The success of this architecture depends on two distinct requirements:  

> •

the embedded document geometry must support accurate retrieval through vector similarity—that is, there must exist a query embedding whose k-nearest documents match the semantically intended documents for that query; and  

> •

it must be computationally feasible to learn a query encoder that produces these query embeddings.

Theoretically, it has been shown that even if documents are embedded optimally, geometry can be the limiting factor when embedding dimension is low and the retrieval task requires realizing many distinct top-k subsets of the document corpus; at the same time, empirical studies have observed that these geometric limits do not necessarily take effect in practice (Weller et al., 2026; S et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026; Okajima and Yoshida, 2026; Bangachev et al., 2026). These findings motivate the questions: to what extent does a document index support high recall, and subject to this ceiling, how much recall can a learned query encoder achieve? We investigate both questions empirically and theoretically. Our first contribution is an empirical study of the gap between the geometric capacity of a document index (i.e. the best recall it can possibly support) and the recall achieved by learned query encoders. We develop a ranking support vector machine (ranking SVM)-based method for efficiently gauging the geometric capacity of a given embedded corpus, then use it to study the recall attained on 20 datasets from the BRIGHT, BEIR and LIMIT retrieval benchmarks by various pre-trained and fine-tuned query encoders. Our results show that geometric capacity is rarely the limit—on most datasets, even a lightweight document encoder can support near-perfect retrieval. On the other hand, the tested query encoders—even when fine-tuned on queries and their gold sets—mostly fail to exceed 50% or even 25% recall on held-out test queries. These findings point to query-side encoding as a more salient barrier than geometric capacity in dense retrieval. These results are given in Section 3. Our second contribution is a theoretical result which shows the existence of a retrieval task for which learning a good query encoder is computationally hard. Namely, we construct a task for which a query encoder with perfect recall exists and can be represented as a small ReLU network, but learning a query encoder with better-than-random recall requires exponentially many steps in the statistical query (SQ) learning model (Kearns, 1993)—a computational model for supervised learning in which a learner may only access the training set via aggregate statistics. This establishes a rigorous setting where retrieval quality is constrained not by the document geometry, but by the inability to efficiently learn a good query encoder. This result is formalized and proved in Section 4. Our results complement the recent line of work on geometric limits of embedding-based retrieval, shining a spotlight on query encoding as a barrier and possible source of hardness. 

1.1 Related work 

Geometric limits of vector retrieval. A recent line of research has explored whether embedding dimension constrains the performance of single-vector retrieval methods by considering the question: what is the mini-mum dimension required to realize all top-k subsets of n documents? Weller et al. (2026) showed dimension lower bounds for realizability with and without margin, and introduced the LIMIT data to showcase these limitations empirically. Subsequent work has extended the no-margin lower bounds beyond inner product scoring (Wang et al., 2026), characterized the minimum embedding dimension for max-margin separation (Bangachev et al., 2026), and examined further limits imposed by quantization (Okajima and Yoshida, 2026). Each of these results assumes the ability to embed documents optimally. Empirically, independent observa-tions have shown that geometry is not, in fact, the primary source of difficulty for the LIMIT dataset (S et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026), instead attributing difficulty to mechanisms like drowning in documents or general learning failures. We show that this phenomenon is not unique to LIMIT—on realistic retrieval benchmarks, dense retrievers often achieve performance far below what is supported by the document geometry. 

SQ lower bounds. The statistical query (SQ) model (Kearns, 1993) restricts a learner’s sample access to aggregate statistics of the training distribution; a setting that captures gradient-based learning under suitable precision and batch-size conditions (Abbe et al., 2021). Following the foundational result that parity functions are not efficiently SQ-learnable (Kearns, 1993), subsequent work has characterized weak SQ-learnability 2for function classes (Blum et al., 1994) and developed SQ lower bounds for search problems in which an algorithm must produce an output satisfying a success criterion (Feldman et al., 2013). These techniques have yielded lower bounds for learning shallow neural networks (Song et al., 2017; Goel et al., 2020; Chen et al., 2022), robust estimation of gaussians (Diakonikolas et al., 2017), and robust classification (Bubeck et al., 2019). In this work, we use these techniques to show that learning a single-vector retrieval model with nontrivial recall can be SQ-hard.

3.1 Capacity lower bounds via ranking SVM 

The purpose of this section is to provide a practical method for computing the lower bounds on the geometric capacity cgeom (Q; X ) of a given retrieval benchmark (Q; X ). To motivate our approach, first notice that the gold set of documents G(q) for query q is perfectly retrievable by a single-vector query embedder precisely when some query embedding E(q) ranks every gold document above every non-gold document by inner product score. Datasets. We evaluate on three popular information retrieval benchmarks. Additional details on the composition of each dataset can be found in Appendix C.1.  

> •

LIMIT (Weller et al., 2026) is a synthetic dataset designed to exhibit limitations of embedding-based retrieval. Each query names an attribute that appears in exactly two documents in a corpus of size 50k. Gold sets cover all top-2 subsets of 46 documents, a construction in which no sufficiently low-dimensional embedding model can achieve perfect recall.  

> •

BRIGHT (Su et al., 2025) is a reasoning-intensive QA benchmark that spans 12 categories, each ranging from 7.9k to 414k documents. Queries are complex questions, and document relevance is determined by the extent to which it helps answer the question. Unlike many other retrieval settings, in BRIGHT gold documents often share little keyword overlap with the query.  

> •

BEIR (Thakur et al., 2021) is a diverse suite of retrieval tasks with corpora ranging from 3.6k to millions of documents. Though originally intended to evaluate zero-shot generalization, the diverse categories are highly useful for our in-domain experiments. Due to compute and licensing constraints, we restrict our evaluation to 7 categories listed in the appendix. 

Models. We evaluate two single-vector embedding models: DistilBERT (Sanh et al., 2020), a lightweight BERT encoder with embedding dimension 768 , and Qwen3-Embedding-4B (Zhang et al., 2025), a large semantic encoder with embedding dimension 2560 . Following the setup of Weller et al. (2026), as a point of comparison we also measure recall achieved by models with a different inductive bias: lexical search via BM25 (Robertson and Zaragoza, 2009), and multi-vector retrieval with late-interaction scoring via

Fine-tuning. When train-test splits are not pre-specified, we partition queries into a 70/30 train-test split. The three semantic embedding models are each fine-tuned using LoRA (Hu et al., 2022) (rank 16) with AdamW optimizer (Loshchilov and Hutter, 2017) and a contrastive multi-positive InfoNCE loss for 3,000 steps at the default learning rate. Negatives are sampled for corpora exceeding 200 k documents (100,000) and for multi-vector training (512). Due to limited data for validation, we report the model checkpoint at step 3,000.

# 5 Conclusion 

Complementing recent work on the geometric limitations of embedding-based retrieval, we explore whether learned query encoders can exploit the geometric capacity of a frozen document index. Our main takeaway is that producing a high-quality query encoder can pose a substantial barrier even when the geometry of the underlying document index is not restrictive. We exhibit this empirically on real-world encoder models and benchmarks through a ranking-SVM method to gauge geometric capacity, and theoretically through a computationally hard-to-learn family of retrieval tasks in the statistical query model. # A Limitations 

Empirical results. The empirical gap we demonstrate on real-world encoders and benchmarks is, in some part, an artifact of our specific fine-tuning setup; it does not preclude the existence of better query encoders which might have not been tested or not yet discovered. Furthermore, although we include multi-vector models as a point of reference, our ranking SVM bounds on geometric capacity do not immediately extend to the multi-vector setting, and we leave it to future work to directly optimize multi-vector embeddings. 

Theoretical results. Our computational hardness result is proven in the SQ model and thus limited to learning algorithms that adhere to it, and our hard retrieval task construction is necessarily contrived for the sake of the proof; it may not resemble retrieval tasks that occur in real applications. Overall, while our results are highly suggestive that query encoding may be a substantial barrier to better recall in dense retrieval, future work may develop better query encoding methods and evolve our understanding of this field.
