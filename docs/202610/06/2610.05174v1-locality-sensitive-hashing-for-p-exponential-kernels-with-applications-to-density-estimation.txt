# Abstract 

A kernel k(x, y ) is LSHable if there exists a locality sensitive hashing scheme H

such that k(x, y ) = Pr h∼H [h(x) = h(y)] for all x, y . This notion plays a key role in efficient kernel methods in high dimensions. In this work, we show that the 

p-exponential kernel k(x, y ) = exp( −∥ x − y∥p) is LSHable in bounded regions for all 1 < p ≤ 2. Previously, this was known only for p = 1 . Our new mosaic LSH scheme is based on a Poisson hyperplane process with hyperplanes sampled as ℓ1-biased p-stable vectors, for which we develop efficient sampling procedures. As applications, our results yield new and efficient density estimation methods based on LSHability for those p-exponential kernels. 

# 1 Introduction 

Kernel densities have long been fundamental to machine learning [21]. Hashing-based techniques have recently emerged as a powerful approach for kernel in high-dimensional settings where tradi-tional techniques are often rendered infeasible. The central notion is the following: 

Definition 1.1 (kernel LSH) . Let k : X × X → [0 , 1] be a map over pairs in X . A locality sensitive hashing (LSH) scheme for k is a distribution H over hash functions on X such that 

∀ x, y ∈ X , k(x, y ) = Pr 

> h∼H

[h(x) = h(y)] . (1) 

H must be endowed with an algorithm for sampling h ∼ H , a finite representation of h, and an algorithm to evaluate x 7 → h(x) given x ∈ X and h. If such H exists, we call k an LSHable kernel .

This notion was studied in Charikar’s seminal work on the SimHash algorithm [14], and more recently has been central in high-dimensional kernel density estimation (KDE) due to a breakthrough work by Charikar and Siminelakis [13]. Intuitively, k encodes the similarity between pairs of elements in X , and H is “locality sensitive” because similar elements are more likely to be hashed together. This property is the driving force behind a range of efficient high-dimensional kernel methods, including relative-error KDE [13, 44, 6], streaming KDE [16, 32], private KDE [17, 48, 47], kernel ridge regression [50], partition function estimation for importance sampling [45], and long-context attention in large language models [26, 49]. Despite its centrality and usefulness, there are rather few kernels for which LSHability is known. Classical examples include MinHash [10] for the Jaccard similarity between sets and SimHash [14] for the angular similarity between points on a sphere. For kernels over Rd, the Random Binning Features scheme of Rahimi and Recht [39] constitutes an LSH scheme for the 1-exponential kernel, a.k.a the Laplacian kernel, k(x, y ) = exp( −∥ x − y∥1). Backurs et al. [6] gave a different LSH scheme for the same kernel in a bounded region. This has left as an open problem even the natural case of the 2-exponential kernel, 1 k(x, y ) = exp( −∥ x−y∥2). Applications of LSH-based methods to the 2-exponential and to other kernels have largely gone through various approximations or heuristics.

Our results. In this work, we design new kernel LSH schemes and prove the LSHability property for all p-exponential kernels with 1 < p ≤ 2 in a bounded region. Our main result is the following. 

Theorem 1.2. Let p ∈ (1 , 2] and σ > 0. There exists a kernel LSH scheme H that satisfies Definition 1.1 for the p-exponential kernel, k(x, y ) = exp( −∥ x − y∥p/σ ), over points in Rd with coordinates bounded in [−1, 1] . The expected time to sample a hash function h ∼ H and to evaluate 

h on a point x are each O(d2/σ ).

The coordinate restriction to [−1, 1] can be scaled to any interval by inversely scaling the kernel bandwidth σ. Thus, Theorem 1.2 provides a kernel LSH scheme for any bounded region. The running time of the LSH scheme grows linearly with the side-length of a bounding hypercube for the region. As applications, Theorem 1.2 leads to new LSH-based algorithms for p-exponential kernels. We highlight two examples in Section 3: relative-error KDE and differentially private KDE. 

1.1 Background and Related Work Metric LSH. LSH was originally introduced by Indyk and Motwani [23] for metric spaces in the following form, for the purpose of approximate nearest neighbor search in high dimensions. 

Definition 1.3 (metric LSH [23]) . Let (X , D) be a metric space. A (r1, r 2, p 1, p 2)-LSH scheme for 

(X , D) is a distribution H over hash functions on X such that for each pair x, y ∈ X ,

D( x, y ) ≤ r1 ⇒ Pr 

> h∼H

[h(x) = h(y)] ≥ p1 and D( x, y ) > r 2 ⇒ Pr 

> h∼H

[h(x) = h(y)] ≤ p2.

They designed metric LSH schemes for ℓ1 and ℓ2 metric spaces. Datar et al. [18] designed metric LSH schemes for all ℓp metrics with p ∈ [1 , 2] . Their approach relies on p-stable distributions, which generalize the spherical invariance (i.e., 2-stable) property of the Gaussian distribution [22] (see Section 2.1). Andoni and Indyk [3] later presented a dramatically improved metric LSH for ℓ2, which turned out to be optimal [38, 35]. Their approach was extended to p ∈ (1 , 2) by Nguyen [36]. The interest in ℓp metrics with p ∈ (1 , 2) has both mathematical and empirical motivations. Mathe-matically, these spaces embed isometrically into an infinite-dimensional L1 space, suggesting that algorithmic results known for ℓ1 might extend to them. In machine learning, ℓp-based formulations offer a useful interpolation between sparsity-promoting ℓ1 formulations and smooth ℓ2 formulations, with empirical benefits demonstrated in several applications [28, 41, 31, 46, 29].. 

Kernel LSH. Charikar [14] focused on kernel LSH as defined in Definition 1.1, which had been previously used for set similarity measures like Broder’s MinHash algorithm for the Jaccard similarity [10]. He showed that kernel LSH is achievable and useful for vectors in Rd, designing the seminal SimHash algorithm. He also showed that if k(x, y ) is LSHable then 1 − k(x, y ) must satisfy the triangle inequality. Further properties of kernel LSH were studied by Chierichetti and Kumar [15]. Rahimi and Recht [39] designed the Random Binning Features method for the 1-exponential (a.k.a. Laplacian) kernel. As observed by Andoni and Indyk in [2], this method constitutes a kernel LSH scheme. Backurs et al. [6] gave a different kernel LSH scheme for the same kernel. Their scheme is restricted to points in a bounded region and is faster in certain parameter regimes. Both of these LSH schemes for the 1-exponential kernel share the same underlying technique: imposing a random axis-aligned grid on Rd and hashing each point to the grid cell that contains it. They differ in how the random grid is selected. The randomized grid approach is well-suited for ℓ1,albeit less so for other ℓp spaces. The metric LSH schemes for p ∈ [1 , 2] by [18] are also based on randomly shifted grids, composed over projections onto p-stable directions. Those projections can be viewed as lossy probabilistic embeddings of ℓp into ℓ1. This approach suffices for metric LSH, which implicitly involves an approximation gap (the gap is c = r2/r 1 in Definition 1.3). Unfortunately, it does not yield the exact collision identity in Definition 1.1 outside the p = 1 case. Therefore, new 2p = 1 .25 p = 1 .5 p = 1 .75 p = 2

Approximate kernel LSH. Approximate variants of kernel LSH relax the equality in (1) to hold only up to an additive [2, 48] or multiplicative [13, 6] approximation factor. Such variants suffice for some LSHability-based methods, with an additional loss depending on the approximation quality. A prominent example is the 2-exponential kernel exp( −∥ x − y∥2). An approximate kernel LSH scheme can be obtained by composing a ℓ2 → ℓ1 embedding step with distortion 1 ± ε, based on a variant of the Johnson–Lindenstrauss (JL) transform [1, 33], with a kernel LSH scheme for 

exp( −∥ x − y∥1). This approach has been used in [13, 6, 48] to apply LSHability methods to the 2-exponential kernel. The embedding distortion parameter ε controls the discrepancy between the collision probability and the target kernel, and the sampling and evaluation costs depend polynomially on ε−1. It therefore offers a trade-off between approximation accuracy and computational cost. For 

p ∈ (1 , 2) , approximate ℓp → ℓ1 embeddings (e.g., [25]) suggest an analogous route to approximate kernel LSH for p-exponential kernels, if they can be made algorithmic. However, such schemes would retain the embedding distortion as a parameter. Our results instead establish exact kernel LSH for all p ∈ (1 , 2] , removing the embedding distortion as a source of error and computational cost. 

Random tessellations. Balog et al. [8] showed that the pointwise cell collision probability of the Mondrian partition process [40] is equal to the Laplacian kernel. O’Reilly and Tran [37] extended this to the 2-exponential kernel via an isotropic STIT process. These results establish stochastic processes with the collision identity in eq. (1), but without the sampling and evaluation bounds needed for LSH. 

1.2 Our Techniques 

The first main step in our new approach to kernel LSH is to simulate Poisson hyperplane processes (PHPs). These are stochastic processes for generating random hyperplanes in Rd. The sampled hyperplanes induce a partition of Rd called a mosaic [42]. The mosaic cells will be our LSH bins. The appeal of PHPs in LSH for p-exponential kernels is the following. For a compact subset A ⊂ Rd

let H(A) denote the set of all affine hyperplanes that intersect it. The number of samples from H(A)

in a PHP admits a Poisson distribution with parameter λA := R 

> H(A)

dν where ν is the intensity measure of the PHP. Two points x, y ∈ Rd will form a hash collision if no hyperplanes cross the interval Ixy that connects them. This occurs with probability Pr[Poisson( λIxy ) = 0] = exp( −λIxy ).Thus, if we could choose the PHP intensity ν such that λIxy = ∥x − y∥p, we would be done. For the 2-exponential kernel it now seems natural to sample hyperplanes with uniformly random directions—e.g., via i.i.d. Gaussian entries—and a uniformly random shift from the origin. For 

p < 2, the natural generalization is to replace the Gaussian entries with i.i.d. p-stable entries. Indeed, these choices lead to the desired identity λIxy = ∥x − y∥p. Unfortunately, the resulting PHP is not computationally realizable, since Rd itself is not compact. Simulating the PHP would mean sampling an infinite number of random hyperplanes. Thus, it does not yield a computable LSH scheme. Our solution to this issue is to restrict the LSH to a compact region C and develop an efficient sampling scheme for the restriction of the PHP to C. At first blush, it may seem like one could simply sample the hyperplane directions with the same p-stable laws and just restrict the random shift to be within the boundaries of C. However, this approach fails, as it skews the PHP intensity measure and 3breaks the key identity λIxy = ∥x − y∥p. Rather, one must identify the actual marginal intensity on 

C and devise a method to efficiently sample from it. Our second main technical step is to show that this is possible for the hypercube C = Q := [ −1, 1] d.We show that the marginal PHP intensity on Q takes the form of an ℓ1-biased p-stable law . We proceed to show how to sample from it efficiently. For p = 2 , sampling a hyperplane from this law amounts to replacing a single (uniformly random) coordinate in the Gaussian vector with a sample from a two-sided variant of the Rayleigh distribution. For p < 2, we do not obtain a closed-form sampling procedure, but we are able to present an efficient rejection sampling scheme. This enables us to simulate the desired PHP on Q and obtain the kernel LSH schemes that prove Theorem 1.2.

Differentially private KDE. Differential privacy (DP) is widely considered the gold standard for privacy-preserving computation in machine learning. It formalizes aggregate computations that leak almost no information about any particular point in the dataset. We refer to [19, 20] and to Appendix B for definitions and background. Coleman and Shrivastava [17] and Wagner et al. [48] proved that kernel LSH schemes imply efficient DP KDE mechanisms in the function release model. In this model, a privacy-preserving representation of the KDE function can be released and queried infinitely many times without further privacy leakage. Their approach enabled DP KDE mechanisms for the 1-exponential kernel through its LSHability, and, by extension, also for the 2-exponential kernel via an efficient ℓ2 → ℓ1 embedding. Backurs et al. [7] gave a different DP KDE mechanism for the 2-exponential kernel via a more complex reduction to the Gaussian kernel, which in turn was handled in [48] with a different technique. Theorem 1.2 produces the first explicit DP KDE mechanisms for p-exponential kernels with p ∈ (1 , 2) ,by plugging it into the LSHability framework of [48] for DP KDE. See Appendix B for details. 

Theorem 3.1. Let ϵ, σ, d > 0, let α, η ∈ (0 , 1) , and let p ∈ (1 , 2) . # 5 Concluding Remarks and Open Questions 

We proved exact kernel LSH schemes for p-exponential kernels in bounded domains, including in the important case p = 2 which was previously open. This substantially expands the available kernel LSH results in the literature. As applications, we obtained efficient LSHability-based KDE methods. Our techniques depart from previous LSHability techniques which were based on randomly shifted grids. Rather, we obtain our results by introducing the use of Poisson hyperplane processes into LSH. As such, our LSH schemes are not generalizations of the existing LSH schemes for p = 1 . In fact, our techniques work for all p ∈ (1 , 2] but not for p = 1 . The technical reason is that the 1-stable distribution (Cauchy) has no finite absolute first moment mp, which our method requires. Two notable limitations of our LSH schemes are the O(d2) dependence in their running time and their restriction to bounded regions. In comparison, existing LSH schemes for p = 1 have running time O(d) and they apply to all of Rd, owing to the axis-alignment and translation-invariance of uniform grids in Rd. Our ℓ1-biased p-stable PHPs do not possess those properties. Whether these aspects of Theorem 1.2 can be improved, particularly for p = 2 , is an interesting open problem. 9Figure 2: p-Exponential kernel estimation by mosaic LSH on the Covertype dataset

# B Omitted Details from Section 3: DP KDE 

We recall the differential privacy definitions relevant to Theorem 3.1. 

Definition B.1 (differential privacy) . Let ϵ > 0. Let M be a randomized algorithm that maps datasets with elements in X to a space of outputs O. M is called an ϵ-differentially private ( ϵ-DP) mechanism if for every two datasets X, X ′ such that X′ is obtained from X by either adding or dropping a single element, and for every set of outputs O ⊂ O , it holds that Pr[ M (X) ∈ O] ≤ eϵ Pr[ M (X′) ∈ O].

Definition B.2 (DP KDE in the function release model) . Let ϵ > 0 and α, η ∈ (0 , 1) . Let k :

X × X → [0 , 1] be a kernel. In the DP KDE problem in the function release model, the goal is to design an ϵ-DP mechanism M that given a dataset X ⊂ X , returns a function FX : X → [0 , 1] that for each y ∈ X satisfies Pr[ |FX (y) − KDE X (y)| ≤ α] ≥ 1 − η.

The mechanisms we obtain in Theorem 3.1 satisfy the above definitions. Theorem 3.1 follows from plugging Theorem 1.2 into Theorem D.3 and Proposition D.4 in [48]. The only thing to check is that the efficiency bounds in Proposition D.4 in [48] depend on log B, where B is the number of bins of the LSH scheme. In our case, the number of bins is 2N where N ∼ Poisson(2 d/σ ) is the number of hyperplanes sampled in the mosaic LSH scheme. Therefore, E[log 2 B] = E[N ] = 2 d/σ .
