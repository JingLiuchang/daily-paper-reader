# A Hierarchical Statistical Framework for Evaluating Agent Reliability 

Mirco Milletar` ı and Tuhin Kundu Microsoft November 17, 2026 

Abstract 

Agents operating in complex scenarios often face uncertainty due to incomplete information about the environment they operate in, ambiguous user queries, complex business rules and the inherently stochastic dynamics of the governing policy. Several new benchmarks [1, 2, 3, 4, 5] have been proposed to evaluate LLM-based agents on complex tasks in multi-tool environ-ments, including settings with policy constraints and varying levels of intent ambiguity. These conditions make it important to evaluate agentic systems systematically and quantify their per-formance under different conditions to assess their reliability in production. This need is evident in the recently introduced Thinkingbox benchmark [5], where even the strongest models strug-gle to perform consistently across diverse business scenarios. This paper presents a statistical framework for evaluating uncertainty and reliability in agentic systems. We define a hierarchy of scenarios, instances, and samples to assess model performance and variability systemati-cally. We apply this framework to common metrics found in the literature: mean pass rate, 

pass @k [6], and passˆ k [1], with uncertainty estimates for each metric. Aggregating metrics at different levels of the hierarchy reveals different structures and properties of the policy model; we connect this hierarchical aggregation with the known process of coarse-graining as intended in the physics and statistical literature. We independently derive exact finite-sample expressions for the sampling variances of the unbiased pass @k and passˆ k estimators; related results also appear in recent manuscripts [7, 8]. Our central contribution is a constructive derivation of hierarchical uncertainty propagation, making explicit its correspondence with coarse-graining and separating execution noise from task heterogeneity. Together, these derivations provide a unified statistical framework for evaluating reliability across repeated attempts, tasks, and scenarios. 

# 1 Introduction 

Large language models (LLMs) have enabled increasingly capable AI agents that combine a policy model, tools for interacting with an external environment, and a harness that orchestrates their execution. Given a task, an agent generates a solution trajectory comprising reasoning steps and actions, potentially modifying the environment along the way. Stochastic decision-making, task ambiguity, and complex interactions with the environment can produce different trajectories and outcomes across repeated executions of the same task. Evaluating these systems therefore requires more than a single aggregate performance score: we must establish how consistently they succeed, where failures concentrate, and how precisely performance has been measured. Yet benchmark reporting often emphasizes a single state-of-the-art (SOTA) result without quan-tifying its statistical uncertainty [9]. When reported differences between models amount to only a few percentage points, point estimates alone do not establish whether those differences reflect 1arXiv:2611.19614v1 [cs.AI] 14 Nov 2026 genuine improvements or sampling noise. This limitation is particularly consequential for agentic systems, whose performance can vary both across tasks and across repeated attempts at the same task. Bjarnason et al. [10] quantify this variability on SWE-Bench-Verified and show that small reported improvements can be comparable to evaluation noise. The recently introduced Think-ingbox benchmark [5] likewise highlights substantial variability among strong models, both within and across domains, with uncertainty estimates complicating claims of a clear leader even within a single domain. These observations motivate a statistical treatment that distinguishes variability in agent behavior from uncertainty in benchmark estimates. Reliability also extends beyond success frequency. Rabanser et al. [11] organize agent reliability into consistency, robustness, predictability, and safety, and find that capability gains need not yield comparable improvements in reliability. Their perspective is complementary to ours: rather than proposing a comprehensive taxonomy of reliability, we focus on the statistical interpretation of success-based metrics and the propagation of uncertainty across repeated attempts, tasks, and scenarios. Such analysis supports, but does not replace, evaluation of robustness or failure severity. From a user’s perspective, an agent should solve a task reliably without requiring repeated requests. Single-attempt success, summarized by pass @1 (mean pass rate), is therefore a central measure of practical performance. However, the average alone neither captures how consistently individual tasks are solved nor indicates how precisely performance has been estimated. Repeated evaluation of each task helps characterize execution variability, while confidence intervals quantify uncertainty in the resulting estimates. These measurements provide evidence relevant to production reliability, although their applicability depends on how closely the benchmark reflects deployment conditions. Reliability must also be considered alongside the cost of obtaining a successful outcome. An agent may eventually find a solution through retries, but this behavior can be undesirable for long-horizon tasks that consume substantial time and computational resources. Secondary metrics such as number of tool calls, retries, and token usage therefore complement success-based measures by characterizing the efficiency of solution trajectories. Together, these measurements can guide reinforcement fine-tuning (RFT) toward more consistent and efficient task completion, rather than improvements in aggregate success alone. Prior work provides complementary approaches to statistical evaluation. Chen et al. [6] intro-duce the unbiased pass @k estimator, while Yao et al. [1] introduce passˆ k and its unbiased estimator to measure agent reliability across repeated trials. Hariri et al. [12] develop a Bayesian alternative to pass @k-based model ranking, with analytical posterior uncertainty and credible intervals for underlying success probabilities. Dewasurendra [13] studies a different problem: finite-sample con-fidence intervals and identification limits for population pass @k, particularly when extrapolating beyond the observed sampling budget. These questions are distinct from the exact frequentist sam-pling variances of the unbiased pass @k and passˆ k estimators studied here; bootstrap uncertainty estimates likewise do not provide analytical expressions for those variances. Recent manuscripts also analyze finite-sample uncertainty for these metrics [7, 8], but do not address hierarchical un-certainty propagation. In this work, we present a unified statistical framework for evaluating performance and uncer-tainty in LLM-based agentic systems. Our contributions are threefold. First, we organize evaluation into scenarios, task instances, and repeated samples, distinguishing execution variability, differences across tasks, and uncertainty in estimated performance. We interpret aggregation through this hi-erarchy as coarse-graining in physics and statistics [14], showing how averages reveal collective behavior while obscuring task-level differences. Second, we analyze uncertainty in mean pass rate and derive exact finite-sample sampling variances for the unbiased pass @k and passˆ k estimators, connecting these per-task results to uncertainty in population-level averages. Third, we illustrate the framework on agentic benchmarks to show what these metrics and their uncertainty estimates 2Figure 1: Hierarchical data structure: scenarios contain multiple instances, each instance is sampled multiple times to account for variability. Aggregating metrics at different levels of this hierarchy corresponds to coarse graining. reveal about reliability. The central contribution is not an isolated metric or variance formula, but an account of how task heterogeneity and finite evaluation budgets jointly shape the conclu-sions drawn from benchmark results. The framework draws on established statistical results; the coarse-graining connection and analytical derivations are developed in Appendices A and B. 

# 2 Modeling Assumptions and Definitions 

Throughout the paper, the policy model governs the agent’s decisions, while the environment in-cludes the tools and infrastructure with which it interacts. We evaluate a fixed agent configuration, including its policy, harness, and environment setup. Following the terminology used in [5], we define the hierarchical structure of our framework as follows: 1. Scenario : A scenario is defined by a specific set of tools ( e.g. , a set of MCP servers) and the environment’s initial state. It is grounded in the real-world context it aims to simulate, such as retail, healthcare, finance, etc. 2. Instance : A specific task that the agent must complete using the tools available in a given scenario. An instance is a data point in our dataset comprising the user’s intent, the system prompt, and other relevant context. A dataset is a collection of instances across one or more scenarios. 3. Sample : A single attempt by the agent to resolve a given instance, also referred to as a ”sample trajectory.” To assess the agent’s performance and variability, we need multiple samples for each instance. For the analysis, we write πθ(y | x) for the trajectory distribution induced by the policy with parameters θ in this fixed evaluation setup. Here, x specifies the task and its context, and y

is the resulting trajectory; the distribution includes any modeled environment or user-interaction randomness. # 3 Statistical Analysis Framework 

The dataset’s hierarchical structure (scenarios, instances, and samples) requires global metrics that capture model performance at different levels of granularity. Moving up the hierarchy corresponds to a coarse-graining process in which we aggregate performance metrics by averaging. However, this process can obscure important information about model performance in specific domains and on individual instances. For example, Ref. [5] presents a detailed benchmark of several models at the scenario and dataset levels and shows that no model consistently outperforms the others across all scenarios, even when some models lead in the across-scenario rankings. This finding highlights the importance of considering both fine- and coarse-grained metrics. Although pass rates can be aggregated throughout the hierarchy by simple averaging, propagating uncertainty across levels requires additional care, particularly for evaluations with limited numbers of instances [9, 15]. We address this issue in Section 3.2 within the hierarchical uncertainty-propagation framework, and provide an explicit derivation in Appendix A, which accounts for variability at multiple levels and their interactions; the derivation borrows the formalism of coarse-graining [14] from the physics and statistical literature. Informally, coarse-graining is the process of zooming out the description of a system, moving from a microscopic to a macroscopic description, e.g. describing a fluid in terms of its individual constituents versus a continuous field. Mathematically, zooming out consists in averaging the microscopic variables, which inevitably leads to a loss of resolution while highlighting collective, population-level aspects.

# 5 Conclusion 

Reliable evaluation of agentic systems requires more than an aggregate success score: it requires identifying what a metric measures, how precisely it has been estimated, and which differences are hidden by aggregation. We have presented a statistical framework organized around scenarios, task instances, and repeated samples, distinguishing stochastic execution variability from finite-sample estimation uncertainty and heterogeneity in task difficulty. Connecting this hierarchy to coarse-graining makes explicit both the value and the limitations of benchmark averages: they summarize collective behavior, but cannot by themselves characterize instance-level reliability or reveal localized regressions. Within this framework, we have analyzed mean pass rate, pass @k, and passˆ k, including exact finite-sample sampling variances for the unbiased estimators of the latter two metrics and hier-archical uncertainty propagation. These results give a principled basis for attaching uncertainty estimates to commonly reported scores and interpreting model comparisons. They also clarify that single-attempt performance, task coverage under retries, and repeated-success reliability answer different questions; no one of these metrics is a substitute for the others. The Thinkingbox case study [5] illustrates the practical importance of this distinction. For the base and reinforcement fine-tuned Qwen-3.8-27b models considered here, improved mean pass rate coexists with similar coverage at larger k and higher point estimates of repeated-success reliability. Together with the instance-level gaps and the shift toward higher pass rates, this pattern shows that the fine-tuned model is making already-solvable tasks more consistently successful, rather than substantially expanding task coverage, although some previously unsolved tasks may still benefit from fine-tuning, as shown by the instance-level and distributional analyses. Scenario averages nevertheless conceal individual improvements and regressions, emphasizing why aggregate gains should be examined alongside paired task-level comparisons and their uncertainty. 18 The resulting recommendation is to report mean pass rate, coverage, and reliability jointly, with uncertainty estimates at the relevant aggregation levels and sufficient instance-level detail to expose regressions. Extending this analysis to dependent executions and evaluation-budget allocation is a natural direction for future work. Ultimately, progress in agentic systems should be judged not only by whether average performance increases, but by whether that improvement is statistically supported, broadly distributed across tasks, and aligned with the reliability users actually need.

As n increases, the within-instance binomial-noise contribution de-creases, while between-instance variability remains; accordingly, the aggregate uncertainty does not in general vanish as n grows. The binomial-noise contribution scales as 1 /(M n ), so increasing the number of runs has diminishing returns, particularly when M is large. The normal-approximation intervals should be interpreted cautiously for small evaluation sets, where they can understate un-certainty [15]. This analysis illustrates the trade-off between computational cost and the statistical precision of scenario-level performance estimates. 28
