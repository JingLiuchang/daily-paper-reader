# Abstract 

On-policy distillation (OPD) transfers specialized capabilities to language-model agents, but its cross-domain effects on interaction remain unclear. We investigate how OPD reshapes ReAct interaction patterns across mathematics, ALFWorld, and τ 2-bench, focusing on reasoning, ac-tions, and observation acquisition. General-reasoning supervision reduces mathematical tool at-tempts from 70 .4% to 14 .4% while increasing accuracy from 16 .7% to 38 .0% ; code-interpreter supervision improves household actions and retail queries. In contrast, ALFWorld supervision reduces τ 2-bench accuracy from 41 .5% to 6.6% , accompanied by frequent tool calls that violate user-information dependencies. To address negative transfer using only source-domain training trajectories, we explore selective OPD guided by teacher preference changes before and after domain-specific RL. Retaining the top 95% of positions raises τ 2 accuracy from 11 .5% to 29 .9% 

with a source-learning trade-off. 

# 1 Introduction 

Large Language Model (LLM) agents’ competence depends on how it connects reasoning, action, and observation. Mathematical assistants may call python interpreter (Xue et al., 2026) to calculate or directly derive an answer; Service agents may query an account or request a missing identifier. Reasoning and Acting (ReAct) (Yao et al., 2022), which interleaves reasoning traces with task-specific actions, allowing an LLM to plan, track its progress, and handle exceptions through thought while gathering information from external environments such as search APIs or tools, decouples reasoning with actions and observations and becomes the most widely-used paradigm (Schick et al., 2023; Qin et al., 2024) which LLMs use to solve agentic tasks (Jimenez et al., 2024; Wei et al., 2025). On-Policy Distillation (OPD), which transfers teacher capabilities by supervising trajectories sampled by the student (Agarwal et al., 2024; Song and Zheng, 2026), has also become an important step in LLM post-training pipeline (Ma et al., 2026; Team et al., 2026; Xu et al., 2026). OPD training can affect models’ choices, especially in the multi-turn agentic tasks, including how to organize reasoning and tool-use, how to obtain new information, interact with the environment, and so on. We study these ReAct interaction patterns across four domains: (1) pure natural-language general reason-ing (Hendrycks et al., 2021) (GR), (2) general reasoning with code-interpreter tools (He-Yueya et al., 2023; Qian et al., 2025) (CI), (3) ALFWorld (Shridhar et al., 2021), and (4) τ 2-bench (Barres et al., 2025). We find two observable ReAct pattern shifts in trajectories of the above agentic tasks. First, OPD can shift the route to a mathematical answer between tool-assisted computation and direct natural-language rea-soning (§ 4.1): GR-domain OPD training starkly reduces models’ tool attempt rate from 70 .4% to 14 .4% 

in the CI tasks. Second, it can change how the student chooses actions, adjusts them after environment feedback, and waits for information or authorization before acting (§ 4.2): CI supervision improves both ALFWorld and τ 2 tasks, accompanied by fewer repeated actions and more complete queries. Meanwhile, we find that ALFWorld-domain OPD makes student’s τ 2 performance drastically drop from 41.5% to 6.6%. The student frequently generates request-like text alongside tool calls without yielding the turn to the user. More importantly, semantic review identifies calls that depend on unavailable user information or authorization in 55 .8% of trajectories. These findings connect cross-domain outcomes to reasoning 1arXiv:2611.19244v2 [cs.AI] 18 Nov 2026 routes, feedback-driven actions, and the acquisition of observations. We analyze that severely overfitting to task-specific ReAct patterns is the main cause of the observed negative transfer. We find that excluding a small fraction of tokens (i.e., with the least 5% RL-induced ∆).) in the ALFWorld-domain OPD train-ing can effectively mitigate the catastrophic negative transfer on the τ 2 tasks, while maintaining most of the performance gain on ALFWorld tasks. We identifies both positive cross-domain transfer and negative transfer, notably ALF-domain OPD training will drastically make student’s τ 2 performance drop down; and (2) To mitigate this failure using only ALF-source training trajectories, we propose selecting OPD token positions according to the log-probability change between a teacher checkpoint before and after domain-specific RL, excluding the lowest-tail, which largely alleviates the degradation of τ 2 accuracy while maintaining most of the performance gain on the ALFWorld. GR-to-CI extension examines whether the method also preserves mathematical tool-use behavior. 

# 2 Related Work 

On-Policy Distillation. Knowledge distillation transfers teacher behavior to students (Hinton et al., 2015). OPD supervises student-generated contexts (Agarwal et al., 2024; Song and Zheng, 2026), while multi-teacher OPD integrates domain experts (Ma et al., 2026). Recent work explores entropy-aware KL objectives (Jin et al., 2026), OPD optimization geometry (Shen et al., 2026b), and efficient offline dis-tillation (Wu et al., 2026b). For agentic OPD, SEED distills hindsight skills (Wu et al., 2026a), ATOD introduces turn-aware weighting (Tan et al., 2026), and ActFirst-OPD separates action and reasoning (Zheng et al., 2026). Studies further examine teacher–student mismatch in multi-turn agents (Wang et al., 2026b; Zhong et al., 2026; Zhou et al., 2026) and cross-domain transfer (Li et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026a). Delta-based distillation leverages teacher–predecessor preference differences (Heo et al., 2026). We examine these effects in agentic trajectories (Shen et al., 2026a), tracing solution routes, responses to feedback, and the timing of information requests. This behavioral focus complements work on forgetting (Kirkpatrick et al., 2017; Lopez-Paz and Ranzato, 2017) and multi-task interference (Yu et al., 2020). 

LLM Agents’ ReAct Patterns. ReAct couples reasoning with actions and observations (Yao et al., 2022); Toolformer and ToolLLM develop tool-use capabilities (Qin et al., 2024; Schick et al., 2023), and API-Bank evaluates tool-augmented interaction (Li et al., 2023). Reasoning methods influence the route to an answer through intermediate derivations and sampled solutions (Wei et al., 2022; Kojima et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2022; Xu et al., 2025). Dynamic reasoning strategies (Aoki et al., 2024) and feedback-based revision (Shinn et al., 2023) likewise make process behavior important. Our comparisons ask how such observable patterns change after domain distillation, including when improved answer accuracy 2accompanies less tool use and when frequent actions bypass necessary user feedback. Tool uncertainty (Liu et al., 2024a), procedurally generated environments (Sullivan et al., 2025), and prompt-injection evaluation (Debenedetti et al., 2024) address complementary aspects of reliable interaction. 

# 3 Preliminaries and Experiment Settings  

> Agent interaction.

An agent receives a goal x and interacts with external participants and systems. At decision turn k, its history hk contains the instructions, previous assistant outputs, tool results, environment observations, and user replies. The policy generates an output uk ∼ πθ (· | hk). An output may communicate with a user, invoke a tool, or complete the task. The interaction process then supplies feedback ok+1 , producing hk+1 = hk ∥ uk ∥ ok+1 . For an assistant-generated token yt at decision turn k,we denote its complete autoregressive context by Ht.We examine ReAct interaction patterns by analyzing how agents organize reasoning, acting, and obtaining observation throughout an interaction (Yao et al., 2022). Reasoning can derive an answer. Acting selects an operation and its arguments. Obtaining observations includes requesting information and yielding the turn so another participant can provide it. They provide three observable perspectives on a trajectory.

> Experimental setup.

In this work, we study four representative task domains with distinct ReAct pat-terns: (1) single-turn General Reasoning (GR) task, which uses natural language to solve math problems without calling tools; (2) Code-Interpretation (CI) (Xue et al., 2026), using Python code-interpreter to solve complex math problems; (3) ALFWorld (ALF) (Shridhar et al., 2021), which simulates real-world em-bodied agentic tasks and supplies text-based household action feedback; and (4) τ 2-bench ( τ 2) (Barres et al., 2025) adds simulated users and account-side tools in airline, retail, and telecom tasks. ALF-World includes 140 in-distribution and 134 out-of-distribution tasks sampled four times. τ 2-bench in-cludes 100 tasks across airline, retail, and telecom, each sampled eight times. # 4 Agentic OPD Reshapes ReAct Patterns 

We train students with OPD on individual task domains and with MOPD on mixtures of two or three domains, then evaluate each student across tool-enabled and no-tool mathematics, ALFWorld, and τ 2-bench. Figure 2 shows that training on one task can substantially change performance on other tasks. For example, CI-only OPD observably raises ALFWorld accuracy from 0.129 to 0.402 ( +0 .273 ) and 

τ 2 accuracy from 0.415 to 0.510 ( +0 .095 ). ALF-only OPD instead drastically lowers τ 2 accuracy from 0.415 to 0.066 ( −0.349 ). The effects of domain-specific supervision therefore extend beyond the task that supplies its training trajectories, with different outcomes across source–target pairs. Task scores establish the scope of this cross-domain effect but do not show what changed in the stu-dent’s interaction policy. We examine two observable ReAct patterns in the resulting trajectories. First, OPD can shift the route to a mathematical answer between tool-assisted computation and direct natural-language reasoning (§ 4.1). Second, it can change how the student chooses actions, adjusts them after environment feedback, and waits for information or authorization before acting (§ 4.2). These analy-ses connect the performance differences in Figure 2 to specific decisions in the agent’s reasoning and interaction process.

> Domain-specific OPD supervision (e.g., from CI) can be associated with more effective and productive action patterns after environment feedback, which can also generalize to other held-out agentic tasks. # 5 Exploring Mitigations for Negative Transfer in Agentic OPD 

In this section, we mainly explore how to mitigate the potential cross-task negative transfer in agentic OPD training, using ALF-to-τ 2 transfer (§ 4.2) as the main case and extending the analysis to GR-to-CI transfer. 

## 5.1 Methodology: Selective OPD Supervision on Tokens with RL-Induced Pref-erence 

We summarize our core insight derived from the previous observations is agentic OPD training can teach student two parts of knowledge: (1) generic agentic abilities (e.g., how to reason and act based on observations) and (2) task-specific ReAct patterns (e.g., action habit of obtaining information in § 4.2). Severely overfitting to part (2) is the main cause of the negative transfer. Hence, the key idea of our proposed approach is to design a selective policy, which keeps the generic agentic abilities (mostly derived from the agentic RL training (Yu et al., 2025)) and discards the plain transfer of surface-level task-specific ReAct patterns. Given a student’s rollout, we use both of the teacher’s checkpoints before and after domain-specific RL to calculate log-likelihood on every token. Insight: Excluding Smallest ∆ Tokens Can Rescue. 

Excluding a small fraction of tokens (i.e., with the least 5% RL-induced ∆) in the ALFWorld-domain OPD training can effectively mitigate the catastrophic negative transfer on the τ 2 tasks, while maintaining most of the performance gain on ALFWorld tasks.

# 6 Conclusion 

Agentic OPD reshapes how LLM agents reason, act, and obtain observations across domains, producing positive and negative transfer. Task-matched MOPD mitigates the conversational failures induced by ALF supervision on τ 2. As a source-only training exploration, we select ordinary OPD supervision po-9sitions using pre/post-domain-RL preference changes. Excluding the low-∆ tail substantially alleviates negative transfer while sacrificing source-domain performance. Token-level analysis reveals concentrated context-dependent suppressive feedback, including reasoning and tool-call onsets. Structural masks, neg-ative masking, and alternative ranking rules yield different source–target trade-offs; none of the reported alternatives reproduces the same target-interaction preservation as signed-∆ Top95 in the ALF compari-son. An extension with a RL-trained GR teacher preserves more CI tool use at equal mean GR accuracy. These findings highlight the importance of controlling where specialization feedback is applied in agentic OPD. 

# Limitations 

Our experiments focus on the Qwen3 model family with 4B students and mathematical and interactive tasks. Extending the analysis to larger students, other model families, and a broader range of agent envi-ronments would help establish the generality of the observed transfer patterns. The effects of supervision selection may also depend on the training recipe and interaction protocol, motivating further study across additional configurations. 

# Ethical Considerations 

This research helps identify potential risks arising when specialized training changes an agent’s interaction behavior. In particular, changes in information gathering and user confirmation can lead to actions based on unsupported assumptions or actions taken before authorization is obtained. Our findings can inform evaluations of tool-using agents and encourage attention to feedback dependencies, confirmation requirements, and execution permissions in practical applications. 10 References

13 Appendix A Discussion  

> What should agentic training evaluate?

Task accuracy, action content, and feedback dependencies cap-ture different properties of agent behavior. The mathematical results pair improved accuracy with fewer tool attempts; CI transfer pairs better outcomes with action adjustment and query coverage. Conversely, ALF transfer produces request-like text alongside tool calls, some of which depend on user information or authorization not yet obtained. Evaluation should therefore measure how an agent prepares an action, ac-quires its prerequisites, and responds to the resulting observation, alongside task utility (Arabzadeh et al., 2024) and benchmark breadth (Liu et al., 2024b; Zhou et al., 2024). Importantly, ask–call co-occurrence captures generated output patterns rather than actual user requests, and should be interpreted alongside dependency violations and task accuracy.  

> How should supervision be composed?

MOPD provides complementary training coverage: CI supports mathematical tool use and feedback-driven actions, while task-matched τ 2 supervision mitigates failures to wait for required user information. These findings connect teacher composition to behavioral cover-age and task grouping (Yu et al., 2020; Standley et al., 2020). Source-only ∆-guided selection offers another approach when target-domain training data are unavailable. Excluding a small low-∆ tail re-duces concentrated suppressive feedback at context-dependent positions, with a source-learning trade-off. Delimiter masking, full-block masking, and negative masking fail to reproduce the same source–target bal-ance. These results highlight the importance of feedback location and direction, while further controlled interventions are needed to isolate the mechanisms underlying cross-domain transfer.

## B.1 Domain Interaction and Tool-Use Instructions 

The domains differ in how reasoning leads to an action and how the agent obtains the feedback needed for its next decision.  

> General reasoning (GR).

GR training uses mathematical problems solved through natural-language reasoning. The model develops a solution and produces its answer without an external tool-feedback loop. This source therefore supervises text-based derivation rather than interaction with an execution environment.  

> Code interpretation (CI).

CI allows the agent to invoke code_interpreter when calculation, symbolic manipulation, or verification is useful. A tool call supplies Python code, and its execution result is appended to the interaction history. The agent can then use that result to continue reasoning, revise a calculation, or produce its answer.  

> ALFWorld (ALF).

At each decision step, the agent receives an environment observation and a list of admissible actions. The prompt asks it to reason about the current situation and execute exactly one listed action through alfworld_step . The action argument must match an entry in the admissible action 14 list. Execution returns the next observation, so information gathering and task progress are both mediated by environment actions.   

> Conversational tool use ( τ2).

The agent alternates between communicating with a customer and invok-ing an available account-side tool. The prompt specifies one interaction branch per turn: a customer-facing message or a tool call. Missing information must be obtained from the customer, and database-changing actions require explicit confirmation under the corresponding domain policy. Customer-side device procedures are communicated as instructions for the customer to perform. Airline, retail, and telecom share these interaction requirements while using different policies and tools. Tool calls in CI, ALF, and τ 2 use JSON objects with name and arguments fields enclosed by <tool_call> 

and </tool_call> markers. This serialization is shared across the tool-using domains; the surrounding reasoning, feedback source, and turn requirements depend on the task.

# C Evaluation Protocols and Behavioral Metrics 

## C.1 Evaluation Data and Execution 

Mathematical evaluation contains 30 AIME2026 (Zhang and Math-AI Team, 2026) questions sampled 16 times and 100 BeyondAIME (ByteDance-Seed, 2025) questions sampled five times, giving 980 trajectories per model. The tool-enabled setting provides code_interpreter ; the no-tool setting evaluates text-based solutions. ALFWorld evaluation contains 140 in-distribution and 134 out-of-distribution tasks, each evaluated four times, yielding 1,096 trajectories per model. The τ 2 evaluation contains 100 tasks sampled eight times: 160 airline, 320 retail, and 320 telecom trajectories. We use the same sampling settings for all training rollouts and evaluations: temperature 1.0, top-p 1.0, and unrestricted top-k.Teacher token scoring computes probabilities for student-generated tokens at temperature 1.0, without generating additional responses. Agent execution uses the Qwen3 chat template and structured tool calls. The loop parses the complete assistant output before choosing the next interaction branch. In τ 2, exactly one parsed call with object-valued arguments is submitted to the environment. If prose accompanies the call, it remains in the agent history but is not sent to the customer. Text-only outputs are sent to the customer. Multiple parsed calls, or a tool marker with no parseable call, produce local error feedback. The τ 2 user simulator uses DeepSeek-V4-Flash (Xu et al., 2026) at temperature 0.7. Natural-language assertions use GPT-5.5 at temperature 1.0.

## F.2 Structural Masking 

Structural masking tests whether removing supervision at fixed tool-call positions preserves target inter-action. ## F.7 Delta-Based Feedback Exploration 

Inspired by On-Policy Delta Distillation (Heo et al., 2026), we also explore directly using pre/post-domain-RL preference differences as distillation feedback. Specifically, we center the delta feedback under the student distribution and retain it when its sign agrees with centered ordinary OPD feedback. Under our multi-turn training setup, this variant exhibits generation degeneration: after 20 updates, both ALFWorld and τ 2 accuracies drop to 0%, and all 800 τ 2 rollouts reach the response-length limit without generating complete tool calls. This result motivates using ∆ for position selection while retaining the ordinary OPD advantage.
